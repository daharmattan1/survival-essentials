## Zone Fit & Why It Earns Space

Maple syrup is a late-winter sugar source from trees you may already have. UMaine says the average taphole gives 5 to 15 gallons of sap in a full season, and each tap yields roughly 0.25 gallon of syrup when done well [1]. UNH expects 8 to 10 sap runs and 8 to 10 gallons of sap per tap over a season [3]. UMaine says syrup at the right density can be stored in a cool, dry place [1]. The hazards are in the work, not the tree: carbon monoxide and fire at the boil, lead from old equipment, and spoiled sap.

## Planting Windows (Zones 4b-6b): Tapping Season

Maples are not planted for this. The window is when sap runs. UMaine explains why: starch stored in the wood turns to sugar when the wood passes about 40 F, and freeze-thaw cycles drive the sap. Days near 40 F or warmer and nights below freezing run from mid-February to early April in southern Maine and mid-March to late April in northern Maine [1].

| Source | Window or condition |
|---|---|
| UMaine Bulletin #7036 [1] | mid-February to early April (southern Maine); mid-March to late April (northern Maine) |
| Cornell/Penn State leaflet [4] | mid to late February (Pennsylvania, southern New York); early to mid March in northern areas and at higher elevations; flow 4 to 6 weeks while freezing nights and warm days continue |
| UNH Extension [3] | cold nights in the 20s, days in the 40s |
| UVM Proctor Maple Research Center [8] | describes its own sugaring season as January to April |

Seasons change each year and by site. Ask a nearby sugarmaker or your county extension office, and watch the forecast. Tap on a day above freezing, to lessen the chance of splitting the tree (Cornell [4], UMaine [1]).

**The end of the season.** Cornell says that when buds break, the sap becomes off-flavored and is not wanted for syrup, and that red maple breaks bud before sugar maple [4]. UNH calls this "buddy sap" [3]. Cornell Maple Program adds that sap spoils fastest late in the season when it is warm [5].

## Growing

- **Identify the tree first.** UMaine: maples have opposite buds and branches; ash and horse chestnut do too, but their shoot tips are blocky and wide, not narrow and pointed [1]. UNH: sugar maple has sharp brown buds, red maple round red buds [3]. Mark trees in leaf. The sources opened do not address boiling sap from non-maples, so tap only trees you have positively identified as maple (general practice).
- **Tree size and tap count.** The sources differ. UNH: no tree under 12 inches across, one tap at 12 to 18 inches, two taps over 18, never more than two [3]. UMaine: at least 10 inches, one tap up to 18 inches, a second tap above 18; its text allows a third only on very healthy trees over 25 inches [1]. Cornell: 10 to 17 inches one tap, 18 to 24 two, 25 or more three [4]. The cautious figures are UNH's.
- **Taphole.** UMaine and UNH: 5/16 inch spout and bit; UNH allows 7/16 for buckets. Drill 1.5 to 2 inches deep into clean, light-colored wood, away from old holes; UMaine says drill level, UNH says a slight upward angle. Drive spouts lightly so they do not split the tree [1][3].
- **Collection.** Covered food-grade buckets, bags, or food-grade tubing [1][2].
- **Legal.** Tap only your own trees or trees whose owner has given permission (general practice). Vermont licenses sugaring on state forestland [9]; check the land manager for any other public land. Selling syrup needs labeling and, in Maine, a processing license [2][3]. Open fires may need a permit; UNH says to contact the local fire warden [3]. This is not legal advice.

## Harvest & Storage

**Sap is like milk.** UMaine and UNH both say so [1][3]. Cornell says sap is a mixture of sugar and water and a perfect medium for bacteria, so collect and process it as quickly as possible [4].

- **Keep it cold.** UNH: gather daily, keep sap cool and below 40 F, and know that above 50 F it spoils fast [3]. UMaine: keep it in cold storage and boil as soon as you can; on the north side of a building, banked in snow, with insulating wrap, helps [1][2]. Cornell: store in the shade in a clean tank [4].
- **Capacity.** Plan 1 to 2 gallons of storage per tap (Cornell [4]), up to 2 gallons per tap per day of sap run (UMaine [2]).
- **Covers.** Keep rain, snow, insects and debris out [1][2].
- **Filter raw sap** through clean cloth or paper before boiling (UNH, Cornell) [3][4].
- **Throw out bad sap.** UNH: discard yellow or cloudy sap, and do not mix it with good, clear sap [3]. Cornell says cloudy sap after warm weather comes from bacterial growth and harms syrup color and taste [4]. Sour or off-smelling sap goes out as well (general practice).

**Syrup storage.** Pack hot (below). Store in a cool, dry place; once opened, refrigerate (UMaine, Cornell) [1][4]. UNH says syrup that was not hot-packed can be kept in the refrigerator or freezer, and hot-packed syrup can be frozen for longer storage [3]. **If mold forms on syrup stored for several months, UMaine says discard it, because of possible contamination by a micro-organism that can cause foodborne illness [1].** The 1998 Cornell leaflet says to skim and re-boil moldy syrup [4]. This entry follows the newer, more cautious UMaine advice.

## Seed Saving

Syrup is not a seed crop, so there is nothing to save. What you keep going is the sugarbush, meaning the trees. UNH says the more holes, broken branches and scars, the less pressure builds in the tree. Do not re-tap old holes or drill extra holes to prolong the run, and remove spouts right after the season [3]. Cornell says do not plug tapholes at season's end; they close on their own in about two years [4].

## ⚠ MANDATORY PREPARATION

**Boil outdoors only. Sap becomes syrup only by boiling it down, and the boil has three hazards that hurt people: carbon monoxide, fire and burns, and lead.**

1. **Carbon monoxide (CO).**
   - CO is a colorless, odorless gas made when fuel burns. Symptoms are headache, dizziness, weakness, upset stomach, vomiting, chest pain and confusion, and sleeping or intoxicated people can die before symptoms [10].
   - OSU Extension lists propane, wood and charcoal among the fuels that make CO [11]. CDC: burning charcoal gives off CO, never burn charcoal indoors, and never use a portable gas camp stove indoors [10].
   - Follow the repo rule, `03_fire.md` ("Fire indoors, in tents, and in vehicles: the carbon monoxide rule"): never burn charcoal, a camp stove, a lantern, a heater or an open fire inside a tent, vehicle, garage, or house, and opening a door or window does not fix it. A wood fire, camp stove or propane burner used for sap goes outdoors, in the open.
   - Cornell says the bulk of the boiling should be done outside the home [4]. UMaine lists an outdoor gas range, outdoor fireplace or hobby evaporator, and says indoors needs a vent fan or dehumidifier for steam [1][2]. UNH and Cornell describe finishing on a stove or kitchen range [3][4]. This entry advises finishing outdoors too. The sources opened give no CO rule for a partly enclosed sugarhouse; treat any space with walls and a roof as indoors unless the equipment was built and vented for it (general practice).
   - **If anyone gets a headache, dizziness or nausea near a burning fuel, get everyone into fresh air first, then call 911** (the repo rule above). Do not go back in.
2. **Fire and burns.** Never leave boiling sap over a wood fire unattended; sap can boil away and burn the pan. Keep sap at least 1.5 inches deep in the pan, and keep a little butter or vegetable oil to drop in if it boils over [1]. UNH adds: hot sap and syrup can cause severe burns, so use gloves; keep a fire extinguisher and fireproof gloves; mind wildfire hazard and fire permits; always put the fire out [3].
3. **Lead.** Old sugaring gear can put lead in sap, and lead is concentrated anywhere from 30 to 100 times in the syrup [6].
   - Spouts should be stainless steel, aluminum or food-grade plastic. UMaine: old tin spouts may contain significant lead; galvanized buckets may contain lead that leaches into sap [2]. The 2015 good-practice guide: old tin or terneplate spiles can add up to 1,700 ppb to sap, and terneplate or galvanized buckets with lead solder should not be used [6].
   - Pans should be lead-free soldered or welded stainless steel; UNH notes lead test kits can be bought at most hardware stores [3]. Old pans with lead solder are the most serious source, especially if a pan scorches or boils dry [6]. The same guide says not to use bronze or brass gear transfer pumps, and not to clean lead-soldered seams to a bright shine [6].
   - Filter hot to take out sugar sand where lead collects, but filtering does not remove lead that has dissolved in the syrup [6]. Send syrup for lab testing if equipment is questionable [6].
4. **Food-grade containers only.** UMaine: no box-store buckets or kitty-litter containers, and nothing that held other food, which gives off-flavors [2]. UNH: no sheetrock-mud buckets [3]. No soap or detergent on equipment or filters; use hot water and triple-rinse [1][3][4].
5. **Boil to density, not by eye.**
   - Finished syrup is 66 to 69 percent sugar (UMaine [1]); UNH gives 67 Brix [3]. The finishing point is 7.1 F above the boiling point of water on that day (UMaine [1]), 7.5 F (UNH [3]), or 7 1/4 F (Cornell [4]), so the sources span 7.1 to 7.5 F. Measure the boiling point of plain water or raw sap on the day and add 7.1 to 7.5 F: if water boils at 212 F, syrup is done at about 219 F [1][2][3]. Adding much less than 7.1 F leaves the syrup thinner than the 66 percent floor (general practice).
   - Use a long-stemmed candy thermometer that reads above 220 F [2][3][4].
   - Too thin (under 66 percent) can ferment or grow mold. Too thick forms sugar crystals [1][3].
6. **Filter hot, then pack hot.** Filter through wool, Orlon or a paper pre-filter [1][3]. Pack at 180 to 190 F into new, clean mason jars or maple jugs; UMaine adds that 180 to 195 F sterilizes the containers [2], and UNH gives 185 to 190 F [3]. UVM research says syrup needs to be 180 to 190 F to kill bacteria, yeast or mold present at bottling, and that small glass jars cool fast [7]. Fill the jars full, cap at once, invert. UMaine says hot-packed syrup of correct density needs no boiling-water bath [1]. Do not reuse recycled jars or jugs; UMaine says they can introduce contaminants or mold [2].

For a question about possible lead exposure, call America's Poison Centers at 1-800-222-1222; call 911 for collapse, seizure or trouble breathing (general practice).
