## Zone Fit & Why It Earns Space

Potatoes are the starch crop of the northern garden. UMaine says they are popular in Maine home gardens because they can be stored through the long winter, and that they can be grown in all parts of Maine [1]. UMaine gives a rough yield of 1 to 2 pounds of potatoes per foot of row [1], and its planting chart says 5 pounds of seed potatoes gives about 50 pounds of potatoes [2]. Properly cured, mature, late-season, thick-skinned potatoes kept cool, dark and humid can last seven to eight months, according to Penn State [7]. UMass puts it at 5 to 8 months [10].

The same plant that feeds you also makes a toxin when light hits the tubers. That is handled in the preparation section below, and it shapes how you hill and how you store.

## Planting Windows (Zones 4b-6b)

Potatoes go in **before** the last frost, not after it. The Northeast sources count it two ways, and they do not agree to the day, so use the one you can measure.

| Source | When to plant |
|---|---|
| UNH Extension planting fact sheet [4] | seed potatoes 1 to 2 weeks before the last frost |
| UVM Extension [5] | 2 to 4 weeks before the last frost |
| UMaine planting chart [2] | central Maine May 1 to June 1; coastal Maine 10 to 14 days earlier; northern Maine 10 to 14 days later |
| UMaine Bulletin #2077 [1] | as soon as the soil warms to 50 F |
| UNH "Growing Potatoes" [3] | as soon as the soil is dry enough to work and has warmed to 45 F |
| Cornell Cooperative Extension (Herkimer County) [6] | do not plant until the soil reaches 40 F |

UMaine warns that seed sitting in cold, wet soil is open to decay [1]. Penn State says the same of soil that is too cold or too wet [7]. Light, well-drained soil dries and warms earlier, so it can be planted earlier [1].

**Last-frost date.** UNH says to find your own last-frost date, and treats Memorial Day as a conservative estimate for central New Hampshire [4]. Counting 1 to 2 weeks back from that gives roughly mid-May for central New Hampshire (simple arithmetic, general practice). Frost dates change with elevation and from year to year, so check a local frost-date source or your county extension office.

**First fall frost.** UNH says the first freeze usually comes in early to mid-October in central and southern New Hampshire and mid-to-late September in the north [12]. UVM says that in Vermont the average first fall frost falls anywhere from early September to late October [13]. Storage potatoes need most of the season, so a late planting in a short-season valley is a risk (general practice).

## Growing

- **Seed.** Buy certified seed potatoes. UMaine says not to use grocery-store potatoes, because they are probably treated so they will not sprout [1]. Seed pieces should be blocky, have at least one eye, and weigh about 1 3/4 to 2 1/4 ounces, or use whole tubers 1 to 2 inches across [1]. UNH gives 1 1/2 to 2 ounces [3].
- **Green-sprouting (optional).** UNH and UMaine both describe spreading seed in a single layer in the light at room temperature for about two weeks before planting, to grow short, strong sprouts [1][3].
- **Depth and spacing.** UMaine: a furrow 4 to 6 inches deep, seed 8 to 12 inches apart in rows 30 to 36 inches apart, covered with about 2 inches of soil at first [1]. UNH: 8 to 10 inches apart in rows 32 to 36 inches apart [3].
- **Hilling.** UMaine says to hill when plants are about 6 inches tall, and that up to three hillings may be needed [1]. UNH: start at 5 to 6 inches and finish at 12 to 15 inches high, covering weeds but not the plants [3]. UVM and Cornell Cooperative Extension: start at 6 to 8 inches and repeat every 2 to 3 weeks [5][6]. Hilling keeps sun off the tubers, which prevents greening [6].
- **Soil.** Fertile and well drained. Soil pH targets differ by source: 5.3 to 6.0 (UMaine) [1], 5.2 to 5.5 to hold down scab (UNH) [3], 4.8 to 5.5 (Cornell Cooperative Extension) [6]. UMaine says manure is not recommended on potatoes because it encourages scab [1]. UNH says any manure should be well composted [3].
- **Water.** About 1 inch a week [5][6]. Cornell Cooperative Extension says to avoid wetting the leaves, which helps prevent blight [6].
- **Pests.** UMaine names late blight as the real threat in home gardens, with early blight, Colorado potato beetle and aphids also common [1]. UNH names aphids, flea beetles and Colorado potato beetles [3]. If you use any spray, follow the label exactly (general practice).
- **Rotation.** UNH: do not plant potatoes in the same spot more than once in three seasons [3]. Cornell Cooperative Extension: avoid spots where potatoes, tomatoes, peppers or eggplant grew in the past 2 years [6].

## Harvest & Storage

**New potatoes.** Penn State says digging can begin about 8 weeks after planting, before the skins thicken [7]. UNH says any time after the plants blossom [3].

**Storage crop.** UNH: dig when soil and air cool and most tops have yellowed, typically around mid-September. Cut or pull the vines 10 days before digging, dig when the soil is dry if possible, and let tubers dry an hour or two on top of the soil [3]. UMaine says to cut or pull vines at least seven days before harvest, and to handle potatoes "like eggs" because they bruise [1]. Cornell Cooperative Extension says to leave tubers in the ground about two weeks after the vines die so the skins set [6]. Get harvested tubers out of the light at once to avoid greening [1]. Use damaged tubers first [7]. Store only sound ones; UNH says to cut out bad spots and use those potatoes right away [3].

**Curing.** Penn State: 2 to 3 weeks in a well-ventilated place at 50 to 60 F and 85 to 90 percent humidity. Do not wash [7].

**Storing.** **The rule this guide uses: hold potatoes at about 40 F, not below 38 F and not above about 46 F, dark and humid.** The sources show different ranges (table below). UNH and UMaine run colder (37 to 40 F, ideal 38 F); UMass, Penn State and Oregon State run warmer (40 to 46 F, Oregon State 41 to 46 F). Their ranges do not all overlap, so about 40 F is the one value close to every source: cold enough to hold sprouting off, warm enough to avoid sugar buildup.

| Source | Conditions |
|---|---|
| UNH "Growing Potatoes" [3] | dark, very humid, 37 to 40 F, on pallets off damp floors |
| UMaine Bulletin #2077 [1] | cool, moist, dark; ideal 38 F and 95 percent humidity; a cool damp basement does fairly well if the floor is not wet |
| UMass/UNH storage table [10] | lower gradually to 40 to 45 F for table potatoes; 90 percent humidity; dark; 5 to 8 months |
| Penn State [7] | dark, 40 to 46 F, 90 percent humidity |
| Oregon State [9] | dark, 41 to 46 F, humidity over 90 percent; in the studies it reviews, toxin levels did not rise and sprouts did not grow over six months at those temperatures |
| Cornell Cooperative Extension (Herkimer County) [6] | dry location at "the lowest temperature possible without freezing"; cool, dark, dry cabinet; "Do not refrigerate" (no degree figure; a kitchen-use sheet, and it conflicts with Penn State's 38 F floor) |

- **Not too cold.** This guide follows the 38 F floor, not Cornell Cooperative Extension's "lowest possible" advice [6]. Penn State says to avoid temperatures below 38 F, which cause sugar buildup [7]. UMass says that at colder temperatures starches convert to sugar [10].
- **Not too warm.** Oregon State says above 46 F potatoes may sprout after two or three months, which can raise glycoalkaloid levels [9]. Penn State says higher temperatures cause decay, sprouting and shriveling [7].
- **Dark.** Light causes greening [1][6][7][9].
- **Keep apples away.** Cornell Cooperative Extension says apples stored with potatoes turn mushy [6].
- **Check often.** Pull soft or rotting tubers before they spread (general practice).

## Seed Saving

Potatoes are grown from tubers, which are clones, not from seed. UMaine says buy certified seed, because it assures varietal purity and a low level of disease [1]. Penn State says it is best not to replant potatoes from your own garden, because they may carry undetectable fungal spores or other disease [7]. The Cornell Cooperative Extension sheet says you may plant tubers from the previous season [6]. The sources that explain the reason advise certified seed, so this entry follows them.

If you cannot get certified seed, small whole tubers from healthy plants are what people replant (general practice). Never replant a tuber from a plant that had blight (general practice; UMaine calls late blight the real threat [1]). Rotate to a bed where potatoes and other nightshades have not grown for 2 to 3 years [3][6][7].

## ⚠ MANDATORY PREPARATION

**Green, bitter, or heavily sprouted potatoes contain more glycoalkaloid (solanine and chaconine), which cooking does not remove.**

- **What causes it.** Light on a tuber, before or after harvest, turns it green and makes solanine and chaconine. They taste bitter, and in large amounts may cause stomach problems, headache and confusion [7][8]. Cornell Cooperative Extension says solanine forms when potatoes are exposed to sunlight or artificial light in storage [6]. Because of the bitter taste, Penn State says it is rare for someone to eat enough to get sick [8].
- **How much green is too much.** Penn State: "Small green spots can be trimmed off." If more extensive greening occurs, throw the potato out [8]. Penn State's gardening page says tubers exposed to sunlight "should not be eaten" [7]. Oregon State says potatoes with pronounced greening or damage must not be eaten [9]. The sources split on small spots, so this guide uses one conservative rule: cut away every green patch and every sprout; if the green covers more than a small area, or the potato is bitter, burning, soft or heavily sprouted, throw it out.
- **Peeling helps, a little.** Oregon State says the skin is far richer in glycoalkaloid than the flesh, and levels are higher still around the eyes, injured areas and sprouts. It says peeling slightly green potatoes lowers the dose, and that potatoes with pronounced greening or damage must not be eaten [9].
- **Cooking does not fix it.** Oregon State says glycoalkaloids are heat stable. Boiling and microwaving have a negligible effect. Boiling peeled potatoes cut the level by about 39 percent in one study, and frying at 210 C (410 F) by about 40 percent, which still leaves a green tuber unfit to eat [9]. Do not count on cooking.
- **Bitter or burning.** Oregon State says levels above about 140 mg per kg taste bitter, and above 200 mg per kg can burn the mouth and throat [9]. **If a potato tastes bitter or burns your throat, stop eating it and throw it out.** Oregon State gives about 12 to 20 mg per kg for a normal tuber and 250 to 280 for a green tuber [9].
- **Sprouts and eyes.** A university poisonous-plant database says sprouted eyes can hold high levels of solanine, and that old and sprouted potatoes and all green parts of the plant are poisonous, particularly the berries [11]. That page is written about livestock. Cut sprouts and eyes out deeply from a firm tuber, and throw out soft, shriveled or heavily sprouted tubers (general practice). Do not eat potato leaves, stems or the small green fruits.
- **Blighted tubers.** The same database says blighted tubers carry more alkaloid than healthy ones [11]. Do not eat potatoes from blight-rotted plants (general practice).

**Symptoms of too much.** Oregon State lists abdominal pain, diarrhea, sweating and vomiting at lower doses, and neurological problems, rapid pulse, low blood pressure, coma or death at severe doses [9]. If someone has stomach pain, vomiting, confusion or a racing pulse after eating green or bitter potatoes, call America's Poison Centers at 1-800-222-1222, and call 911 for severe symptoms. Do not make the person vomit unless Poison Control says to, and keep a sample of what was eaten (general practice).

**Always cook potatoes** before eating them. This entry gives no raw-potato advice and no cooking time, because the sources opened give none (general practice: cook until tender).
