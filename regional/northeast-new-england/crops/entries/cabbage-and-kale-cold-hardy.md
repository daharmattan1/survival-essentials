## Zone Fit & Why It Earns Space

Cabbage and kale are the frost-proof end of the garden. They go in early, they take the cold, and they feed you into winter. UNH sorts fall crops by frost tolerance: carrots, broccoli, cauliflower, lettuce, mustard greens and spinach handle light frost without injury, while beets, kale, collards, radishes and bunching onions may stand up to hard frost [3]. UVM says kale tolerates frost and keeps growing in the cool days of autumn, getting sweeter [4]. Cabbage is not on UNH's hard-frost list, but UNH has it going out four weeks before the last frost [1], and Cornell Cooperative Extension (Warren County, New York) lists cabbage and kale among the crops that survive hard freezes at 25 to 28 F [14]. UNH says young cabbage plants can withstand 10 to 15 F for a night or two, and that mature cole crops will take several frosts or even snow in the fall [15]. Maryland Extension, which is out of region, gives a lower figure for cabbage, heavy frosts and temperatures as low as 15 to 20 F [8]. Treat that as a best case. Heads left in the field below the upper teens are at risk, and UNH's mild-frost line for many fall crops is a minimum of 28 F [3].

Cabbage also earns its space by keeping. In a cold, humid cellar a head can last several months (see Harvest & Storage). Kale does not store that way, but it can be picked from the garden into the cold months.

Cabbage and most kale (curly and Lacinato types) are cultivar groups of one species, *Brassica oleracea*, the same species as broccoli, cauliflower, collards and kohlrabi [13]. Red Russian kale is a different species, *Brassica napus* var. *pabularia* (UVM) [4].

## Planting Windows (Zones 4b-6b)

These go in **before** the last frost in spring, and again in midsummer for a fall crop.

| Source | Crop | When |
|---|---|---|
| UNH planting fact sheet [1] | cabbage | seed indoors 4 to 6 weeks, then set out 4 weeks before the last frost |
| UNH planting fact sheet [1] | kale | seed indoors 4 to 6 weeks, then set out 4 weeks before the last frost |
| UMaine planting chart [2] | cabbage (plants) | central Maine May 1 to August 1; coastal Maine 10 to 14 days earlier; northern Maine 10 to 14 days later |
| UVM Extension [4] | kale, fall crop | midsummer sowing: add 14 days to days to maturity and count back from the average first fall frost |
| UNH fall guide [3] | many fall crops | sow late July through August |

**Last and first frost.** UNH says to find your own last-frost date and treats Memorial Day as a conservative estimate for central New Hampshire [1]. Four weeks before that falls in late April to early May (simple arithmetic, general practice). An early set-out is a judgment call, so cover young plants if a hard freeze is forecast. UNH says first fall freeze usually comes in early to mid-October in central and southern New Hampshire and in mid-to-late September in the north [3]. UVM says Vermont's average first fall frost runs from early September to late October [4].

**Working a fall date.** UVM's method: take the days to maturity on the seed packet, add 14 days because growth slows as days shorten, and count that many days back from your average first fall frost [4]. UNH adds that days to maturity are counted from germination, not from sowing, and that starting seed indoors and transplanting is easier and more reliable than direct seeding in late-summer heat [3]. Shade and water the bed before sowing seed in hot weather [3].

## Growing

- **Spacing, cabbage.** UMaine: plants 12 to 18 inches apart in rows 24 to 36 inches apart; 6 to 7 plants per 10 feet of row; 3/4 to 3 pounds per plant [2].
- **Spacing, kale.** Maryland Extension: 12 to 24 inches apart in rows 30 to 36 inches apart for large plants; closer spacing gives small plants you can cut again and again [9]. The New England sources opened give no kale spacing.
- **Fall sowing.** UVM: pull bolted or tired early crops, weed, add compost, and direct-seed in a space that held a different crop last time. Mulch and keep the soil moist. Shade cloth keeps soil cool and, if it covers the seedlings fully, stops white butterflies laying eggs [4].
- **Pests.** UVM names cabbage worms and cabbage moths [4]. Most insect pests do not survive the cold, so a fall crop is often cleaner than a spring one [4]. If you use any spray, follow the label (general practice).
- **Cold protection.** UNH says many fall vegetables do fine through mild (minimum 28 F) or brief frosts, and that hard freezes can cause some damage. Row covers or breathable cloth trap ground heat; take them off once the temperature is above freezing, so the leaves do not cook. Thick mulch moderates soil temperature [3].
- **Heat.** The sources opened give no temperature figure for heat damage or bolting. UVM and UNH both point to the cool days of late summer and fall as the easier season for these crops [3][4].

## Harvest & Storage

**Kale.** Pick outer leaves, young leaves, or whole plants [9]. Maryland says kale and turnip greens tolerate 15 to 20 F, and that kale may re-sprout from the stems in spring if winter is not too severe [9]. That figure is from a Mid-Atlantic source (out of region); the New York figure for kale is 25 to 28 F [14]. In the colder New England zones, treat spring re-sprouting as a bonus, not a plan (general practice). Kale will keep for 2 to 3 weeks at 32 F and about 95 percent humidity [9].

**Cabbage, harvest.** Cut when heads are firm. Maryland says that if you cannot harvest at maturity, bend the stem over to break part of the roots, which lowers the chance of head-splitting [8].

**Cabbage, cold storage.** UMass gives the best case: 32 F and 98 to 100 percent humidity, lasting 4 to 6 months. Cabbage freezes at 30 F in storage, and storability begins to fall above 34 F. Light in storage can lessen leaf yellowing. Cabbage is highly sensitive to ethylene, so keep it away from ethylene producers such as apples and pears [5]. Maryland gives 32 F, 95 percent humidity, up to 5 months [8].

**Cabbage, refrigerator.** UMaine says use within 2 weeks [7]. UMass says green and red cabbage can keep up to 2 months in the refrigerator, and elsewhere on the same page says to use red or green cabbage within 2 weeks [6]. The figures differ across sources; judge by look and smell, and cut away and discard yellowed or slimy leaves (general practice).

**Freezing, cooked use only.** NCHFP says freezing greens is recommended only for use as a cooked vegetable, because their cells burst when they freeze. Blanching slows the enzymes that spoil them [10].

| Item | Water-blanch time | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Most greens, including spinach | 2 minutes | NCHFP [10]; UNH [11] |
| Collards | 3 minutes | NCHFP [10]; UNH [11] |
| Cabbage | 1 1/2 minutes (wedges, slices, whole leaves) | NCHFP [10]; UMass [6] |
| Cabbage, for drying | water 1 1/2 to 2 minutes, or steam 2 1/2 to 3 minutes | NCHFP [10] |

- Neither NCHFP nor UNH names kale. Treat kale as a "green": 2 minutes is the figure for most greens, and 3 minutes like collards is a reasonable choice for thick leaves (general practice).
- Use about 2 gallons of water per pound of leafy greens. Start counting once the water returns to a boil, then cool in ice water or cold water (60 F or below) for the same length of time. Drain thoroughly [11].
- Pack with 1/2 inch of headspace and freeze at 0 F or lower. UNH says quality holds 8 to 12 months [11]. UMass says use cabbage within 12 months [6].

**Canning.** NCHFP says spinach and similar soft greens can be canned only in a pressure canner, and that "other greens" must be like spinach, not hard firm leaves like cabbage [10]. This entry gives no canning method for cabbage or kale. A boiling-water bath is not enough for low-acid vegetables, and a jar of unknown origin goes in the trash (see `05_food.md`).

## Seed Saving

Cabbage and kale are biennial. They make heads or leaves the first year and flower and set seed the second [12]. They are insect-pollinated and, as one species, cross with each other and with broccoli, cauliflower, collards and kohlrabi [12][13]. This holds for cabbage and for curly and Lacinato kale; Red Russian kale is *Brassica napus*, a different species [4]. Illinois Extension says insect-pollinated crops such as broccoli should be about 200 yards apart for pure seed, and its chart for *Brassica oleracea* crops lists isolation distances of hundreds of feet up to half a mile. If distance is not possible, stagger flowering times or cover flowers with bags [12].

In the Northeast, the hard part is the winter. The sources opened give no New England method for carrying plants through winter to the seed year, so buying seed is the practical route (general practice). Keep seed cool and dry in paper envelopes [12]. It is usually easier to buy seed than to isolate a *Brassica* (general practice). The sources opened give no germination life for this seed, so test a few seeds before planting.
