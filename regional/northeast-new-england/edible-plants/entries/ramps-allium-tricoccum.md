## ⚠ POSITIVE-ID SAFETY GATE

> ⚠ **RAMPS vs. FALSE HELLEBORE: an onion smell is necessary, never sufficient. A wrong call can poison the heart.**
>
> **Positive-ID checklist (ALL must hold):**
> - **Crush one leaf from the single plant you mean to eat. A strong onion or garlic odor must come from that leaf.** Go Botany lists ramp leaves as smelling of onion or garlic, and false hellebore and lily of the valley leaves as having no particular smell [1][2][3]. The smell of a patch, or on your hands from other plants, proves nothing. In an Idaho case (a different plant, same lesson) a whole patch smelled of onion, and an agency botanist thought a poisonous look-alike was growing among the onions [4]. Test plants one at a time and wash your hands between them (general practice).
> - The leaves rise straight from the ground or bulb, one to three per plant, each flat and elliptic. No leaf is attached up a stem [5][6][1][21] (Vermont says false hellebore only a few inches tall is easily confused with ramps [24]; a plant that small may show no stem yet, so this trait clears nothing on its own). **A leaf stalk proves nothing either way:** Go Botany says ramp var. *burdickii* leaves are "not or scarcely petiolate" [1], and the sources disagree on false hellebore (Go Botany: no leaf stalk [2]; Vermont Department of Health: leaves "grow from a stalk" [21]).
> - Any flowers sit in a round cluster on a bare stalk with no leaves on it, and appear only as the leaves die back [5][6].
> - A person who knows ramps and false hellebore by sight has confirmed the plant in person (this guide's judgment).
>
> **Do NOT use if ANY of these are present (disqualifying traits):**
> - **No onion or garlic smell from that leaf.** That is a stop, not a puzzle [6].
> - Leaves alternate up an upright stem, or each leaf wraps the stem at its base [2][7].
> - A downy stem [7].
> - Broad leaves that are strongly ribbed or pleated [7][8].
> - Two leaves growing as a pair, with stalks wrapped around each other near the ground [9].
> - White bells hanging from a stalk beside green leaves, or small yellow-green flowers on a branching stem [10][7].
> - The plant is in a garden bed or ornamental planting [3][9].
> - Any doubt, or you cannot test each plant separately.
>
> **Confusable with:** American false hellebore (*Veratrum viride*) → leaves up a stem, ribbed, clasping, no onion smell → steroidal alkaloids; vomiting, slow heart rate and low blood pressure; deaths reported for *Veratrum* [7][11]. Lily of the valley (*Convallaria majalis*) → paired leaves with wrapped stalks, no onion smell → cardiac glycosides; slow irregular pulse, vomiting, seizures [9][10].
>
> **Verdict:** Positive ID on every checklist item, or do not consume. **This species must never be eaten on field identification alone.** A smell can rule a plant out. It cannot rule one in.

## Deadly / Toxic Look-Alikes

All three plants can be up in the same woods in spring. A paper on *Veratrum* poisoning says the leaves and bulbs of ramps and *Veratrum* look alike in the leaf-only stage [11]. Never clear a plant on one trait. Smell, leaf color, leaf shape and habitat each fail on their own.

### ☠ American false hellebore (*Veratrum viride*)

- **What it is.** An upright perennial 2 to 6 feet tall [7]. Go Botany lists habitat as floodplains, forests, marshes, swamps and wetland margins [2]. That overlaps the floodplain forest where ramps grow [1].
- **Leaves.** Alternate on the stem, with no leaf stalk, the base clasping the stem. Blades 150 to 350 mm long and 100 to 180 mm wide, with parallel veins [2]. NC State: broad, lance-shaped, strongly ribbed, clasping a stout downy stem [7]. University of Saskatchewan: "enormous pleated leaves" [8]. Go Botany's stem-hair entry lists both hairy and nearly hairless, so hairs alone settle nothing [2]. No particular smell [2].
- **Flowers.** Many small yellow-green flowers on a branching stem [7].
- **Young plants.** Vermont's Department of Health says the young leaves of false hellebore can resemble ramps [21]. The other sources opened describe mature plants and none describes the early leaf stage, so do not rely on a mature-plant description for a young one.
- **What Vermont says.** The Vermont Department of Health (April 2023) and the Vermont Agency of Agriculture advisory warn foragers about this mix-up [21][22]. Ramp leaves are flat, grow directly from the ground and smell strongly of onion; false hellebore leaves are pleated, grow in floodplains, marshes and swamps, and do not smell like onion [21][22]. The symptoms are severe nausea and vomiting, which often move on to slow heartbeat and low blood pressure; most people who eat false hellebore need to go to the hospital, and the state toxicologist says it is not a poisoning to treat at home [21][22].
- **What it does.** NC State rates poison severity high. All parts are poisonous if large amounts are eaten: steroidal alkaloids; burning of mouth and throat, salivation, headache, stomach pain, vomiting, diarrhea, weakness, sweating, convulsions [7]. Case papers add a slow heart rate and low blood pressure; one paper says a few severe cases needed resuscitation and some ended in death [11].
- **Documented mix-ups.** A report from a University of Virginia toxicology group describes *V. viride* eaten as ramps, with vomiting, tingling, sweating, slow heart rate and low blood pressure [12][13]. A Georgia couple ate *V. parviflorum* (a southern Appalachian species) as wild leeks, with nausea, vomiting, low blood pressure and slow heart rate [11].

### ☠ Lily of the valley (*Convallaria majalis*)

- **What it is.** An introduced garden perennial that has spread into the wild across eastern North America [3].
- **Leaves.** Basal, paired, elliptic, smooth-edged, parallel-veined, 90 to 260 mm long and 25 to 50 mm wide, on leaf stalks 80 to 240 mm long that wrap a partly buried stalk [3][9]. No particular smell [3].
- **Flowers and fruit.** White drooping bells in May and June, then red berries [10].
- **What it does.** Cardiac glycosides and saponins [9][10]. NC State rates severity low and toxic in larger amounts: slow irregular pulse, abdominal pain, diarrhea, low blood pressure, disorientation, coma, seizures [9]. Germany's risk agency lists all parts as toxic, with nausea and vomiting, and heart rhythm trouble after a large amount, and warns of confusion with wild garlic or autumn crocus [10].

### The smell and the sample

Rubbing a leaf is a way to reject a plant. Do not nibble a leaf to check. Taste is not a test, and NC State lists burning of the mouth and throat among the signs of false hellebore poisoning [7].

## Identification

**Leaves.** One to three broad, flat, shiny green leaves from a bulb [5][6]. Maine gives 10 to 25 cm long and 2.5 to 7 cm wide, tapering to a broad point [5]. Go Botany gives 15 to 40 cm by 1.5 to 9 cm, with parallel veins [1]. The regional key it carries says var. *burdickii* leaves are "not or scarcely petiolate" and var. *tricoccum* leaves are "petiolate" [1], so the leaf stalk is not a reliable discriminator. UNH describes two glossy oval leaves that smell oniony when torn or bruised [14]. NC State says green stems are often reddish near the base [6]. Stem color varies, so it clears nothing.

**Flowers and fruit.** Six white petals in a round cluster on a bare stalk, late June to July in Maine, after the leaves have withered. The fruits are three-lobed [5]. The flowering stalk is 6 to 10 inches tall in UNH's description [14].

**Bulb.** An onion-like bulb, more slender than a true onion [5]. Digging the bulb kills the plant [14]. Do not dig plants to check them.

**Varieties.** Native Plant Trust lists two varieties in New England: var. *tricoccum*, common and in all six states, and var. *burdickii*, rather rare and found only in Maine, New Hampshire and Vermont [1].

## Habitat & Range (this biome)

Ramps grow in rich, moist forests and high-terrace floodplains throughout New England [1]. Maine describes rich, usually alluvial hardwood forest, documented in 38 towns in 11 counties, at the northern limit of its range [5]. UNH describes moist woodland under maple, beech or oak, with trout lily, bloodroot and Dutchman's breeches nearby [14].

**Season.** Leaves appear in early spring: early to mid May in Maine, and they usually wither by the time flowers open in late June to July [5]. UNH says the foliage and bulbs must be harvested before the leaves go dormant [14]. Timing shifts with latitude, elevation and year, so no calendar date is given here (general practice).

**Rarity.** Maine lists wild leek as Special Concern (state rank S3) [5]. UNH calls ramps quite rare in New Hampshire and says var. *burdickii* is considered in danger of going extinct [14].

## Uses & Nutrition

No ⚠ MANDATORY PREPARATION block: the safety work is the gate above.

NC State lists leaves, stems and bulbs as edible, with a pungent garlic-onion flavor, high in vitamin C, to be used sparingly [6]. No calorie figure was found in the sources opened. A ramp patch is a spring seasoning, not a staple (this guide's judgment).

## Cautions

1. **If anyone may have eaten false hellebore, lily of the valley or any unknown plant:** call Poison Help at **1-800-222-1222** now. Do not wait for symptoms [15][21]. For false hellebore, the Vermont Department of Health says to call the Northern New England Poison Center right away, and gives the same number, 1-800-222-1222 (chat at nnepc.org, or text "poison" to 85511) [21][22]. Call **911** if the person collapses, has a seizure, has trouble breathing or cannot be woken [15]. Do not induce vomiting [15]. Keep a piece of the plant and a photo of the plants picked (general practice). Case papers report slow heart rate and low blood pressure treated in hospital, and deaths from *Veratrum* [11]. This is commonly taught first aid, not medical advice.
2. **Over-harvest is the second danger.** Native Plant Trust calls wild leek one of the most over-harvested wild foods and cites a study concluding that taking 10 percent of a patch once every ten years is the maximum sustainable harvest [1]. UNH says the plants grow slowly and recover slowly [14]. Vermont's botanist says harvest hits ramps harder than ferns because the whole plant is usually pulled, and that leaves-only picking, leaving the bulb in the ground, can be sustainable [16]. UNH advises one leaf per plant [14]; NC State says leave the bulbs and one leaf [6]. Native Plant Trust adds that if bulbs are taken at all, leave the base with its roots and wait until seeds have ripened [1]. Skip small or rare patches, and any patch that shows earlier picking (general practice).
3. **Law and land rules (check the land manager; not legal advice).**
   - **Vermont.** On Vermont Department of Forests, Parks and Recreation (DFPR) lands, rule I.2 says no plant may be uprooted or cut without a special written permit, except that wild berries, fruits, seeds, nuts or mushrooms may be collected for personal use at the collector's risk [23]. Ramps (whether the leaves are cut or the bulb is dug) are not on that list, so ask DFPR before picking [23]. Commercial harvest of ramps and fiddleheads is prohibited on state and conservation land; Vermont notes there is no regulation on private property [16][17]. Vermont Fish & Wildlife lands authorize non-commercial picking of berries, nuts, fungi and other wild edibles, except ginseng [18].
   - **Maine.** State parks and historic sites do not allow gathering or collecting of any kind. Maine's public lands page lists no-permit picking of berries, fiddleheads and mushrooms, and does not list ramps, so ask the Bureau of Parks and Lands first [19]. Ramps are a Special Concern species in Maine [5].
   - **New Hampshire.** Rule Res 7301.05 bars removing, damaging, defacing, disturbing or altering any plant on DRED properties. Res 7301.01 applies Res 7301 to all properties of the Department of Resources and Economic Development (DRED) listed in Res 101.06, and extends its operating-season provisions to state parks open in the operating season [20]. The rule text opened names no berry, ramp or mushroom exception.
   - **Other states and federal land.** Rules for New York, Massachusetts, Connecticut, Rhode Island and national forests were not opened for this entry. Check them. No survival exemption is claimed here. Ask the landowner on private land.
4. **Do not use the Universal Edibility Test** on anything that might be a ramp, lily or hellebore (general practice).
