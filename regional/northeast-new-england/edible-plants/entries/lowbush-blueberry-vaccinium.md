## ⚠ POSITIVE-ID SAFETY GATE

> ⚠ **WILD BLUEBERRY: a low-hazard fruit, but "blue-black berries" is not an ID. Match the plant, not the berry.**
>
> **Positive-ID checklist (ALL must hold):**
> - A low shrub: lowbush 4 to 15 inches tall, highbush up to about 6 feet [1].
> - Small leaves set singly along the twig, with tiny regularly spaced teeth on the edge in lowbush (leaves 1.5 to 3.2 cm long) [2][1].
> - The berry is round and waxy, powder-blue (or black with less wax) [1]. In flower earlier in the year, the flowers are small, white or pink and bell-shaped [2][3].
> - Open, sunny, acidic ground for lowbush: old fields, barrens, ledges, mountain tops. Wet ground for highbush [2][3][4][1].
> - Berries come with the plant: you saw the shrub they hang from, not berries on the ground or in a bag.
>
> **Do NOT use if ANY of these are present (disqualifying traits):**
> - Green, erect stems with star-shaped white flowers (yellow center) and clusters of shiny black berries: black nightshade, a high-severity poison [5].
> - Dark blue, bead-like berries on a long stalk above two to four curved leaves, in cool moist woods: bluebead lily, whose berries NC State calls poisonous [6][7].
> - Fruit that is moldy, shriveled or rotten, or the field has been sprayed or you cannot tell (general practice).
> - Any doubt about the plant.
>
> **Confusable with:** black huckleberry (*Gaylussacia baccata*), velvet-leaved or sourtop blueberry (*Vaccinium myrtilloides*) and highbush blueberry: the sources opened list their berries as edible and name no toxic effect [1][8][9][10][4]. The toxic berry plants above are named by the sources opened as poisonous, not as blueberry look-alikes.
>
> **Verdict:** Positive ID on every checklist item, or do not consume. This entry reports what the sources say. It does not say any wild berry is edible for you.

## Deadly / Toxic Look-Alikes

The sources opened name no poisonous plant that is commonly mistaken for wild blueberry. That is missing data, not proof that none exists. These notes are the closest the sources give.

**Edible confusables (named by sources).**
- **Black huckleberry (*Gaylussacia baccata*).** UMaine calls it a weed in wild blueberry fields [1]. NC State: berries edible raw or cooked; leaf undersides have fine resinous yellow dots and hairs [8]. A shrub about 3 feet tall of dry woods, with berries that are dark purple-black [10].
- **Sourtop (*Vaccinium myrtilloides*).** NC State says lowbush is often confused with it [3]. Hairy, light green-white leaves and stems; leaf edges with few or no teeth; berries smaller, less sweet, bright blue with wax [1][9]. It interbreeds with lowbush blueberry, so exact species can be hard to pin down [2].
- **Highbush and half-high blueberries.** Up to 6 feet and in bogs, or 20 to 60 inches near lakes and ponds, with large light-blue berries [1]. Go Botany: highbush on wetland margins, berries mature in August [4].

**Poisonous berry plants (not named as blueberry look-alikes).** These are included because their berries are dark and they can be picked by mistake by a person who does not look at the plant.
- **American black nightshade (*Solanum americanum*).** NC State rates poison severity high: leaves and berries toxic; nausea, vomiting, salivation, drowsiness, abdominal pain, diarrhea, weakness, respiratory depression, convulsions, hallucinations; may be fatal. Green, erect stems; star-shaped white flowers with a yellow center; clusters of green berries that ripen shiny black, each under a quarter inch [5]. This is a North Carolina source; its range in New England was not checked.
- **Bluebead lily (*Clintonia borealis*).** A forest herb with 2 to 4 curved leaves and dark blue, bead-like berries on a stalk [6]. Go Botany lists it in New England forests [7]. NC State rates it low severity and calls the berries poisonous (elsewhere on the page, suspected to be the poisonous part) [6].

If a berry plant does not match every checklist item, leave it.

## Identification

**Lowbush blueberry.** A fire-tolerant, rhizomatous shrub that colonizes open ground. Leaves 1.5 to 3.2 cm long with tiny regular teeth; flowers white to pink, bell-shaped, 4 to 8 mm long. Go Botany warns that blueberry species interbreed, making exact identification a challenge [2]. UMaine: "low-sweet" blueberry, smooth dark green leaves, powder-blue waxy berries, and a black form that is simply less waxy [1]. NC State: juicy berries in summer [3].

**Highbush blueberry.** Taller, with leaves 38 to 80 mm long, in bogs, swamps and wet shores [4][1].

## Habitat & Range (this biome)

Lowbush blueberry is native to northern New England and Atlantic Canada. In Maine it is not planted: it inhabits large fields on mountain tops and glacial outwash plains [1]. Go Botany lists meadows, fields, woodlands, summits, ledges, balds and alpine or subalpine ground [2].

**Season.** UMaine: harvest in August, after which commercial fields are pruned to the ground by mowing or burning and have a leaf-only year before they fruit again [1]. Go Botany: highbush berries mature in August [4]. Timing moves with latitude, elevation and the year; no source opened gives a date for Concord, NH or New York (general practice).

## Uses & Nutrition

NC State describes the lowbush berries as highly edible, enjoyed fresh, cooked or frozen [3]. No calorie or nutrient figure was opened for this entry.

No ⚠ MANDATORY PREPARATION block: nothing in the sources opened makes the ripe berry need a treatment step. Wash your hands before picking and use clean water if washing the fruit [11]. Throw out moldy or rotten berries (general practice).

## Cautions

1. **Managed barrens.** Much Maine wild blueberry land is farmland: UMaine counts 38,000 acres managed by 485 farmers [1]. Go Botany notes that intensive pesticide use in agricultural barrens has reduced pollinators [2]. No source opened gives re-entry intervals, residue limits or spray calendars for people picking by hand. Treat a managed field as sprayed unless the grower says otherwise, ask the owner before picking, and wash the fruit (general practice).
2. **Where you pick.** Vermont's produce-safety advice: scout for animal manure and do not pick the plants it has touched [11].
3. **If someone ate an unknown berry:** call Poison Help at **1-800-222-1222**, and **911** if the person collapses, has a seizure, has trouble breathing or cannot be woken. Do not induce vomiting [12]. Keep a piece of the plant and a photo (general practice). This is commonly taught first aid, not medical advice.
4. **Law and land rules (check the land manager; not legal advice).**
   - **Maine.** The public-lands page lists berry picking as needing no permit. State parks and historic sites do not allow gathering or collecting of any kind [13].
   - **Vermont.** On Vermont Department of Forests, Parks and Recreation (DFPR) lands, rule I.2 lets you collect wild berries for personal use at your own risk; commercial harvest needs a special written permit [16]. Fish & Wildlife lands authorize non-commercial picking of berries, nuts, fungi and other wild edibles [14].
   - **New Hampshire.** Rule Res 7301.05 bars removing, damaging, defacing, disturbing or altering any plant on DRED properties. Res 7301.01 applies Res 7301 to all properties of the Department of Resources and Economic Development (DRED) listed in Res 101.06, and extends its operating-season provisions to state parks open in the operating season. The rule text opened has no berry exception [15].
   - **Other states, federal land and private fields.** Not opened for this entry. Check the agency or owner. No survival exemption is claimed here.
5. **Do not use the Universal Edibility Test** on a berry you cannot identify (general practice).
