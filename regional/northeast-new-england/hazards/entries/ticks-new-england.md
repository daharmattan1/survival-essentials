## Identify

In New England and New York, one tick causes most tick-borne illness: the **blacklegged tick**, also called the deer tick (*Ixodes scapularis*). Vermont's health department says six tick species bite people in Vermont [7], and that of the three species that can carry Powassan virus, only the blacklegged tick commonly bites humans [5]. Maine's CDC calls the deer tick the main carrier of most tick-borne illness reported in Maine [4]. Connecticut's health department names it as the carrier of Lyme disease there [13]. Ticks pass on germs as they feed [3], so the answer is the same for all of them: remove the tick, then watch for illness. You do not need to identify it to act.

| Tick | Where it matters here | What it can pass on, per state health agencies |
|------|-----------------------|------------------------------------------------|
| **Blacklegged (deer) tick** | Named by the Maine, Vermont, New York and Connecticut agencies [4][7][9][13] | Lyme disease, anaplasmosis, babesiosis, Powassan virus, hard tick relapsing fever. Maine and Vermont list all five [4][7]. New York lists the first four under this tick, and names hard tick relapsing fever among New York's tickborne diseases [9] |
| **American dog tick** | One of Vermont's six biting species, active April through September [6] | Rocky Mountain spotted fever, per New York's health department [9] |
| **Lone star tick** | Established in parts of southern New England and Long Island. Not established in Vermont [6][10] | Ehrlichiosis and alpha-gal syndrome (a meat allergy), per New York [9]. Connecticut's experiment station adds tularemia, Heartland virus disease and southern tick-associated rash illness [10] |

**Lone star range.** Connecticut's experiment station reported in 2020 that lone star ticks were established in Fairfield and New Haven counties, Long Island, coastal Rhode Island, and Cape Cod and the Islands. It said they had been detected, not established, in Maine, New Hampshire and New York, and in Massachusetts outside Cape Cod and the Islands, where they are established [10]. That release is several years old and the range was expanding, so check your state agency for the current picture. This entry did not find or open a New Hampshire, Massachusetts or Rhode Island health department tick page, so those three states are covered only through the Maine, Vermont, New York and Connecticut sources; check your own state's health department.

**Where blacklegged ticks wait.** New York's health department says they live in shady, moist places at ground level and cling to tall grass, brush and shrubs, usually no more than 18 to 24 inches off the ground. They also live in lawns and gardens, at the edges of woods and around old stone walls [9].

**When.** Vermont reports that blacklegged nymphs peak in May and June, and that activity rises again in October and November as adults look for hosts [6]. Maine's CDC says the deer tick can be active at any temperature above freezing and that fall brings adult activity [4]. A warm day in November still counts.

## Avoid

- **Stay on cleared trails and walk in the center of them.** Avoid dense woods and brush. Avoid sitting on the ground, on fallen logs or on stone walls [9].
- **Dress so you can see ticks.** Light-colored, tight-weave clothes. Enclosed shoes, long pants and long sleeves. Tuck pants into socks or boots and the shirt into the pants [9].
- **Use an EPA-registered repellent** if you choose a repellent, and follow the label [7][9].
- **Check yourself while outdoors,** then shower soon after you come in. New York says within two hours is best [9]. Do a full-body check that night. Check children, pets, clothing and gear [7][9].
- **Remove an attached tick as soon as you find it.** New York says prompt removal lowers the chance of catching what the tick carries [9]. It does not remove the chance. New York and Vermont both say Powassan virus can pass in as little as 15 minutes [5][8].

## If Exposed / Bitten / Stung

**This is commonly taught field response, not medical advice.**

**Remove the tick yourself.** Vermont's health department says you do not need a health care provider to remove a tick [7]. CDC's steps [1]:

1. Use clean, fine-tipped tweezers. Grasp the tick close to the skin so you do not squeeze its body. Regular tweezers or fingers also work.
2. Pull straight out with steady pressure. Do not twist or jerk.
3. If mouthparts break off and will not come out easily, leave them. The skin pushes them out as it heals.
4. Do not use petroleum jelly, heat, nail polish or similar. CDC says these can agitate the tick and push infected fluid into the skin.
5. Clean the bite and your hands with soap and water, rubbing alcohol or hand sanitizer.
6. Check the rest of your body for more ticks.

**The tick itself.** Put it in a sealed container, wrap it tightly in tape, flush it, or drop it in alcohol. Do not crush it with your fingers [1]. CDC says testing a removed tick is generally not recommended and results should not guide treatment: a positive result does not confirm infection and a negative one can give false reassurance. If you become ill, do not wait for test results [1]. Some labs in this region, such as the University of Maine Cooperative Extension Tick Lab, take ticks for identification or testing [12]. Treat that as information, not as a diagnosis. Note the date and where you probably picked it up.

**Watch for illness.** CDC says a rash or fever can appear within several days to weeks after removal [1]. Maine's CDC and New York's health department both use 30 days as the watch period [4][9]. If a rash or fever shows up, see a clinician and tell them about the bite, the date and the place [1].

**Lyme disease.** CDC says the expanding rash (erythema migrans) begins 3 to 30 days after the bite, on average about 7 days, and can spread to 12 inches or more across. It does not always look like a "classic bull's-eye." Fever, chills, headache, fatigue, muscle and joint aches and swollen lymph nodes can occur with no rash. Later signs can include severe headaches, neck stiffness, facial palsy, irregular heartbeat, nerve pain and joint swelling, especially in the knees [2].

**What the region's agencies list for each illness** [4][8]:

| Illness | Early signs |
|---------|-------------|
| Anaplasmosis | Fever, headache, malaise, body aches |
| Babesiosis | Extreme fatigue, aches, fever, chills, sweating, dark urine, anemia |
| Hard tick relapsing fever | Fever, chills, headache, body and joint pain, fatigue |
| Lyme disease | Fever, headache, joint and muscle pain, rash |
| Powassan virus | Fever, headache, vomiting, weakness, confusion, loss of coordination, speech difficulty, seizures; can involve the brain (encephalitis) or its lining (meningitis) |

Maine's CDC says most early symptoms of tick-borne disease appear within 30 days of the bite, can resemble flu or COVID-19, and that one deer tick bite can pass on more than one infection at once. It also says most infected people have mild or no symptoms, but infections can be serious and even fatal [4].

**Powassan and deer tick virus.** New York's health department says that, unlike other tick-borne diseases, a tick can pass Powassan virus after being attached for as little as 15 minutes [8]. Vermont says one study showed blacklegged ticks passing it to mice in as little as 15 minutes [5]. Symptoms usually start 1 to 4 weeks after the bite. About 10 percent of Powassan encephalitis cases are fatal, and half of survivors have lasting nerve problems such as recurring headaches, muscle wasting or memory problems [8]. No specific medicine treats it. Care is supportive [4][5][8]. Deer tick virus is a variant of Powassan virus. In a study of Maine ticks, every virus-positive tick carried this variant [11]. Vermont has confirmed four human cases (1999, 2022, 2023 and 2025). It says the illness is hard to diagnose and likely underreported [5]. Maine recorded one Powassan case in 2025 through mid-October [4].

**Treatment.** Maine's CDC says most tick-borne diseases in Maine are treatable and most patients recover with appropriate treatment. Untreated infections can cause serious joint, heart and nerve problems, including Lyme carditis and brain infection [4]. If it was a deer tick bite in a high-Lyme area, call a clinician about the bite right away and ask whether any preventive treatment applies. New York says preventive antibiotics are not routine but may help some people after a deer tick bite, which is a clinician's decision [9]. Do not self-treat. Doses are not given here.

## ⚠ WHEN IT'S AN EMERGENCY

**Get medical care, say you had a tick bite, and do not wait for a bull's-eye rash.**

- A fever, severe headache or rash after a tick bite [1][2][4].
- **Confusion, loss of coordination, trouble speaking or a seizure** after a tick bite or tick exposure. These are listed Powassan signs [4][8]. Call 911 (this guide's rule, because they can mean brain involvement [8]).
- Severe headache with a stiff neck, a drooping face, numbness or weakness, or a fluttering or irregular heartbeat [2].
- Dark urine with extreme tiredness, fever and chills (listed babesiosis signs) [4].
- A sudden allergic reaction after eating meat, such as hives, swelling or trouble breathing, may be alpha-gal syndrome, which New York ties to lone star tick bites [9]. Call 911 for trouble breathing. See [anaphylaxis and allergic reaction](../../../../substrate/06_first_aid/entries/anaphylaxis-and-allergic-reaction.md).
