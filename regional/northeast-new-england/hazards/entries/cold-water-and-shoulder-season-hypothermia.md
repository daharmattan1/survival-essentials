## Identify

Two cold-injury problems catch people in the spring and fall in New England and New York. One is **falling into cold water**. The other is **getting wet and chilled on land** on a day that does not feel cold.

**Cold water.** The National Weather Service says a sudden plunge can cause cold shock: sharp changes in breathing, heart rate and blood pressure, with gasping and rapid breathing for the first 2 to 3 minutes or more [1]. Cold shock can be as dangerous in 50 to 60 F water as in much colder water, and gasping can start in water as warm as 77 F [1]. Warm air does not mean warm water [1]. The Coast Guard's Northeast office says Northeast waters stay cold well into spring and used a rescued kayaker in 43 F water as its example [2].

Maryland's natural resources police describe the "1:10:1" principle. It is about **one minute** of cold shock to get your breathing under control, about **10 minutes** of meaningful movement to get help or get out, and about half an hour to an hour before hypothermia knocks you unconscious [4]. Treat these as rough guides, not a clock you can count on. The National Center for Cold Water Safety calls the 1-10-1 misleading: it says your ability to help yourself declines the whole time you are in the water, and it quotes a cold-water physiologist's 2006 text giving cold shock as able to kill within seconds to two minutes and cold incapacitation as occurring within two to thirty minutes [8]. So the two sources do not agree on the numbers; both say to wear a life jacket and dress for the water. The same agency says a life jacket keeps your airway above water during the first uncontrolled gasp [4]. The National Weather Service adds that the loss of muscle control in cold water makes self-rescue or staying afloat without help close to impossible, which is why it says to wear a life jacket [1]. The Coast Guard warns that an unconscious swimmer can drown however good a swimmer they are [2].

**Hypothermia** is a body temperature below 95 F [5][6]. You usually cannot measure it in the field. MedlinePlus lists its causes as long periods in the cold, falling into cold water, wet clothing in windy or cold weather, and heavy exertion without enough food or drink. It also says you are likelier to get it if you are very old or very young, chronically ill, overly tired, or under the influence of alcohol or drugs [6]. CDC says it can occur even at cool temperatures, above 40 F, if a person is chilled by rain, sweat or cold water [5].

**Shoulder season on land.** New Hampshire Fish and Game reported hikers with hypothermia on Mt. Lafayette in June 2026, in cold, wet, rainy conditions, with one hiker unresponsive and severely hypothermic [7]. That was June, so do not treat spring and fall as the only risky months.

**Signs.** Shivering, exhaustion, confusion, fumbling hands, memory loss, slurred speech and drowsiness [5]. MedlinePlus adds pale cold skin, slowed breathing or heart rate, weakness and loss of coordination. Shivering may stop at very low body temperatures [6]. A person may not know they need help [6].

## Avoid

- **Wear a life jacket, not just carry one.** The National Weather Service says to always wear one in and around the water and that it significantly increases the chance of survival [1]. Connecticut law requires everyone aboard a paddlecraft to wear a life jacket from October 1 through May 31 [3]. Other states set their own dates, so check your state's boating agency.
- **Dress for the water temperature, not the air temperature** [1][4]. Connecticut and the Coast Guard both point to a wetsuit or drysuit for paddlers [2][3].
- **Tell someone where you are going and when you expect to be back** [3].
- **Keep your insulation dry.** Pack a spare dry layer and a rain and wind layer, and check the forecast before you go. New Hampshire Fish and Game tells hikers to pack the ten essentials and to check the Mount Washington Observatory's Higher Summits Forecast [7]. If you are wet, cold and tired, stop and add layers rather than pushing on (this guide's rule). See [stop heat loss: ground, wet and wind](../../../../04_shelter.md#stop-heat-loss-ground-wet-and-wind) and the [cold-weather sleep system](../../../../substrate/04_shelter/entries/cold-weather-sleep-system.md).
- **Do not wade a cold, fast river to save time** (this guide's rule). See [river and stream crossing](../../../../substrate/07_navigation/entries/river-and-stream-crossing.md).
- **No alcohol before or during time in the cold.** MedlinePlus lists alcohol among the causes of higher risk and tells people not to drink before going out in the cold [6].

## If Exposed / Bitten / Stung

**This is commonly taught field response, not medical advice.** It matches this guide's [cold section](../../../../06_first_aid.md#cold-hypothermia-and-frostbite) and the [hypothermia protocol](../../../../substrate/06_first_aid/entries/thermal-injury-hypo-hyperthermia.md).

### You fell in (or someone near you did)

The National Weather Service advises [1]:

1. Stay calm. Spend as little time in the water as you can. Use anything that floats and get as much of your body out of the water as you can.
2. If you can swim to safety, do it calmly. If not, save your energy and heat.
3. If you cannot reach safety, use the H.E.L.P. position: knees drawn to the chin, legs together, arms at your sides, head out of the water. If others are in the water, huddle.

**If you are the rescuer** (on shore or in a boat), Maryland's natural resources police give the sequence "Reach, Throw, Row, Don't go": reach with a stick or paddle, throw a flotation device or rope, row closer only to offer help from a safe distance, and do not enter the water yourself [4]. Call 911. Staying with a capsized boat is often safer than trying to swim to shore [4].

### After the water, or on land

**Call 911 whenever you suspect hypothermia** [6]. Call right away if the person is confused, drowsy or having trouble thinking [6]. After a cold-water rescue, NWS also says to call 911 and watch breathing and circulation [1]. Someone out of cold water can keep cooling. NWS says core temperature can continue to drop to dangerous levels, so treat anyone who was in cold water as at risk even if they say they feel fine [1].

1. **Out of the wind and rain and off the cold ground, gently, with as little effort from the person as possible** [6]. Cover the head and neck [6].
2. **Wet clothes.** Once you are somewhere warm and sheltered, replace wet or tight clothes with dry, loose ones [5][6]. In the open, put dry layers and a waterproof layer over the wet clothes instead.
3. **Body heat and dry blankets first.** Warm the center: chest, neck, head and groin. Use your own body heat, skin to skin under dry blankets [5][6]. If you have heat packs, wrap them and put them on the chest, armpits and back, never on bare skin (see the [first-aid page](../../../../06_first_aid.md#cold-hypothermia-and-frostbite)).
4. **A warm, sweet drink only if the person is alert, shivering and can swallow normally.** No alcohol or caffeine [5][6]. If they are confused, drowsy or not shivering, give nothing by mouth. Stay with them.

### ⚠ DO NOT

- **Do not warm quickly or directly.** NWS: do not warm the person too quickly and do not put them in warm water [1]. MedlinePlus: no hot water, heating pad or heat lamp [6]. No stove or fire either (this guide's rule, matching its [first-aid page](../../../../06_first_aid.md#cold-hypothermia-and-frostbite)).
- **No alcohol** [5][6].
- **Do not assume a person lying motionless in the cold is dead** [6]. If they are unconscious and not breathing normally, call 911, start CPR, and keep warming them. CDC says to start CPR even if the person appears dead [5].
- **Do not call a drowsy, confused person "just tired."**

## ⚠ WHEN IT'S AN EMERGENCY

- **Any suspected hypothermia: call 911** [6]. A body temperature below 95 F needs medical attention immediately [5].
- **Confusion, drowsiness, trouble thinking, or shivering that has stopped:** right away [6].
- **Anyone pulled out of cold water:** call 911 and keep watching their breathing [1].
- **Unconscious and not breathing normally:** 911, CPR, keep warming [5][6].
- **Frostbite with blisters, black skin or signs of hypothermia:** treat hypothermia first and call 911 (see the [first-aid page](../../../../06_first_aid.md#cold-hypothermia-and-frostbite)).
