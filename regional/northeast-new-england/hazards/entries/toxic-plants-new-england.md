## ⚠ POSITIVE-ID SAFETY GATE

> ⚠ **FALSE HELLEBORE, WATER HEMLOCK AND POISON HEMLOCK: they look like food, and they can kill or put you in the hospital**
>
> **This list can help you recognize a suspect. It can never clear a plant.**
>
> **Positive-ID checklist for any wild ramp, wild carrot, parsnip or other "wild root or green" (ALL must hold, and an expert must have confirmed it in hand):**
> - You know the exact species, not just "looks like a ramp" or "looks like a carrot."
> - It has none of the traits listed below for the plants in this entry.
> - A person who knows the local plants has looked at the whole plant, with stem base and leaves, in person.
>
> **Do NOT eat, dig or taste the plant if ANY of these are present:**
> - **Early-spring leaves that are pleated or strongly ribbed, clasp a central stem, or have no onion smell** (false hellebore, below). No single trait clears a plant, and a leaf stalk (or no leaf stalk) proves nothing either way [7][8][9][15][16]
> - **A white umbrella-shaped flower cluster on a hollow stem, with a root or seed head you are thinking of eating** (water hemlock and poison hemlock, below) [1][2][3]
> - **Any sign that the plant might be one of the above, or that you cannot say what it is**
>
> **Confusable with:**
> - **False hellebore** (*Veratrum viride*) with **ramps** (wild leeks, *Allium tricoccum*) [7][8][9] → ramp leaves are flat and rise from the base of the plant and smell of onion; false hellebore leaves are pleated and ribbed, alternate up a stem with their bases clasping it, and have no particular smell [8][15][16]. No single trait separates them. Vermont warns that smell alone does not confirm a ramp [7], and a leaf stalk proves nothing: Go Botany lists ramp var. *burdickii* leaves as "not or scarcely petiolate" [16], and the sources disagree on whether false hellebore leaves have a stalk (Go Botany: none [15]; Vermont: "grow from a stalk" [8]) → severe vomiting, then a slow heartbeat and low blood pressure [7][8].
> - **Water hemlock** (*Cicuta maculata*) with parsnips and with wild ginseng roots [12] → seizures; one man died about 3 hours after eating a bit of root in Maine [12].
> - **Poison hemlock** (*Conium maculatum*) with wild carrot and parsnip; its seeds are mistaken for caraway [1] → human deaths have followed eating the root [1].
>
> **Verdict:** Positive ID by an expert, or do not consume. These plants must never be eaten on field identification alone. Do not taste them to check.

## Identify

### False hellebore (*Veratrum viride*): early-spring greens, ramp season

- **What it does.** Vermont's health department says false hellebore is toxic to eat, affects pets and livestock, and is not toxic to touch [7]. It contains alkaloids, and eating it can make people very sick; in most cases people who ate it need the hospital [8]. NC State rates it high severity, with all parts poisonous [9].
- **Look.** NC State: a native perennial 2 to 6 feet tall, with leaves 6 to 12 inches long, lance-shaped, strongly ribbed with parallel veins, clasping a stout downy stem. Small yellow-green flowers in spring and summer [9]. Go Botany: leaves alternate (one leaf per node up the stem), simple, with the base clasping the stem, and parallel veins [15]. Vermont: leaves pleated in appearance [8]. Do not use leaf stalk, smell or any other single trait to clear it (see the gate).
- **Where.** Swamps, wet woods, meadows, forest, stream banks [9]. Vermont says floodplains, marshes and swamps [8], and also says it is found almost anywhere in Vermont: backyards, forests, roadsides, wet soil [7]. Vermont notes some people call false hellebore "skunk cabbage," so a forager who says "skunk cabbage" may mean this plant [7]. A wet or dry place does not clear a plant.
- **When.** It comes up very early in spring [7]. At a few inches tall it is easily mistaken for ramps [7]. NC State also lists pink lady's slipper as a plant it is often confused with [9].
- **Ramps for comparison.** Flat leaves rising from the base of the plant, in rich upland forests, with a strong onion smell [8][16]. These are necessary traits and never sufficient alone. Do not rely on a leaf stalk either way: ramp leaves may or may not have one [16]. Learn ramps from a trusted foraging class before you pick any [7].

### Water hemlock (*Cicuta maculata*)

- **Range and place.** Present in all six New England states [11]. Nearly always in wetlands: marshes, lake and river shores, wet edges [11][2].
- **Related plant.** Bulblet-bearing water hemlock (*Cicuta bulbifera*) also grows in marshes and shores in all New England states, and Go Botany says the entire plant is toxic to humans and livestock, particularly the roots, stem bases and young shoots [17]. Treat every wetland umbel the same way.
- **Look.** 3 to 6 feet tall. Hairless stems, pale green with purple striping, a slightly swollen base and a cluster of chambered taproots [2]. Maine's agriculture department says stems may be green or purple or have purple streaks or spots, and describes leaflets with sharp teeth and veins that end in each notch, and white umbels up to 6 inches across [1]. Purple streaking does not separate it from poison hemlock.
- **Danger.** Maine's agriculture department calls it a native plant where all parts can be deadly, and one of the most toxic in the United States. Seedlings, seed heads and roots are the most deadly [1]. Cornell says toxicity is highest in spring and the lower stem and roots hold most of the toxin. Cicutoxin can also be absorbed through the skin. Do not burn it [2]. CDC says it smells like fresh turnips, and a 2 to 3 cm piece of root can kill an adult [12]. Never taste it to check.
- **The Maine case.** Two brothers digging wild ginseng in a swampy area of Maine each ate root from one plant. Within 30 minutes the younger man vomited and convulsed. He died about 3 hours after eating it. His brother, who took one bite, had seizures and delirium 2 hours later and survived [12]. CDC says there is no antidote and care is supportive [12].

### Poison hemlock (*Conium maculatum*)

- **Range.** An introduced plant, present in all six New England states, in disturbed ground, roadsides, waste areas, forest edges and fields [10]. Go Botany says only a small amount can kill a person [10].
- **Look.** Maine: 4 to 9 feet, bright green fern-like glossy leaves, smooth waxy stems with purple blotches, small white flowers in flat-topped clusters [1]. Cornell: stems slightly grooved, hairless, hollow except at the nodes, with purple blotches, 2 to 10 feet, in flower June to August. Crushed plants have a pungent smell [3]. Do not crush or sniff a plant to identify it.
- **Danger.** All parts can be deadly. The seeds hold the most poison and are often mistaken for caraway [1]. Human poisoning is uncommon and usually involves children handling stems or people mistaking the plant for something edible [3]. Maine reports human deaths from roots mistaken for wild carrots or parsnips [1].

### Sap that burns in sunlight: giant hogweed and wild parsnip

These two carrot-family plants harm the skin, not the gut. Their sap plus sunlight causes phytophotodermatitis, a chemical burn. Touching is enough. Do not eat or handle them bare-skinned.

- **Giant hogweed** (*Heracleum mantegazzianum*). New York DEC: 7 to 14 feet, hollow ridged stems 2 to 4 inches across, green with purple splotches and coarse white hairs, white flower clusters up to 2.5 feet wide, leaves up to 5 feet wide [6]. It is found throughout New York [5] and sporadically in Maine [1]. A reaction can start about 15 minutes after contact. Blisters can form within 48 hours and darken, and scars can last for years [6]. Sap in the eyes can cause temporary or permanent blindness [1][5][6]. Do not mow or burn it [7].
- **Wild parsnip** (*Pastinaca sativa*). Up to 5 feet when flowering, with yellow flat-topped flower clusters, yellowish-green grooved hollow stems and toothed leaflets [4]. Cornell describes 3 to 5 leaflets coming off the central stem and Maine counts 5 to 15, so do not use leaflet count to clear a plant [1][4]. It is common on roadsides and fields across New York [4]. Vermont says the burns can resemble a second-degree sunburn [7]. Cornell says symptoms can last up to two years [4].
- **Cow parsnip** (*Heracleum maximum*) is a look-alike of giant hogweed. Maine says it can cause a blistery rash in sensitive people and that its sap can leave skin very easily sunburned [1].

### Poison ivy (*Toxicodendron radicans*)

CDC NIOSH: urushiol, an oil, is released when the plant is damaged or burned. Eastern poison ivy is typically a hairy, ropelike vine with three shiny green leaves on one small stem. "Leaves of three, let it be" does not apply to poison sumac, which usually has 7 to 13 leaves per cluster. The oil can stay active on objects for up to 5 years [13].

## Avoid

- **Do not dig, taste or eat any wild white-flowered umbel, and do not eat early-spring greens you cannot name.** A wet habitat does not clear a plant.
- **Never make whistles or pipes from hollow stems.** CDC links water hemlock stem whistles to child deaths [12].
- **Wear long sleeves, long pants and gloves** around giant hogweed and wild parsnip, and cover your wrists [4]. Keep sap away from your face and eyes (general practice).
- **Never burn brush that might hold poison ivy or hogweed.** NIOSH says inhaling smoke from burning poison ivy can cause severe allergic breathing problems [13]. Vermont says not to mow or burn giant hogweed [7]. Treat tools and clothing that touched either as contaminated until washed.
- Keep children away from hemlock stems, and keep pets and livestock away from hemlock and false hellebore [3][7].

## If Exposed / Bitten / Stung

**This is commonly taught first-aid guidance, not medical advice. It does not replace a poison center or an emergency room.**

### Something was eaten: false hellebore, either hemlock, or any unknown plant

1. **Call Poison Help, 1-800-222-1222, now,** even if the person feels fine. In Vermont this number reaches the Northern New England Poison Center [8]. **Call 911** for seizure, collapse or trouble breathing [14][18].
2. **Do not make the person vomit** unless poison control or a health care provider says to [14][18].
3. **Have ready** the person's age, weight and condition, when it was swallowed, how much, and the name and part of the plant if known [14]. Take the plant or photos to the emergency room if you can without more handling (general practice).
4. **Do not treat it at home.** Vermont's toxicologist says false hellebore poisoning should not be treated at home [8]. For false hellebore, symptoms can start within 30 minutes to 4 hours and include severe vomiting, a slow heartbeat, low blood pressure, slowed breathing, weakness, dizziness, numbness or tingling, sweating and drooling [7][8]. Most people need hospital care [7].
5. There is no antidote for water hemlock. Treatment is supportive [12]. Treat poison hemlock the same way until a poison center says otherwise (general practice).

### Sap on skin (giant hogweed, wild parsnip)

- **Wash the area with mild soap and water as soon as possible and keep it covered and out of sunlight.** Vermont says soap and cold water [7]; Cornell says warm water and mild soap for wild parsnip [4]. New York DEC and Vermont say keep covered at least 48 hours [6][7]. Cornell gives about 8 hours for wild parsnip [4]. Use the longer time.
- **Sap in the eyes:** rinse with water, shield them from light, and get medical care [6][7].
- See a physician for a reaction or blistering [6].

### Poison ivy, oak or sumac

- **Rinse promptly, and keep rinsing,** so the wash does not dry on the skin and spread the oil [13]. Vermont says to wash as soon as possible, with a urushiol cleanser if you have one [7].
- Call 911 or go to an emergency room for swelling or trouble breathing. Get medical help for severe cases or a rash on the face or genitals [13].

## ⚠ WHEN IT'S AN EMERGENCY

- **Any suspected eating of false hellebore, water hemlock or poison hemlock:** call Poison Help (1-800-222-1222) now. Do not wait for symptoms [8][12]. Call 911 for seizures, collapse or trouble breathing [14].
- **Water hemlock acts fast.** Mild symptoms can start within 15 to 90 minutes, and a death came about 3 hours after a few bites [12].
- **Sap in the eyes, blisters that spread, or blistering with fever:** medical care [6].
- **Poison ivy:** 911 for swelling or trouble breathing, including trouble breathing after smoke from burning plants [13].
