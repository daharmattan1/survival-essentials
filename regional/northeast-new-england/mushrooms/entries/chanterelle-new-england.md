## ⚠ POSITIVE-ID SAFETY GATE

> ⚠ **Chanterelle vs. jack-o'-lantern and false chanterelle — per a Cornell mycology editor's note (2007), the most common mushroom poisoning call in the Northeast comes from this mistake [12]**
>
> **Positive-ID checklist (ALL must hold):**
> - **False gills, not true gills.** The underside has forked, blunt-edged ridges, with or without cross-veins, running down the stem. Never thin, sharp blades [4][7][8][9].
> - **Cut the stem lengthwise on every mushroom. The flesh is white inside,** which may have yellow edges, and is not orange [4][6][7]. Jack-o'-lantern flesh is pale orange throughout (one source, Kuo [11]). **White flesh rules out jack-o'-lantern only.** The false chanterelle also has whitish flesh [13], so the underside still has to be checked: false gills, not true gills.
> - **A cap that is smooth to nearly smooth, on a central stem that is a distinct column** [5][7]. Not a deep vase of soft, flattened scales whose stem flares straight into the cap: that is the scaly vase [20]. Scales alone do not decide it, because *C. camphoratus* caps are described as finely scaly [4]; the vase shape and the missing separate stem do.
> - **On the ground, never on decaying wood** [8][9]. The northeastern species the sources describe grow with conifers such as spruce, fir and white pine [2][3][4].
> - **Cap and ridges yellow to orange-yellow** [4][7].
> - **Fruiting in summer to early fall** [7][8].
>
> **Do NOT use if ANY of these are present (disqualifying traits):**
> - Thin, sharp-edged **true gills** [9][10][11][12]
> - **Orange flesh** anywhere inside the stem [11]
> - A dense cluster on a stump, a tree base or buried wood [10][11][12]
> - Soft, flimsy flesh with thin, repeatedly forked orange gills, on litter or woody debris, often under conifers [13]
> - A deep vase-shaped mushroom with a dark orange to reddish-orange cap covered in soft, flattened scales, shallow forked folds running deep down a stem that flares into the cap, usually under conifers (scaly vase, *Turbinellus floccosus*, formerly *Gomphus floccosus*) [20]. The NAMA registry lists 3 adults with stomach distress, 2 of them with severe cramps, in Colorado, Maine and West Virginia [15]
> - Any mushroom you cannot cut open and check; any bag mixing species
>
> **Confusable with:** Jack-o'-lantern (*Omphalotus illudens*) → true sharp gills, dense clusters on wood, pale orange flesh → severe vomiting, cramps and diarrhea, usually an emergency room visit [10][11][12][14]. False chanterelle (*Hygrophoropsis aurantiaca*) → true thin forked gills, soft flesh → the Forest Service says not recommended [8][13]. Scaly vase (*Turbinellus floccosus*) → scaly vase-shaped cap, stem flaring into the cap, with conifers → stomach distress and cramps in the NAMA registry [15][20].
>
> **Spore print does NOT separate these.** Golden chanterelles print white, cream, yellow or pinkish depending on species [4][5][6][7]; jack-o'-lantern prints white to creamy or pale yellow [11] (cream per Missouri [10]); false chanterelle prints white [13]. The colors overlap, so a print clears nothing.
>
> **Verdict:** Positive ID on every checklist item, confirmed by a mushroom expert who has the specimen in hand, or do not eat it. **This species must never be eaten on field identification alone.** Photos and descriptions are never enough.

## ☠ Deadly Look-Alikes

The sources opened do not call jack-o'-lantern deadly. Missouri says eating it "will make you very sick for a few days" and is not life-threatening to healthy adults [10]. Cornell's post says most victims end up in the emergency room, and an editor's note on that post calls jack-o'-lantern the most common mushroom poisoning call in the Northeast, usually passing without lasting effects [12]. It is listed here because the mistake is common and costly. Dehydration from vomiting and diarrhea is the danger, most of all for children, older people and anyone already ill (general practice; NAMA stresses replacing lost fluids and electrolytes [14]).

### Jack-o'-lantern (*Omphalotus illudens*, eastern North America)

| Check | Golden chanterelle | Jack-o'-lantern |
|---|---|---|
| Underside | Forked, blunt gill-like ridges [4][7][9] | True gills, sharp-edged, close or crowded, running down the stem [10][11][12] |
| Flesh inside | White, with yellow edges at most [4][6][7] | Pale orange, unchanging (Kuo only; no second source opened) [11] |
| How it grows | On the ground, alone, scattered or in groups; some fuse [4][7] | Large dense clusters on stumps, tree bases or buried roots of hardwoods, especially oaks [10][11][12] |
| Cap | Yellow to orange-yellow [4][7] | Bright orange to pumpkin orange; "more orange than yellow" [11][12] |
| Spore print | White to yellow or pinkish by species [4][5] | White to creamy or pale yellow [11] |

What to do: pick one mushroom, turn it over and cut the stem lengthwise. Thin sharp blades, orange inside, or a tight cluster on wood means throw all of it out. A cluster on the ground is also a flag, not a verdict: *C. camphoratus* is described as often clustered or fused [4], so cut every one. Season does not help; jack-o'-lantern fruits July to October [10], the same weeks as chanterelles. Do not use the glow: the gills are luminescent when fresh [11], but Cornell says the glow is faint, needs minutes in the dark and fades after picking, so it proves nothing [12].

Kuo says jack-o'-lantern is widely distributed in eastern North America and frequently found in urban settings [11].

### False chanterelle (*Hygrophoropsis aurantiaca*)

- Kuo: true gills, repeatedly forked, pale to bright orange and soft; cap velvety, flesh thin and whitish; on litter and woody debris, usually under conifers; a soft, flimsy mushroom next to hard-fleshed, sturdy chanterelles; spore print white [13].
- The Forest Service rates it "not recommended" and notes it is often mistaken for the true chanterelle, which grows from soil [8].
- Kuo makes no toxicity claim [13], and no source opened here states how toxic it is. Treat it as not for eating.

### Scaly vase (*Turbinellus floccosus*, formerly *Gomphus floccosus*)

- Kuo: vase-shaped and fleshy, 6 to 14 cm high, with a shallow to deep central depression; the upper surface is scaly with soft, flattened scales, dark orange to reddish orange; the undersurface runs deeply down the stem with shallow folds that are often forked or cross-veined, creamy when fresh; the stem flares into the cap and is not distinctly separate from it; flesh white to whitish; grows with conifers (pines, spruces, firs and hemlocks), summer and fall, in northern and montane North America [20].
- It passes the false-gill, white-flesh, on-the-ground and orange-colour checks, so those checks alone do not exclude it. The scaly cap and the stem that flares into the cap are what differ from a chanterelle.
- The NAMA registry lists *Gomphus floccosus* (the older name) with 3 adults, stomach distress in all three and severe cramps in two, onset 3.5 to 9 hours (average 5.5), in Colorado, Maine and West Virginia [15]. Kuo's page gives no edibility or toxicity information [20]. No source opened calls it deadly. Do not eat it.

### Other chanterelle-like mushrooms

Missouri lists smooth, golden, cinnabar and black trumpet chanterelles as similar species [9]. This entry covers golden chanterelles only. For any other chanterelle-like mushroom it gives no basis to eat; the scaly vase is the one with a registry record in this region.

## Identification

**Underside:** decurrent, forked, blunt-edged folds with or without cross-veins, pale yellow to yellow or pale orange [7]. The Forest Service describes shallow, yellow, blunt gills running down the stalk [8]. In *C. enelensis* the folds are narrow, deeply decurrent and deep yellow to orange-yellow; in *C. camphoratus* they are moderately to widely spaced with blunt, round edges [4].

**Cap:** 1.6 to 14 cm across in Bessette, convex to nearly flat, sometimes depressed, dry and nearly smooth, orange-yellow to yellow [7]. *C. enelensis* caps are 2 to 17 cm and orange-yellow; *C. camphoratus* caps are yellow and finely scaly [4].

**Flesh:** solid stem; "white with yellow borders" in *C. enelensis* and "white with yellow walls" in *C. camphoratus* [4]. Bessette describes the flesh as thick, firm and white [7]. Smooth chanterelle flesh is white and unchanging [6]. The false chanterelle's flesh is thin and whitish [13], so white flesh does not exclude it.

**Spore print:** orangey yellow in *C. enelensis* and whitish yellow in *C. camphoratus* [4] (a newsletter by the species' authors; their original paper in the journal *Botany* was not opened). Bessette gives pinkish cream to pale buff [7]. Kuo lists white, creamy, yellow, pinkish and deep pinkish prints for North American golden chanterelles, notes three species within 65 feet of each other with three different print colors, and says thin prints can look whitish [5].

**Smell:** fruity, apricot-like [4][5]. NAMA says color, insect damage, smell and folklore are not reliable tests of edibility [17].

**Which species:** Kuo says the name *C. cibarius* is strictly European and the North American golden chanterelles were lumped under it [5]. Bessette (1997) and the Forest Service (2012) still use that older name [7][8]. UNH Extension's page labels a chanterelle photo *C. enelensis* [1]. IUCN says *C. enelensis* appears likely to be widespread with conifers in northeastern North America and that *C. camphoratus* is likely more widespread into the northeastern USA than its confirmed Newfoundland, Nova Scotia and Quebec records [2][3]. IUCN adds that most eastern golden chanterelles "are not identifiable to species" without microscopy or DNA [2][3]. Kuo notes other eastern species described from Wisconsin and Texas [5]; the sources opened give no New England records for them. The hardwood-associated smooth chanterelle (*C. lateritius*) is listed as widely distributed east of the Rockies, without a New England record in the source opened [6]. So this entry covers the group. A species name is not needed to run the gate, and cannot be settled in the field.

## Habitat, Substrate & Fruiting (this biome)

- **Grows with:** *C. enelensis* with fir, spruce and white pine; *C. camphoratus* with conifers, more often fir than spruce, in small colonies in open places with low moss [2][3]. The Forest Service lists pine and upland hardwoods [8]. Always from soil, never from wood [8][9].
- **Season:** July to September, fairly common, solitary, scattered, in groups or sometimes clustered (Bessette) [7]; summer to fall (Forest Service) [8]. In Newfoundland, *C. enelensis* runs from the end of July to the end of September, peaking in August [4]. Peak weeks for New England were not found in a source opened and are left out.
- **Poisoning reports:** the NAMA registry lists 96 adults and 2 children with stomach illness from *Omphalotus* (filed under the names *O. olearius* and *O. olivascens*), average onset about 2 hours (0.1 to 6 hours), with Connecticut, Maine, Massachusetts and New York among the places [15].

## ⚠ MANDATORY PREPARATION

Missouri labels chanterelles edible and adds "Always be cautious when eating edible mushrooms" [9]. Bessette says "edible, choice" and the Forest Service rates them "Choice" [7][8]. Correct IDs still show up in poisoning records: the registry lists 17 adults with stomach illness from *C. cibarius* and *C. formosus*, average onset about 1.8 hours [15]. No source opened says to eat a chanterelle raw.

1. Confirm every item in the gate on every mushroom, including the lengthwise cut. Have a mushroom expert confirm the collection first.
2. Throw out any old, decayed or insect-damaged mushroom.
3. **Cook thoroughly** (doctrine point 6). No source opened gives a cooking time or temperature. Do not eat raw.
4. **Eat only a small amount the first time** [9]. A small portion does not clear a doubtful mushroom: jack-o'-lantern illness starts in 1 to 3 hours [14] and amatoxin poisoning, if a deadly mushroom was picked by mistake, can show nothing for 6 to 24 hours [14].
5. Keep one species per container. Never mix collections.
6. If anything about the ID is in doubt, do not eat it.

## Toxicology of the Confusables

### Jack-o'-lantern (*Omphalotus illudens*)

- **Toxin:** long thought to be muscarine, but NAMA says the symptoms do not match classic muscarine poisoning [14].
- **Onset:** typically 1 to 3 hours after the meal [14].
- **Symptoms:** nausea in about 90% of cases, vomiting about 73%, abdominal pain, headache, exhaustion and weakness; sweating and salivation about 15%, diarrhea about 22% [14].
- **Duration:** most victims recover within 12 hours (NAMA) [14]. Missouri says very sick for a few days [10]. Cornell says symptoms pass within a day or two but most victims end up in the emergency room [12].
- **Cooking:** no source opened says cooking removes the cause. Do not count on it.

### False chanterelle

No source opened states its toxicity (see above), so no onset or symptoms are given.

### If someone may have eaten a look-alike

This is commonly taught first aid for when help is slow to arrive, not medical advice.

1. **Call Poison Help, 1-800-222-1222, now** [16]. Do not wait for symptoms. NAMA likewise says not to wait for symptoms to become severe [17].
2. **Call 911** if the person collapses, has a seizure, has trouble breathing, or cannot be woken [16].
3. **Do not make the person vomit** [16].
4. **Keep photos or specimens** of what was eaten [17]. Tell them what was eaten, when and how much.

## Legal note

Picking is regulated and the rule differs by land manager. On Vermont Department of Forests, Parks and Recreation lands, mushrooms may be collected only for personal use at the collector's risk, and commercial harvest needs a special written permit [18]. Acadia National Park prohibits taking mushrooms [19]. In New Hampshire, personal-use harvesting needs no license, while selling to food establishments requires a licensed harvester [1]; that is a food-sale rule, not a land rule. On New Hampshire DRED properties, and on state parks in the operating season, Res 7301.05 bars removing, damaging or disturbing any plant; the rule text does not say whether mushrooms count, so ask DRED before picking on state land [21]. The sources opened do not cover other New England and New York lands, so check the agency before picking. No survival exemption was found.
