## ⚠ POSITIVE-ID SAFETY GATE

> ⚠ **King bolete vs. bitter, blue-staining and red-pored boletes — a wrong pick can mean a bad night, severe stomach illness or worse**
>
> **Positive-ID checklist (ALL must hold):**
> - **Pores, not gills,** on the underside of the cap. Pore surface white when young, then yellow to olive-yellow and later brownish yellow; pores do not turn blue [1][4].
> - **Flesh white (the Forest Service says white-yellow) and unchanging** when sliced, with no blue staining anywhere on the cap, pores or stem [1][4][9].
> - **A solid, thick stem with a fine whitish net (reticulum) over the upper third or more,** and no ring [1][9]. Some eastern edulis-like species have a stem that is not prominently netted, which is one reason an expert is needed [4].
> - **An olive-brown spore print** [1]. This does not separate it from *B. huronensis*, which prints olive-brown too [7].
> - **On the ground in woods,** with conifers such as spruce and pine, and in some references oak and birch [1][4][9].
> - **Flesh not bitter** [2]. Bitterness is how the bitter bolete gets noticed, but tasting an unconfirmed mushroom is not part of this gate (general practice).
>
> **Do NOT use if ANY of these are present (disqualifying traits):**
> - Red or orange pores, at any age [8]
> - Any blue staining when cut or bruised, including slow or patchy bluing [7][8]
> - Yellow flesh that stains blue [3][7]
> - Pores that go from white to pink or pinkish tan, a pinkish-brown spore print, a coarse brown net on the stem, or bitter flesh (bitter bolete) [2][5]
> - A stem with no white net at the top and a bulbous, yellowish base (see *Boletus huronensis*) [7]
> - Gills instead of pores; insect-damaged or decayed flesh; a bag mixed with other kinds of mushrooms
>
> **Confusable with:** *Boletus huronensis* (under hemlock in the Northeast) → bruises blue (sometimes slowly or not at all), yellow flesh, no white net at the stem top → severe stomach illness and reports of vertigo, even after cooking [7]. Red-pored and blue-staining boletes → NAMA lists certain red-pored boletes as causing severe vomiting and diarrhea within 15 to 30 minutes [10]. Bitter bolete (*Tylopilus felleus*) → pink pores, brown stem net, very bitter → inedible [2][5].
>
> **No single trait clears a bolete.** The spore print is olive-brown in both the king bolete [1] and *B. huronensis* [7], so the print does not separate them. White young pores, no blue staining, a stem net and a conifer habitat all fit the bitter bolete too [2][5]. The Missouri rule to avoid orange to red pores and anything that bruises blue removes candidates; it never confirms one [8]. **No blue staining does not clear a bolete,** because Bessette says *B. huronensis* flesh is typically bluing when cut "but sometimes slowly or not at all" [7].
>
> **Verdict:** Positive ID on every checklist item, confirmed by a mushroom expert who has the specimen in hand, or do not eat it. **This species must never be eaten on field identification alone.** The group cannot be resolved to species in the field (Kuo: the eastern edulis-like boletes are "in desperate need of an extensive study" [4]), and its look-alikes cause severe illness. Photos and descriptions are never enough.

## ☠ Deadly Look-Alikes

The sources opened name **no deadly look-alike for this group in New England.** That is a statement about those sources, not a promise that none exists. They do name look-alikes that make people sick. The NAMA case registry records one death among two adults who ate *Boletus pulcherrimus* in Oregon (a West Coast case; the other effects listed were stomach distress, fever and low blood pressure) [11]. Doctrine applies in full, and this entry is `field_id_not_appropriate: true` because the group cannot be settled in the field and its look-alikes make people seriously ill.

### *Boletus huronensis*

Bakaitis's NAMA article places it under hemlock in the Northeast, with a range given in the literature of eastern Canada south to New York and Connecticut and west to Michigan, or New Hampshire west to Michigan [7].

| Check | King bolete | *B. huronensis* |
|---|---|---|
| Pores and flesh | Do not turn blue when cut; flesh white [1][7] | Pore surface bruises blue, sometimes slowly or erratically; pale yellow flesh typically bluing when cut, "but sometimes slowly or not at all," so lack of blue does not clear it [7] |
| Stem top | White net-like reticulum [1][7] | No white net [7] |
| Stem | Thick, solid, whitish [1] | Bulbous base, yellowish, sometimes reddish tints [7] |
| Spore print | Olive-brown [1] | Olive-brown, the same [7] |

Reported effects: severe stomach illness, later reports of intense vertigo, and a cited authority (Lincoff) saying it will "give you a bad night nonetheless" even after cooking. The article does not give an onset time. The author and a companion say they have no intention of eating it again [7].

### Red-pored and blue-staining boletes

- Missouri calls Frost's bolete (*Boletus frostii*) edible but "not recommended" because it can be confused with poisonous red-pored boletes, and says to avoid boletes with orange to red pores and those that bruise blue [8]. Bessette lists the same species simply as "edible," with a dark red cap, dark red pores and blue-staining yellow flesh, on the ground under hardwoods, especially oak, July to October [3]. The sources disagree. Do not eat it on this entry.
- The *B. subvelutipes* group is a brown-capped, red-pored, blue-staining bolete with a stem that lacks a net but has a finely fuzzy coating over the base, under hardwoods east of the Rockies [6].
- NAMA's muscarine group names "certain red-pored" boletes (species not named on that page), with salivation, sweating, tears and severe vomiting and diarrhea within 15 to 30 minutes [10].
- The registry has stomach-illness reports from *Boletus sensibilis* (New Hampshire, 2 adults, severe cramps) and *B. subvelutipes* (New York) [11].

### Bitter bolete (*Tylopilus felleus*)

Pores white at first and then pink or pinkish tan; stem prominently netted with a coarse brown net over at least the upper third; flesh white and unchanging, very bitter; spore print pinkish brown to reddish brown [2][5]. Bessette lists it as inedible because of bitterness and says it is sometimes confused with the king bolete [2]. Bessette gives its habitat as on the ground or on decaying wood under conifers or mixed woods [2], so a bolete on the ground does not clear it. Very young specimens can have white pores, so pore color alone does not separate them [2][5]. Kuo notes North American collections are probably not the same species as the European one [5].

### Others

*Boletus chippewaensis* has a pale lemon-yellow cap splashed with brick to bright rusty red and creamy lemon-yellow pores [1]. No source opened gives its edibility. Treat it as not for eating.

## Identification

**Cap:** brown to yellowish tan or reddish brown, 4.5 to 25.5 cm across; dry and smooth to slightly wrinkled, sticky when wet [1][9]. Eastern edulis-like boletes vary from orangish brown to cinnamon to yellow-brown; one has a finely velvety cap [4].

**Pores:** small (2 to 3 per mm), white when young, then yellow to olive-yellow and finally brownish yellow; they bruise yellowish olive to dull orange-cinnamon, not blue [1].

**Stem:** 5 to 25.5 cm long, 2 to 7.5 cm thick, solid, with a distinct whitish net on the upper third or more; no partial veil or ring [1].

**Flesh:** white and not turning blue [1]; the Forest Service describes it as white-yellow [9].

**Spore print:** olive-brown [1].

**Which species:** Kuo says whether the true European *B. edulis* occurs in North America "is up for debate" and the eastern group is "in desperate need of an extensive study" [4]. The Forest Service says research suggests different strains in North America [9]. This entry covers the group, not one named species.

**Smell, color, insect damage:** NAMA says these are not reliable tests of edibility [14].

## Habitat, Substrate & Fruiting (this biome)

- **Grows with:** Kuo places *B. cf. edulis* under spruces and pines in northeastern North America and other eastern species under oaks and other hardwoods [4]. The Forest Service lists pine, spruce, oak and birch [9]. Bessette says on the ground in woods, especially under conifers [1]. Hemlock stands also hold *B. huronensis* [7], so the habitat does not clear a bolete.
- **Season:** June to October, fairly common (Bessette) [1]; late summer to fall (Forest Service) [9]. Peak weeks and rain triggers for New England were not found in a source opened and are left out.
- **Range:** Bessette's book covers northeastern North America and gives season and frequency, not a map [1]. The NAMA registry has king bolete stomach-illness reports from Connecticut and New Hampshire among other places [11].

## ⚠ MANDATORY PREPARATION

Bessette lists the king bolete as "edible and choice" and the Forest Service rates it "Choice" [1][9]. Even a correct ID has a record of upset: the registry lists 13 adults for *B. edulis* and *B. barrowsii* together (average onset about 2 hours, range 0.3 to 3 hours; Connecticut and New Hampshire among the places). Stomach distress is listed in 11 of them, and the list also includes atrial fibrillation, a slow heart rate (bradycardia), chills, severe cramps, disorientation, flushing, weakness and an itchy throat [11]. NAMA lists *B. edulis* under "Other Alcohol-Induced Syndromes," with stomach distress in susceptible individuals and a delay of up to 5 hours [10].

1. Confirm every item in the gate on every mushroom, including a lengthwise cut and a spore print. Have a mushroom expert confirm the collection first.
2. Throw out old, decayed or insect-damaged mushrooms.
3. **Cook thoroughly** (doctrine point 6). No source opened gives a cooking time or temperature, and none says to eat it raw.
4. **Eat only a small amount the first time** (general practice). A small portion does not clear a doubtful ID.
5. **Skip alcohol** with the first meal [10].
6. Keep one species per container. Never mix collections.
7. If anything about the ID is in doubt, do not eat it.

## Toxicology of the Confusables

- ***B. huronensis*:** severe stomach illness and reported vertigo; onset not stated [7].
- **Red-pored boletes (muscarine group):** onset 15 to 30 minutes with salivation, sweating, tears and severe vomiting and diarrhea [10].
- **Bitter bolete:** the sources opened name the bitter taste as the problem, not a toxin [2][5].
- **Cooking:** a cited authority says *B. huronensis* still gives a bad night after cooking [7]. Cooking does not make a poisonous mushroom edible [13].

### If someone may have eaten a look-alike

This is commonly taught first aid for when help is slow to arrive, not medical advice.

1. **Call Poison Help, 1-800-222-1222, now** [12][13]. Do not wait for symptoms. NAMA likewise says not to wait for symptoms to become severe [14].
2. **Call 911** if the person collapses, has a seizure, has trouble breathing, or cannot be woken [12].
3. **Do not make the person vomit** [12].
4. **Keep photos or specimens** of what was eaten [14]. Tell them what was eaten, when and how much.

## Legal note

Picking is regulated and the rule differs by land manager. On Vermont Department of Forests, Parks and Recreation lands, mushrooms may be collected only for personal use at the collector's risk, and commercial harvest needs a special written permit [15]. Acadia National Park prohibits taking mushrooms [16]. In New Hampshire, personal-use harvesting needs no license, while selling to food establishments requires a licensed harvester [17]; that is a food-sale rule, not a land rule. On New Hampshire DRED properties, and on state parks in the operating season, Res 7301.05 bars removing, damaging or disturbing any plant; the rule text does not say whether mushrooms count, so ask DRED before picking on state land [18]. The sources opened do not cover other New England and New York lands, so check the agency before picking. No survival exemption was found.
