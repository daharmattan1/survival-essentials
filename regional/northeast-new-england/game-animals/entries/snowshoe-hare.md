## Legal & Ethical Note

Snowshoe hare is a regulated game animal in this region. It is taken by hunting in a set season with a state license (Connecticut's guide, for example, lists a firearms or archery hunting license [11]). **No page cited here gives a survival or emergency exemption from season, license, bag-limit or method rules, so do not assume one.** Hunting and trapping are separate systems with their own licenses and seasons. The dates below are hunting seasons only; this guide gives no snare or trap method for hare, and Vermont's trapping-season list names furbearers such as mink, fox and raccoon, not hares [8].

Seasons on agency pages (they change every year):
- **Maine (2026-27):** Sept 26 to Mar 31; 4 a day, 8 in possession. Vinalhaven Island closes Feb 27 [1].
- **Vermont (2026-27):** Sept 26 to Mar 14; units D and E stay open to Mar 31 [8]. The agency lists hare and rabbit together [8][9].
- **New Hampshire (2026-27):** Oct 1 to Mar 31; the bag limit varies by unit [7]. The agency's 2024-25 table showed 3 a day in the northern units and 2 a day in southern ones [6].
- **Massachusetts (2026-27):** Oct 17 to Feb 27; closed during shotgun deer season [10].
- **Connecticut (2026 guide):** Nov 21 to Dec 31 and Jan 1 to 31; 2 a day, 10 a season [11]. European hare has its own Connecticut season and limits (1 a day, 10 a season); do not mix the two [11].
- **New York (2025-26):** Oct 1 to Mar 15 in the Northern Zone, with a later start in some Southern Zone units [14]. Check DEC for the current year.

**Cottontails are different.** Maine has no open season on cottontail rabbit [1]. New Hampshire's cottontail season is limited to a few units, with closed areas, and the New England cottontail is legally protected there [5][6]. See the look-alike section. **Know your regulations and check the state agency; this is not legal advice.** Rhode Island is not covered here; check Rhode Island DEM.

## Identification & Behavior

**Snowshoe hare:** Maine gives 16 to 20 inches long and about 3 pounds, with longer ears and much longer feet than a cottontail [2]. Connecticut gives 18 to 20 inches and 3 to 4 pounds, with long, coarse hair on the soles of the feet [12]. The coat changes over about 10 weeks, from brown in summer to mostly white in winter [4]. The ear tips stay black in winter [2][12]. A white winter animal is a hare, not a cottontail [2]. During the fall and spring molt the coat is mixed (general practice).

**Behavior and habitat:** active at dawn, dusk and night, resting by day in dense cover [2][4]. New Hampshire says they favor lowland spruce-fir with dense softwood understory and cut-over areas, avoid open ground, and are most common in the northern three counties [4]. Vermont says hare are found statewide and are most abundant where forests are actively managed, in the Green Mountains and Northeast Kingdom [9]. Maine calls it an abundant game species statewide [2]. In Connecticut the species had shrunk by 2000 to small remnant populations in the northwest and north-central parts of the state [12]. Droppings are slightly flattened brown pellets [4]. Populations rise and fall in cycles of about 9 to 11 years [4].

## Look-Alikes: Cottontails, Including the Protected New England Cottontail

New England cottontail (NEC) is Maine's state endangered rabbit, now in six southern coastal towns [3]; New Hampshire lists it as Endangered and makes take illegal [5]; it lives in eastern New York, Connecticut, Rhode Island, Massachusetts and southern New Hampshire and Maine, and is gone from Vermont [13]. The eastern cottontail is more common and looks nearly the same [13]. **Do not shoot a rabbit-shaped animal you cannot tell from a protected cottontail where NEC lives.** The cottontail season is open in Massachusetts (Oct 17 to Feb 27), Connecticut (Jan 1 to Feb 28 and Oct 17 to Dec 31) and upstate New York (from Oct 1) [10][11][14], and NEC cannot be told from eastern cottontail in the field, so this entry gives no cottontail hunting guidance.

| Trait | Snowshoe hare | Cottontails |
|-------|---------------|-------------|
| Size | 2.5 to 4 lb; 16 to 20 in [2][3] | NEC 1 to 2.5 lb, 14 to 17 in [3] |
| Ears and feet | Longer ears; very long, furred hind feet [2][4] | Shorter ears and feet [2] |
| Winter coat | Turns white [2] | Stays brown [2] |
| Habitat | Dense conifer, though also in hardwoods [2] | NEC: dense deciduous shrub and young forest [2] |

Between the two cottontails, size and color do not settle it. About half of eastern cottontails have a white star-like mark on the forehead; NEC do not [13][3]. NEC may show a black spot between the ears and a black leading edge on the ear, but these are not always present [5][3]. A young hare can also show a white forehead spot [3]. **The reliable tests are the skull or DNA** [3][5][13][15]. If you are in NEC range and cannot rule it out, do not take the animal.

## Harvest

Hunt with the method allowed for the season and unit. Hare run hard rather than hole up, so many hunters use hounds [9]. This guide gives no snare or trap method (see the legal note).

## Field Dressing & Skinning

1. **Gloves on before you touch it.** Wear latex or nitrile gloves so blood and tissue do not touch your skin [18]; take extra care with cuts or sores on your hands (general practice).
2. **Do not handle a hare that acts sick or was found dead** [16].
3. **Dress and cool small game as soon as possible.** Skin it, wrap the carcass and chill it in a cooler [20].
4. **Open the belly shallowly and lift out the organs** (general practice). Check the liver (see below).
5. **Do not blow on the carcass or hose it hard**, since breathing in contaminated aerosols is one route for tularemia [17]; the older CDC report describes lung cases after people handled unopened rabbits or poked at a dead rabbit [19] (general practice for the method).
6. **Wash hands** well with soap and water afterward (general practice).

---

## Butchering & Preserving

Take off the four legs, the saddle and the rib meat; discard the organs and the spine (general practice). **Refrigerate** at 40°F or below and use unfrozen meat within two or three days [21]. **Freeze** wrapped meat; game meat keeps about 9 to 12 months [21]. Freezing is storage, not cooking, and it does not remove tularemia risk from your hands; gloves and cooking do that.

## ⚠ DISEASE & PARASITE

> ⚠ **TULAREMIA (RABBIT FEVER) — a bacterial disease that can be life-threatening; rabbits, hares and rodents are especially susceptible [17].**
>
> **Screening checklist (ALL must hold; a healthy-looking hare can still carry disease, so gloves and full cooking apply every time):**
> - The animal ran and behaved normally when you took it
> - It looks normal on skinning, with no sores or lumps (general practice)
> - The liver shows no numerous white pinhead-sized spots [18]
>
> **Do NOT handle or eat the animal if ANY of these are present (disqualifying traits):**
> - It was found dead or acts sick [16]
> - The liver has numerous white lesions about the size of a pinhead; discard the animal [18]. This check is written for rabbits; this guide applies it to hares too (general practice)
>
> **How people catch it:** CDC lists tick and deer fly bites, skin contact with infected animals, drinking contaminated water, and breathing contaminated aerosols or dust [17]. An older CDC report says most cases from rabbits come from skin or eye contact with blood and tissue while skinning and dressing, and that hares are susceptible but have rarely been the direct source [19].
>
> **Cold weather helps but does not remove the risk:** winter hunting lowers exposure because ticks and biting flies are less active [18].
>
> **MANDATORY PRECAUTION:**
> - **Wear gloves** when handling the animal [16][18].
> - **Cook the meat thoroughly** [16] (see below).
> - **If you feel ill after handling a hare or rabbit, seek medical care and tell the doctor you may have been exposed to tularemia** [18]. CDC says most infections can be treated with antibiotics [17]; a clinician decides which.
>
> **Verdict:** A normal liver is not clearance; it is only a test for discarding the animal. Do not count on freezing or drying to remove the risk. Gloves protect your hands; cooking protects the meal.

---

## ⚠ MANDATORY PREPARATION

- **Cook to 165°F internal** at the thickest part of the thigh or loin, with a food thermometer. Missouri says heat rabbit meat to at least 165°F to kill any potential disease [18]; the USDA chart as reproduced by UMaine lists 160°F for rabbit [22]. The sources differ; this guide uses 165°F so both are met.
- **Cool and keep cold:** 40°F or below, and use within two or three days [21].
- **Do not eat the liver or other organs** (this guide's rule).
- **Do not eat** a hare that was found dead, acted sick, or had a spotted liver, or meat that smells sour or off (do not taste it to check).
- **Hare is very lean.** Do not rely on it as your only food for long; eat it with any fat or starch you have (general practice).
