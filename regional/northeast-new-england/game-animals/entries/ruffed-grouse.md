## Legal & Ethical Note

Ruffed grouse is a regulated game bird in every state of this region. It is taken by hunting in a set season with a state license; some states add a stamp (Connecticut requires a Resident Game Bird Conservation Stamp with the hunting license [10]). **No page cited here gives a survival or emergency exemption from season, license, bag-limit or method rules, so do not assume one.** Hunting and trapping are separate systems. The dates below are hunting seasons only, and this guide gives no snare or trap method for grouse. Maine's season page adds that, apart from migratory game birds, there is no hunting season on any bird or animal it does not list [1].

**Protected look-alike: the spruce grouse.** Maine lists no open season on spruce grouse [1], and New Hampshire law (RSA 209:4) says there shall be no open season for spruce grouse [17]. Vermont calls it a state endangered species, with a $2,000 fine for killing or wounding one [5]. New York lists it as Endangered and names female spruce grouse shot by hunters as a threat [12]. See the look-alike section. **Know your regulations and check the state agency; this is not legal advice.** Rhode Island is not covered here; check Rhode Island DEM.

Seasons on agency pages (2026 unless noted; they change every year):
- **Maine:** Sept 26 to Dec 31; 4 a day, 8 in possession [1].
- **Vermont:** Sept 26 to Dec 31; 4 a day, 8 in possession [4][6].
- **New Hampshire:** Oct 1 to Dec 31 [7]. The agency's 2024-25 table showed 4 a day [8]; confirm the current limit.
- **Massachusetts:** Oct 17 to Nov 28, zones 1 to 14 [9]. Check the agency for the bag limit.
- **Connecticut:** Oct 17 to Nov 30; 1 a day, 8 a season [10].
- **New York:** the 2025-26 season ran Sept 20 to Feb 28 in the Northern Zone and Oct 1 to Feb 28 in the Southern Zone [11]. Check DEC for the current year.

## Identification & Behavior

**What it is:** Maine's agency describes the ruffed grouse as one of the smaller North American grouse, 17 to 25 ounces, in a gray phase (most common in Maine) or a red phase [2]. Males and females look alike at a glance [2]; New York notes males are slightly larger, with long shiny black neck feathers [13]. New York describes a round, plump bird with a broad, flat, fan-shaped tail that has a dark band near the tip [13].

**Field marks (Maine):** a broad black band on the tail; black "ruff" feathers on both sides of the neck that can be spread like an umbrella; small feathers that point up from the top of the head [3]. The ruff is easy to miss on a bird that is not displaying (general practice).

**Behavior:** it often "perks" its head up when approached, usually flushes and flies when disturbed, or lowers its head and runs for cover [3]. Active by day, most near sunset; in deep snow (10 inches or more) it burrows in and roosts under the snow, then bursts out [2].

**Habitat:** mixed hardwood and softwood woods, old fields and orchards, with aspen the most important food in Maine [2]. Vermont's agency points hunters to overgrown apple orchards, abandoned hillside fields and regrowing clear-cuts with aspen and birch, and to streams and swamps early in the season [4]. Vermont names the Northeast Kingdom as its best grouse country [4].

## Protected Look-Alike: Spruce Grouse

A spruce grouse is protected in Maine and New Hampshire (no open season) and is state endangered in Vermont and New York [1][5][12][17], and Maine says the two species can occur in the same places and may look similar in some light [3]. Vermont's spruce grouse live only in a few pockets of Essex County [5]; New York asks hunters in units 5C, 5F, 6F and 6J to take care not to shoot one [11]. Use more than one trait:

| Trait | Ruffed grouse | Spruce grouse |
|-------|---------------|---------------|
| Tail | Broad black band near the tip [3][13] | Dark brown or blackish tail that ends in a red-brown or chestnut tip, without the broad black band [3][12] |
| Neck and head | Black neck ruff; small upright feathers on the head [3] | Neither feature [3] |
| Male and female | Look alike at a glance; males slightly larger [2][13] | Slate gray and black above, with a bare red patch above the eye [3]; black upper breast and throat [12] |
| Female | Gray or red phase [2] | Gray and brown above, white and brown below; in some light may resemble the red-phase ruffed grouse [3]; brown with black barring and a chestnut tail band [12] |
| Behavior | Perks up, flushes, or runs [3] | Very tame; may let you come within a few feet, crouches, runs a short way or flies to a nearby tree [3][12] |

**Do not shoot a grouse you cannot tell apart from a spruce grouse.** No single trait clears a bird: a tame bird, a dark bird or a tail tip alone is not enough, and the female is the hard case [3][12]. Where spruce grouse live, skip the shot if you cannot see the tail.

## Harvest

Hunt with the weapon and shot allowed in your state for the season. Grouse flush fast and dodge through cover, so most hunters use a shotgun, often with a bird dog [13]. Wait for a clear view of the tail and a clear backstop behind the bird before you shoot (general practice).

## Field Dressing & Handling

1. **Do not handle a bird that is obviously sick or found dead** [14].
2. **Gloves on.** CDC advises gloves when dressing wild birds, an N95 respirator if you have one, and otherwise a well-fitting facemask, plus eye protection [14]. Do not eat, drink or put anything in your mouth while you clean game [14].
3. **Dress it in the field when possible**, with tools set aside for the job [14]. Remove the entrails and the crop promptly; grain in the crop can ferment [15]. Birds may be plucked or skinned in the field [15].
4. **Keep it clean.** Wipe the cavity with a clean cloth or paper towel. Do not use grass or snow, which contaminates the carcass [15].
5. **Cool it.** Do not pile warm birds together; cool them to 35 to 40°F [16]. Hang or lay them in a ventilated place, and bag each bird on ice in hot weather [15].
6. **Afterward**, throw away the gloves and facemask and wash your hands with soap and water [14].
7. **Storage:** refrigerate at 40°F or below and use within three days; freeze at 0°F or lower and use within a year [15].

## ⚠ DISEASE & PARASITE

> ⚠ **BIRD FLU — wild birds can carry avian influenza; gloves, clean handling and full cooking are the controls [14].**
>
> **Screening checklist (ALL must hold):**
> - The bird was alive and behaving normally when you took it
> - You will wear gloves and wash your hands afterward
> - You can cook it to 165°F
>
> **Do NOT handle or eat the bird if ANY of these are present (disqualifying traits):**
> - It was found dead, or looked obviously sick [14]
> - You cannot keep raw bird meat and juices away from other food and surfaces (general practice)
>
> **Verdict:** CDC says to always cook game-bird meat to an internal temperature of 165°F before eating [14]. Gloves and hand washing protect you during dressing [14].

---

## ⚠ MANDATORY PREPARATION

- **Cook to 165°F internal** (NDSU: wild game cooked thoroughly to at least 165°F, juices clear and no pink left [15]; CDC: game-bird meat to 165°F [14]). Use a food thermometer.
- **Cool and keep cold:** 35 to 40°F after dressing; 40°F or below in the refrigerator for no more than three days [15][16].
- **Do not eat** a bird that was found dead or looked sick, or meat that smells sour or off (do not taste it to check).
- **Wash** hands, knives and boards after handling raw bird (general practice).
