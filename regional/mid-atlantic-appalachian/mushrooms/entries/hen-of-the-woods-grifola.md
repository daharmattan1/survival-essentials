## ⚠ POSITIVE-ID SAFETY GATE

Read the [mushroom doctrine and look-alike matrix](../_index.md) first. This entry is a draft and has not had its expert safety read. Expert confirmation is required before eating; this entry's checklist is not a substitute.

> ⚠ **Hen of the woods: a pore fungus with look-alikes of unclear edibility; the sources opened name no deadly look-alike, and that is not proof there is none**
>
> **Positive-ID checklist (ALL must hold):**
> - **Pores, not gills.** The underside is a layer of tiny angular pores, white to yellowish [1]. Kuo describes the pore surface as lavender gray when young, turning white, running down the stem, with about 1 to 3 pores per millimetre [2]. Kuo says it does not bruise [2].
> - **Many small caps from one base.** A clump of fan or spoon-shaped caps, gray to gray-brown on top, each about 3/4 to 3 inches wide [1] (Kuo: 3 to 14 cm [2]), on short, branching, white stalks [1][2].
> - **At the base of a hardwood.** Oak is the main host in every source opened [1][2][7]. It also grows from stumps and roots [1][2].
> - **Autumn.** Late summer through fall [2][7]. See the habitat section for dates.
> - **White spore print** [1][2]. This fits but does not confirm: both look-alikes below also have white prints [3][5][6].
> - **A person who knows the species has looked at it in hand.**
>
> **Do NOT use if ANY of these are present (disqualifying traits):**
> - Real gills, or white gills with a ring or a cup at the base anywhere in your collection (doctrine points 2 and 3).
> - Pores or caps that turn dark brown or black where touched, cut or handled. Three sources give this as the sign of the black-staining polypore, not hen of the woods [1][3][4].
> - A single huge, thick, cream or white cap or clump with wide pores and a rooting yellowish stalk (Berkeley's polypore) [5][6].
> - Ribbon-like folds and no pores (a cauliflower mushroom, not a polypore) [1][5].
> - Old, gray, soft, slimy, rotten or insect-damaged flesh. One source says to use only fresh, tender portions [1].
>
> **Confusable with:** black-staining polypore (*Meripilus sumstinei*): bruises black, finer pores, white when young. Berkeley's polypore (*Bondarzewia berkeleyi*): thicker, paler, wide pores. No opened source says what eating the black-staining polypore does to you; treat it as unknown.
>
> **Verdict:** Positive ID on every checklist item, or do not consume. No opened source names a deadly look-alike, so this entry sets `field_id_not_appropriate: false`, but `expert_id_required: true` stays on. A photo or a description alone is never enough.

## ☠ Deadly Look-Alikes

**No deadly or toxic look-alike is named by any source opened for this entry.** That is what the sources say. It is not a promise that none exists. Missing information is not a green light. The look-alikes the sources do name are below. Each is a polypore or similar fungus that someone may take for hen of the woods.

### Black-staining polypore (*Meripilus sumstinei*)

- **How it differs:** Kuo says it has larger individual caps (5 to 20 cm), a pore surface that is pure white when young, much finer pores, and surfaces that bruise and discolor black [3]. Kuo gives 6 to 8 pores per mm for it [3]. Emberger gives the pore surface as white, bruising black, 4 to 7 pores per mm (the two T2 pages disagree, so call it 4 to 8 per mm against 1 to 3 for hen of the woods), and the cap margin as bruising black where handled or in age [4]. Kuo's hen of the woods page says the same three differences: larger caps, smaller pores, black bruising [2]. Missouri's guide says it "blackens when bruised or handled" [1].
- **Where it grows:** Parasitic and saprobic, from the roots around stumps or living deciduous trees, especially oak, July through November [3][4]. That overlaps hen of the woods almost exactly.
- **Spore print:** White in Kuo [3]. Single-sourced.
- **What it does to you:** The sources opened say nothing on edibility or toxicity [2][3][4]. One nonprofit post says the only look-alikes of hen of the woods are also edible but tougher [8]. That is a Tier 3 claim and does not replace expert confirmation.
- **Why the difference matters:** Black staining is the plain field sign. If it blackens, it is not what this entry describes. Do not use it as the only test.
- **If in doubt, do not eat.**

### Berkeley's polypore (*Bondarzewia berkeleyi*)

- **How it differs:** Missouri describes cream-colored caps 3 to 12 inches wide, wide angular pores running down the stalk, and a yellowish rooting stalk 2 to 4 inches long [5]. Kuo says the cap is white when young, then cream to dull yellowish, with pores 1 to 2 per mm when young that become angular and wider with age, and usually no bruising [6]. Hen of the woods has many smaller gray to brownish caps [5].
- **Where it grows:** On the ground near the bases of deciduous trees, especially oaks, July to October [5]; parasitic on hardwoods, especially oaks, summer and fall [6]. It also returns to the same spot [5].
- **Spore print:** White in both sources [5][6]. Missouri adds that the spores are round, colorless and warted [5]. That is a lab feature, not a field test.
- **What it does to you:** Missouri lists it as edible when young and easy to cut, and says it becomes tough, woody and bitter with age [5]. The same page says to try a small amount first and cook it thoroughly [5].
- **If in doubt, do not eat.**

### Other fungi the sources name

- **Eastern cauliflower mushroom (*Sparassis spatulata*):** ribbon-like folds, no pores [1][5]. Named only by Missouri; no second source opened.
- ***Polyporus umbellatus*:** Kuo says it has more clearly defined individual caps and stems than hen of the woods [2]. Single source.

## Identification

- **Whole fruiting body:** a rounded clump that Missouri says can reach about 3 feet wide and usually weighs 5 to 10 pounds, sometimes up to 50 [1].
- **Caps:** spoon, fan or wedge shaped, gray to gray-brown above, sometimes with vague concentric zones; they yellow with old age and have wavy margins [2]. Kuo's range is 3 to 14 cm across [2]. UMass describes the young caps as lavender gray, turning grayish-brown and sometimes dark brown with age [7].
- **Fertile surface (where the spores are made):** pores, not gills and not teeth. White to yellowish, angular [1]. Kuo gives 1 to 3 pores per mm, tubes 1 to 3 mm deep, the surface running down the stem and turning yellowish with age (an age change, not a bruise reaction) [2]. UMass says the pore layer is usually off-white and turns tan to brown as it matures [7].
- **Stalk:** short, branched, white, often off center, tough [1][2].
- **Spore print:** white [1][2]. Set a cap piece pore side down on dark paper or foil under a bowl for several hours (general practice).
- **Red flags to check every time:** any gills; any black bruising; one big thick cream cap instead of many small gray ones; soft or slimy flesh.

## Habitat, Substrate & Fruiting (this biome)

- **What it grows on:** Kuo says it is weakly parasitic on living oaks and other hardwoods and also lives on decaying wood, causing a white butt rot, and fruits near the bases of trees [2]. Missouri says it grows on the ground at the base of oaks or stumps, in moist hardwood forests and river bottoms with mature or aging trees [1]. UMass names oaks as the primary host and says other hardwoods are attacked less often [7].
- **It comes back:** Missouri and Kuo both say it often returns to the same tree in later years [1][2].
- **When:** Missouri gives September through November [1]. Kuo says summer and fall, and widespread east of the Rocky Mountains [2]. UMass reports fruiting from late summer through autumn, with most in mid-September to early October in southern New England [7]. A Western Pennsylvania Mushroom Club officer's post (Tier 3) describes autumn trips to the bases of large oaks [8]. No regional agency source was opened that gives dates for Virginia, Maryland, West Virginia or Pennsylvania, so treat "September to November" as the pattern the sources support and not as a calendar for your woods.
- **Range and zones:** Kuo gives eastern North America generally [2]. The entry's zone range is the repo's region scope, not a source claim.

### Picking rules: check the land manager first

Foraging rules differ by land manager and change. Check the current rule before you pick. Nothing here is legal advice, and no source opened gives a survival exemption.

- **Shenandoah National Park:** the park says small amounts of edible mushrooms may be collected for personal use, and that collecting or disturbing medicinal or woody shelf mushrooms or other inedible mushrooms is prohibited. It sends readers to the superintendent's compendium for details [9].
- **Pennsylvania state forests:** DCNR's rules prohibit removing a living or dead plant, including fungus, without written permission, and separately permit gathering edible wild plants or plant parts for personal or family consumption. The page does not say whether the second rule covers mushrooms, so ask DCNR [10].
- **George Washington and Jefferson National Forests and Virginia wildlife management areas:** Virginia DWR says personal gathering is allowed in the national forest and on WMAs, and that commercial gathering needs a permit or is prohibited [11].

## ⚠ MANDATORY PREPARATION

**Do not eat hen of the woods raw.** Cook it. Virginia DWR says to always cook wild mushrooms before eating [11]. Missouri says to use only fresh, tender portions and simmer them slowly in salted water until tender [1]. No opened source gives a time or a temperature, so none is given here.

1. **Confirmed ID first.** Everything above must hold, and a person who knows the species has seen it in hand.
2. **Fresh, tender portions only.** Leave tough, old or damaged parts [1].
3. **Simmer slowly in salted water until tender** [1], then cook it further as you would any food (general practice).
4. **First-portion rule.** Eat a small amount the first time and wait for any reaction [1][8]. Waiting several hours is common practice (general practice). Missouri's own Berkeley's polypore page says some people react to certain fungi [5].
5. **Keep one piece for identification later** [11]. Never try two new species at once and never mix collections in one bag (doctrine points 4 and 7). Virginia DWR also says some people get stomach trouble even from edible varieties, so start small [11].
6. **No source opened ties hen of the woods to a problem with alcohol.** The doctrine's no-alcohol point is for inky caps. Skipping alcohol on a first trial is general practice.

## Toxicology of the Confusables

The sources opened give no toxin and no symptom list for the black-staining polypore, Berkeley's polypore or the other fungi named above. This entry will not invent one. What the sources do say:

- Some people react to certain fungi even when the species is listed as edible [5]. Start with a small amount [1][5].
- Virginia DWR says numerous poisonous look-alikes of edible mushrooms exist, and that *Amanita* species are especially harmful and common in Virginia [11]. NAMA describes the amatoxin syndrome as a 6 to 24 hour wait with no symptoms while the liver and kidneys are damaged, a possible apparent recovery around day two or three, then kidney and liver failure that often leads to death [12]. Hen of the woods does not look like an *Amanita*, but a mixed bag can hold one. That is why doctrine says no mixed collections.

**If someone ate a wild mushroom and may be sick:** call Poison Control at 1-800-222-1222 at the first suspicion, call 911 for collapse, seizure or trouble breathing, do not make the person vomit, and save a sample. Details are in [the food guide](../../../../05_food.md#if-someone-ate-something). NAMA says not to wait for symptoms after a suspected amatoxin ingestion [12].
