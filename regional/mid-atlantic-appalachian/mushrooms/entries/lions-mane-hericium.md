## ⚠ POSITIVE-ID SAFETY GATE

Read the [mushroom doctrine and look-alike matrix](../_index.md) first. This entry is a draft and has not had its expert safety read. Expert confirmation is required before eating; this entry's checklist is not a substitute.

> ⚠ **Lion's mane group: a white tooth fungus on hardwood; the sources opened name no poisonous look-alike, and that is not proof there is none**
>
> **Positive-ID checklist (ALL must hold):**
> - **Teeth (spines), not gills and not pores.** The spore-bearing surface is a mass of soft, hanging, tooth-like spines. Missouri gives each as 1/2 to 2 inches long [1]. Kuo gives mature spines as mostly over 1 cm [2].
> - **White when fresh.** Spines are white, aging to yellowish or brownish [1][2]. Minnesota says it is best eaten while white [5].
> - **Shape tells the species group.** *H. erinaceus*: one unbranched clump of long dangling spines with no branches [1][2]. *H. americanum* and *H. coralloides*: spines hang from branches [3][4][5].
> - **On hardwood.** Wounds of living hardwoods, especially oaks, or dead logs and stumps [2][5]. Oak, beech and maple are named [5].
> - **Late summer through fall** [2][5], August to November in Missouri [1].
> - **White spore print** in Missouri's guide and all three Kuo pages [1][2][3][4]. This fits but does not confirm; other white-printed fungi exist.
> - **A person who knows the species has seen it in hand.**
>
> **Do NOT use if ANY of these are present (disqualifying traits):**
> - Overlapping shelves or brackets of short, ragged spines on a living maple or beech, rather than one clump or a branched mass: that is northern tooth, not lion's mane [12].
> - Gills or pores instead of spines, or a cap and stalk with spines under the cap (Missouri says the hedgehog mushroom looks very different, with a cap and stalk and an orangish-tan color) [1].
> - Growing from soil instead of from wood. Every source opened places these fungi on wood, mostly hardwood (Kuo says *H. americanum* is occasionally found on conifer wood) [1][2][3][4][5].
> - Yellow, brown, sour-smelling or soft aged flesh. Minnesota says yellowing means aging and a possible bitter taste [5]; Missouri says it turns sour and bitter with age [1].
> - Any white-gilled mushroom with a ring or a cup in your collection (doctrine points 2 and 3).
>
> **Confusable with:** other *Hericium* species (branched forms), northern tooth (*Climacodon septentrionalis*: overlapping white shelves with short ragged spines), and the hedgehog mushroom (*Hydnum repandum*), which has a cap. No poisonous look-alike is named in the sources opened; Minnesota states "no poisonous look-alikes" [5].
>
> **Verdict:** Positive ID on every checklist item, or do not consume. No opened source names a deadly look-alike, so this entry sets `field_id_not_appropriate: false`, but `expert_id_required: true` stays on. A photo or a description alone is never enough.

## ☠ Deadly Look-Alikes

**No deadly or toxic look-alike is named by any source opened for this entry.** Minnesota's conservation magazine states this mushroom "has no poisonous look-alikes" [5]. That is the source's claim for Minnesota. It is not a promise for Virginia or Pennsylvania, and missing information is not a green light. The confusables the sources do name are other tooth fungi.

### Other *Hericium* species (branched forms)

Sources treat these in different ways. Minnesota calls all three lion's mane [5]. Missouri lists only *H. erinaceus* as "bearded tooth", a choice edible, and names *H. coralloides* as its look-alike [1]. Kuo has a separate page for each species [2][3][4].

- ***H. erinaceus*:** the only one that makes a single clump of dangling spines instead of hanging them from a branched structure [2]. Kuo warns that young branched species may start as one clump, and that young *H. erinaceus* may have short spines, so judge mature fruitings [2]. Kuo also notes that DNA work suggests several species hide inside what is now called *H. erinaceus* [2].
- ***H. americanum*:** a tightly branched body 15 to 20 cm across, spines 0.5 to 4 cm long hanging from the branches, on dead hardwood logs and stumps or wounds of living hardwoods, occasionally conifer wood [3]. Minnesota gives its teeth as shorter than *H. erinaceus*, typically an inch or less, from branches off a central base [5].
- ***H. coralloides*:** branches 0.5 to 1 cm thick, spines 0.5 to 1 cm long, on fallen hardwood [4]. Minnesota says it has the shortest teeth, half an inch or less, in a coral-like form [5]. Missouri calls it the comb tooth, with multiple branches covered in tufts of short spines on decaying trees [1].
- **Spine length overlaps.** Kuo gives 1 to 4 cm for *H. erinaceus* and 0.5 to 4 cm for *H. americanum* [2][3], so do not sort species by spine length. Rely on one unbranched clump versus spines hanging from branches.
- **Name mix-ups:** Kuo says older books call *H. coralloides* "*H. ramosum*", and *H. americanum* is sometimes labeled *H. coralloides* [4]. Check which name your guide uses.
- **Spore print:** white for all three in Kuo [2][3][4]; Missouri gives white for *H. erinaceus* [1].
- **Edibility:** No opened source states edibility for *H. americanum* or *H. coralloides* on its own. Minnesota's page treats all three as lion's mane and says it is best eaten while white [5]. Kuo's pages carry no edibility information [2][3][4]. Treat their edibility as stated only by Minnesota.
- **If in doubt, do not eat.**

### Northern tooth (*Climacodon septentrionalis*)

- **How it differs:** a white toothed fungus that grows as overlapping shelves, not as one shaggy clump. Emberger gives individual caps up to 30 cm wide and 2.5 to 5 cm thick at the base, in overlapping clusters up to 80 cm high; whitish to creamy yellow when young, yellow-brown with age; hairy to rough on top. The spines underneath are crowded, whitish, 0.5 to 2 cm long, with ragged tips, and turn yellowish with age. Emberger says it "looks like a polypore until the spines are noticed" [12].
- **Where it grows:** living deciduous trees, particularly maple and beech, July through October [12]. That overlaps lion's mane in hardwood and season.
- **Smell and taste:** not distinctive when young. Old specimens smell like "old, spoiled ham" and taste bitter [12].
- **What it does:** Emberger's page makes no edibility statement [12]. A Tier 3 page calls it "Non-toxic, inedible" [13]. No Tier 1 or Tier 2 source opened reports it as toxic, and none says to eat it. It is not the target. Discard it.
- **If in doubt, do not eat.**

### Hedgehog mushroom (*Hydnum repandum*)

Missouri says it shares the "hedgehog" name and has spines, but "looks very different," with a cap and stalk and an orangish-tan color [1]. No second source was opened for this one. Do not rely on the shared name; check the shape.

### Conifer and other tooth fungi

MykoWeb (Tier 3) describes *H. abietis* as compact and branching with shorter clustered teeth on conifer logs, and *H. ramosum* as sparsely branched with short teeth in rows on hardwoods [6]. Kuo says *H. alpestre* is a European species that prefers conifers and may be the same species as *H. americanum* [3]. Hardwood versus conifer matters here only for a correct name; it is not a toxicity claim in any source.

## Identification

- **Lion's mane proper (*H. erinaceus*):** a round, unbranched mass of hanging spines with no stalk, 3 to 10 inches across [1]; Kuo says 8 to 24 cm, one clump of soft spines hanging from a tough hidden base on the tree [2]. Mature clumps often have shorter spines on top that look hairy [2].
- **Fertile surface (where the spores are made):** teeth (spines) hanging down. No gills, no pores. Missouri says each is 1/2 to 2 inches long [1]; MykoWeb says up to 5 to 6 cm [6]. Mature spines are mostly over 1 cm; young specimens can have shorter ones [2].
- **Color:** white, aging yellowish to brownish [1][2].
- **Flesh:** white and unchanging when cut in the branched species [3][4]. Odor and taste are not distinctive in those species [3][4].
- **Spore print:** white [1][2][3][4]. A white print on dark paper or foil is a weak check because many fungi share it (general practice).
- **Red flags to check every time:** any gills or pores; a cap and stalk; growth on soil; yellow or brown aged flesh; any specimen you cannot place on wood.

## Habitat, Substrate & Fruiting (this biome)

- **What it grows on:** Kuo says *H. erinaceus* fruits from the wounds of living hardwoods, especially oaks, and is usually found alone or in pairs [2]. Minnesota names fallen oak, beech or maple, logs, stumps and wounds of living trees [5]. Missouri says it grows singly on living deciduous trees and on fallen trees and logs [1].
- **When:** Missouri gives August through November [1]. Kuo says late summer and fall, or over winter and spring in warmer climates [2]; the branched species are the same in late summer and fall [3][4]. Minnesota says late summer through fall [5]. A personal blog from Virginia shows photographs taken in August, early October, late October and early November on downed or wounded hardwoods, including oak (Tier 3, a record of one person's finds) [7]. No regional agency source was opened that gives dates for Virginia, Maryland, West Virginia or Pennsylvania, so treat "late summer through fall" as the pattern the sources support and not as a calendar.
- **Range:** Kuo says *H. erinaceus* is widely distributed in North America from Canada to Mexico [2].

### Picking rules: check the land manager first

Foraging rules differ by land manager and change. Check the current rule before you pick. Nothing here is legal advice, and no source opened gives a survival exemption.

- **Shenandoah National Park:** small amounts of edible mushrooms may be collected for personal use; collecting or disturbing medicinal or woody shelf mushrooms or other inedible mushrooms is prohibited. The park sends readers to the superintendent's compendium [8].
- **Pennsylvania state forests:** DCNR's rules prohibit removing a living or dead plant, including fungus, without written permission, and separately permit gathering edible wild plants or plant parts for personal or family consumption. The page does not say whether the second rule covers mushrooms, so ask DCNR [9].
- **George Washington and Jefferson National Forests and Virginia wildlife management areas:** Virginia DWR says personal gathering is allowed in the national forest and on WMAs, and that commercial gathering needs a permit or is prohibited [10].

## ⚠ MANDATORY PREPARATION

**Do not eat it raw. Cook it.** Missouri says to make sure it is cooked and to sample a small amount first, because some people are allergic to certain chemicals in certain fungi [1]. Virginia DWR says to always cook wild mushrooms before eating [10]. No opened source gives a time or a temperature, so none is given here.

1. **Confirmed ID first.** Every checklist item holds, and a person who knows the species has seen it in hand.
2. **Young and white only.** Missouri says it is tasty only when young and fresh, and sour and bitter with age [1]. Minnesota says yellowing means aging [5].
3. **Cook thoroughly** [1][10].
4. **First-portion rule.** Eat a small amount the first time and wait for any reaction [1]. Waiting several hours is common practice (general practice).
5. **Keep one piece for identification later** [10]. Never try two new species at once and never mix collections in one bag (doctrine points 4 and 7). Virginia DWR also says some people get stomach trouble even from edible varieties, so start small [10].
6. **No source opened ties this mushroom to a problem with alcohol.** The doctrine's no-alcohol point is for inky caps. Skipping alcohol on a first trial is general practice.

## Toxicology of the Confusables

The sources opened name no toxin and no symptom list for any tooth-fungus look-alike, including northern tooth. This entry will not invent one. What the sources do say:

- Some people are allergic to fungal chemicals even in a mushroom listed as edible [1].
- Virginia DWR says numerous poisonous look-alikes of edible mushrooms exist, and that *Amanita* species are especially harmful and common in Virginia [10]. NAMA describes the amatoxin syndrome as a 6 to 24 hour wait with no symptoms while the liver and kidneys are damaged, a possible apparent recovery around day two or three, then kidney and liver failure that often leads to death [11]. Lion's mane does not look like an *Amanita*, but a mixed bag can hold one. That is why doctrine says no mixed collections.

**If someone ate a wild mushroom and may be sick:** call Poison Control at 1-800-222-1222 at the first suspicion, call 911 for collapse, seizure or trouble breathing, do not make the person vomit, and save a sample. Details are in [the food guide](../../../../05_food.md#if-someone-ate-something). NAMA says not to wait for symptoms after a suspected amatoxin ingestion [11].
