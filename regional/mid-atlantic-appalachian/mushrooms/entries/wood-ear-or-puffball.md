## ⚠ POSITIVE-ID SAFETY GATE

Read the [mushroom doctrine and look-alike matrix](../_index.md) first. For the poisoning itself, read the [amatoxin mushrooms hazard entry](../../hazards/entries/amatoxin-mushrooms-amanita-galerina.md).

> ⚠ **Puffball vs. *Amanita* "egg" button: hours of nothing, then liver and kidney failure**
>
> **Positive-ID checklist for PUFFBALL (ALL must hold):**
> - **Cut it from top to bottom, every time, before you pick or cook it** [1][2]. The inside must be **pure white and solid, like a marshmallow,** with **no sign of a developing cap, stem or gills** [2].
> - A round, pear-shaped or cushion-shaped body with no real stem above the ground. Gem-studded puffball is covered with small cone-shaped spines [3]. Pear-shaped puffball is nearly smooth and grows in clusters on decaying wood [2].
> - **A thin skin,** about as thin as an eggshell [5]. A thicker rind may mean an earthball [5].
> - Where it grows does not decide it. Gem-studded puffball grows on the ground [1]. Pear-shaped puffball grows on wood [2][3]. The cut test decides.
> - Summer to fall [1][2][3].
> - **A person who knows puffballs has seen the sliced mushroom in hand.**
>
> **Do NOT use a puffball if ANY of these are present (disqualifying traits):**
> - Any outline, shape or shadow of a cap, stem or gills when sliced. Any shade other than pure white (yellow, olive, brown, purple or gray) [2][5].
> - A hard, thick rind, or an inside that is dark from the start [4][5].
> - You did not cut it, or you cannot judge the cut surface in good light. Discard it.
> - It was bagged with other mushrooms.
>
> **Confusable with:** (1) *Amanita* "egg" buttons, including the destroying angel (*Amanita bisporigera*) → inside, a tiny mushroom shape is folded up; the cut surface is not uniform → amatoxins [1][2][8]. (2) Earthballs (*Scleroderma*, pigskin puffball) → inside dark brownish purple, thick rind → see below [1][4].
>
> **Verdict:** Positive ID on every checklist item, or do not consume. This species must never be eaten on field identification alone. **Slicing every one, top to bottom, is mandatory.**

> ⚠ **Wood ear (*Auricularia*): no deadly look-alike found in the sources opened, which is not proof that none exists**
>
> **Positive-ID checklist for WOOD EAR (ALL must hold):**
> - Thin, rubbery, gelatinous, ear-shaped, wavy or cup- or fan-shaped, about 2 to 5 cm across, often in clusters [6].
> - Brown to reddish-brown on the upper surface; the underside has a whitish bloom and is finely hairy when fresh [6].
> - Growing on decaying hardwood sticks, logs or stumps. Never from soil [6].
> - No gills, no pores, no stem. It becomes hard and black when dry [6].
> - A person who knows the species has seen it in hand.
>
> **Do NOT use if:** it is on soil or looks different from the above; it is a tightly packed, very watery gelatinous cluster (see look-alikes); it is slimy, rotten or insect-damaged; or it was bagged with other mushrooms.
>
> **Verdict:** Positive ID on every checklist item, or do not consume.

## ☠ Deadly Look-Alikes

### *Amanita* "egg" button (destroying angel, *A. bisporigera*, and other *Amanita* spp.)

- **How it differs:** Missouri says some mushrooms, including the deadly destroying angel, have round "button" stages that look like puffballs [1][2]. UBC says a small mushroom shape folded inside may be the egg stage of a dangerous species such as the death cap, and advises cutting the puffball in half from top to bottom [5]. The destroying angel is all white, with a ring on the stalk and a large saclike cup at the base, sometimes hidden underground, and a white spore print [8]. Cornell says a button can also be taken for the edible, brown-spored white button mushroom [9].
- **Why the cut can fool you:** the outline can be faint, especially in an all-white species (general practice). If you see any structure or any non-uniform color, discard it. If you are not sure what you see, discard it.
- **Where *Amanita* species are reported:** see the [amatoxin hazard entry](../../hazards/entries/amatoxin-mushrooms-amanita-galerina.md), which gathers the sources for the Mid-Atlantic. Missouri gives June to November for *A. bisporigera* [8].

### What an *Amanita* does: the amatoxin timeline

Timing is what kills. The sources agree on the shape and differ on the hours:

- **Silent wait.** NAMA: symptoms are delayed 6 to 24 hours after eating, and "typically do not appear until 12 or more hours" [7]. Missouri: 6 to 24 hours [8]. CDC: a latency of 6 to 24 hours [10]. StatPearls: 6 to 12 hours [11]. Cornell: 5 to 12 hours [9].
- **Stomach phase.** Vomiting, diarrhea, cramps [8][9][10].
- **False recovery.** The person seems to improve while the liver is being damaged. NAMA: the patient "may appear to recover about day two or day three and be sent home" [7]. CDC: a recovery phase 24 to 36 hours after eating, and people evaluated early may go home and return later with liver failure [10].
- **Organ failure.** Kidney and liver failure, often leading to death [7]. CDC: typically 3 to 5 days after eating [10]. StatPearls: death can occur in 3 to 7 days; no specific antidote, care is supportive [11].
- **How often it kills depends on how fast good care starts.** NAMA's poisoning-syndromes page gives about 50% without prompt, knowledgeable medical treatment and about 10% in the U.S. and Canada [7]. CDC cites a case fatality rate of 10% to 20% [10]. StatPearls says fewer than 5% of cases in developed countries with early intensive care [11]. The amatoxin hazard entry cites a NAMA review of over 2,000 cases (western Europe, the United States and Canada) with an average of 11.58%. The range is wide on purpose: do not take comfort from the lower numbers.

### Earthballs (*Scleroderma* spp., pigskin puffball)

- **How they differ:** Missouri: the pigskin puffball (*Scleroderma citrinum*) is brownish outside and dark brownish purple inside [1][2]. Kuo: hard, scaly, yellowish to yellow-brown, often cracked, with a whitish rind 1 to 3 mm thick; the inside is hard and purplish gray to purple-black, mottled with white, and turns blackish to dark brown and powdery when mature. It grows with oaks and other hardwoods, summer and fall, often in mixed woods and moss [4].
- **What it does:** no source opened gives symptoms or timing for earthball poisoning. NAMA says stomach-and-bowel irritant mushrooms in general usually cause symptoms within 20 minutes to 4 hours [7]. Do not use that early onset to rule out an *Amanita*: if anyone is sick after a puffball meal, call Poison Control (see below).
- **The same cut test catches it.** If the inside is not pure white, discard it.

### Wood ear look-alikes

- Kuo names *Exidia recisa* (ridged, concave depressions) and *Tremella foliacea* (more gelatinous, a tight fused cluster) as look-alikes. *Auricularia fuscosuccinea*, a southeastern species, looks virtually identical and differs only under a microscope [6]. No source opened says whether any of them is toxic. Kuo's page carries no edibility information [6].
- Wood ear is not known to have a deadly look-alike in the sources opened. That is a statement about those sources, not a promise.

## Identification

### Puffball

- **Shape and skin:** gem-studded puffball is an inverted-pear or cushion shape, 2 to 7 cm wide and 3 to 8 cm high, covered when young with firm cone-shaped spines about 1 mm high [3]. Pear-shaped puffball grows in large clusters on decaying wood, logs and stumps [2].
- **Inside, young:** "white and spongy at first" [3]. Missouri: "pure white" [1]. This is the test.
- **Inside, old:** turns yellowish, then olive to olive-brown (Kuo) [3], then brown dust [1]. An old puffball is not worth eating: UBC says a yellow or brown inside means too old and bitter [5].
- **Spore release:** the mature spore mass is olive brown [3].
- **Names:** Missouri lists the pear-shaped puffball as *Lycoperdon pyriforme* (also *Morganella pyriformis*) [2]. Kuo calls the same group *L. pyriforme* and says it grows on wood [3]. Check which name your guide uses.

### Wood ear

- **Form:** wavy, irregular, often ear-shaped but sometimes disc-, fan- or cup-shaped, 2 to 5 cm across, thin, often clustered, attached at a central or side point [6].
- **Color and texture:** brown to reddish-brown, smooth or wrinkled above; whitish bloom below when fresh; thin, rubbery and gelatinous; hard and black when dried [6].
- **Spore print:** white [6]. Rarely taken in the field.
- **Names:** Kuo says the traditional European names (*A. auricula-judae*) were split by genetic work. The eastern hardwood species has no valid name yet. Kuo writes "*Auricularia americana*" in quotation marks as a placeholder and says the proposed name *A. angiospermarum* is invalid. *Auricularia polytricha* is the Asian name and does not appear on the Kuo page for this region [6]. Do not rely on a species name for ID.

## Habitat, Substrate & Fruiting (Mid-Atlantic / Appalachian)

- **Gem-studded puffball:** on the ground, singly or in clusters, in open woods, along roads and in waste areas, July to October in Missouri [1]. Kuo: late spring through fall in temperate regions, under hardwoods or conifers, along roadsides and in towns [3].
- **Pear-shaped puffball:** decaying wood, logs and stumps, July to November in Missouri [2].
- **Wood ear:** saprobic on decaying hardwood sticks, logs and stumps, spring through fall, especially after rain [6].
- No Virginia, Maryland, West Virginia or Pennsylvania agency source was opened for any of these dates. Treat them as the pattern the sources support, not as a calendar for your woods.

### Picking rules: check the land manager first

Foraging rules differ by land manager and change. Nothing here is legal advice, and no source opened gives a survival exemption.

- **Shenandoah National Park:** small amounts of edible mushrooms may be collected for personal use; collecting or disturbing medicinal, woody shelf or other inedible mushrooms is prohibited. The park sends readers to the superintendent's compendium [13].
- **Pennsylvania state forests:** DCNR prohibits removing a living or dead plant, including fungus, without written permission, and separately permits gathering edible wild plants or plant parts for personal or family consumption. The page does not say whether the second rule covers mushrooms, so ask DCNR [14].
- **George Washington and Jefferson National Forests and Virginia wildlife management areas:** Virginia DWR says personal gathering is allowed, and commercial gathering needs a permit or is prohibited [15].

## ⚠ MANDATORY PREPARATION

### Puffball

1. **CUT AND INSPECT, every one, top to bottom (non-negotiable)** [1][2]. Pure white and solid, or discard.
2. **Use only young, fresh puffballs.** Yellow, olive or brown inside means discard [5].
3. **Cook them.** Missouri: make sure they are cooked [2]; cook them before eating [1]. No opened source gives a time or temperature, so none is given here.
4. **First-portion rule.** Eat a small amount the first time and wait for any reaction [1].
5. **Never mix collections.** Puffballs only in the container, and never try two new species at once (doctrine points 4 and 7).
6. **Keep one uncooked specimen** (sliced, bagged) aside in case anyone gets sick [15].

### Wood ear

No source opened for this entry states that wood ear is edible or how to cook it. As a matter of general practice it is eaten cooked, and the first-portion rule applies. This guide gives no times.

1. Confirmed ID first (the wood ear gate above).
2. Clean off bark and grit (general practice).
3. Cook it (general practice), eat a small first portion, never mix collections.

## Toxicology of the Confusables

See the [amatoxin hazard entry](../../hazards/entries/amatoxin-mushrooms-amanita-galerina.md) for the full course of amatoxin poisoning. The timeline above is the part that matters in the field: **no symptoms for hours, then stomach illness, then a false recovery, then organ failure.** NAMA says amatoxin poisoning is "doubly dangerous" because of the delay [7].

**If anyone may have eaten a mushroom that could have been an *Amanita* (including a puffball you did not cut):** call Poison Control at **1-800-222-1222** now, even if they feel fine [12]. Call **911** if the person collapses, has a seizure, has trouble breathing or cannot be woken. Do not make the person vomit unless Poison Control says to. Save a sample of what was eaten in a paper bag and photograph it. Go to a hospital; early care matters and easing symptoms are not a reason to stop. Details: [the food guide](../../../../05_food.md#if-someone-ate-something). This is commonly taught guidance for when professional care is far away, not medical advice.
