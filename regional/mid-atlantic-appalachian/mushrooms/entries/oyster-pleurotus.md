## ⚠ POSITIVE-ID SAFETY GATE

> ⚠ **Oyster vs. Jack-o'-lantern — deadly GI toxin**
>
> **Positive-ID checklist (ALL must hold):**
> - Cap is **white, tan, or pale gray** (not orange or reddish)
> - **Off-center stem or no stem** — the cap attaches to the wood at an angle, not centered like a traditional mushroom
> - **Gills run downward** (decurrent, toward the stem) — they reach the stem and run along its length
> - **Clustered shelf-like growth** on dead wood — typically grow in overlapping tiers or bouquets on logs and fallen branches
> - **Growing on dead hardwood** (oak, beech, ash, birch, etc.) — no buried wood roots or disturbed soil
> - **Spore print is white to pale lilac** — must be taken to confirm (place mushroom gill-side-down on white paper for 8+ hours)
>
> **Do NOT use if ANY of these are present (disqualifying traits):**
> - Cap is bright orange or orange-red
> - Mushroom grows in dense clusters on buried wood roots or in clumps in soil (off buried wood) — characteristic of Jack-o'-lantern
> - Gills have true sharp edges (thin, blade-like) vs. the decurrent blunt ridges of oyster
> - Spore print is orange or yellow
> - Growing in summer/fall from wood partially buried in soil or roots
>
> **Confusable with:** Jack-o'-lantern (*Omphalotus illudens*, eastern NA species) → **Jack-o'-lantern: orange, true gills, grows on buried wood/roots in clumps, often glows faintly** → *Omphalotus* (illudin toxins): severe GI illness.
>
> **Verdict:** Positive ID on cap color + attachment style + spore print, expert confirmation recommended, or do not consume. **This species must never be eaten on field identification alone.** Deadly look-alike present.

## ☠ Deadly Look-Alikes

### Jack-o'-lantern (*Omphalotus illudens* — eastern NA species)

**The discriminators (verbatim from the deadly-look-alike matrix):**
- Oyster: **white-to-tan, off-center stub or no stem, decurrent gills, on dead hardwood, grows in shelving clusters**
- Jack-o'-lantern: **orange**, true gills, grows on **buried wood/roots in clumps**, often glows faintly

**What it does:** Jack-o'-lantern contains **illudins** (DNA-alkylating cytotoxins), which cause severe gastrointestinal illness: rapid onset of nausea, vomiting, severe cramping, and watery diarrhea. Illudins are NOT cholinergic agonists — whether muscarine is also present is unconfirmed, and the clinical picture is severe GI illness, not a cholinergic syndrome. Some reports include salivation and muscular weakness, but these are secondary to GI distress. Rarely fatal in healthy adults. Hospitalization for severe dehydration is possible.

**Why this matters:** Jack-o'-lantern is common, especially on buried stumps and partially decomposed roots in disturbed ground. Its bright orange color attracts foragers, but it is unmistakably a look-alike *when confusion occurs*. The key discriminators are **cap color** (oyster = white/tan; jack-o'-lantern = orange) and **spore print** (oyster = white/pale lilac; jack-o'-lantern = **white-to-cream** — note: the spore print is white, NOT orange, even though the cap and gills are orange). Do not rely on spore print alone to distinguish these two; use cap color and growth habit as primary discriminators.

### False Oyster (*Pleurocybella* spp., *Mycena* spp., and other gilled imitators)

Some small gilled fungi on wood mimic oyster shape but are inedible or toxic. Distinguish by spore print color and gill attachment details.

## Identification

**Cap:**
- **White, cream, tan, or pale gray** — never orange or reddish
- **Shell or fan-shaped** — often asymmetrical
- **Smooth or slightly wrinkled** surface
- **Tender and fragile** when young; becomes tougher with age
- Diameter typically 5–20 cm

**Gills:**
- **White or pale gray** — not yellow or orange
- **Decurrent** — run downward and attach along the stem for the full length
- Close together (crowded)
- Blunt edges (not sharp knife-edge typical of jack-o'-lantern)

**Stem (stipe):**
- **Absent or very short** (stub only a few millimeters to 1–2 cm)
- **Off-center** or lateral attachment to the cap
- White or pale gray; no ring or volva

**Flesh:**
- Thin, white, tender when young
- Becomes tough and fibrous with age

**Bruising:**
- Does not bruise noticeably

**Spore print (REQUIRED FOR CONFIDENT ID):**
- **White to pale lilac** — absolutely critical; if unsure, spend the time to take a print
- Place mushroom cap (gills down) on white paper; cover loosely and leave overnight (8–12 hours)
- Jack-o'-lantern (*O. illudens*) produces a **white-to-cream spore print** — the cap and gills are orange, but the spores are white; spore print alone does NOT separate these two species; rely on cap color (white/tan vs. orange) and growth habit as primary discriminators

**Smell:**
- Faint, pleasant, or slightly fruity; not a primary ID trait

**Bioluminescence (informational):**
- Jack-o'-lantern can faintly glow in the dark (bioluminescence)
- Oyster does not glow
- This is NOT reliable for field identification but can be supportive

## Habitat, Substrate & Fruiting (Mid-Atlantic / Appalachian, Zone 7a)

**What it grows on:**
- Dead hardwood: **oak, beech, ash, birch, hickory, other hardwoods**
- Logs, stumps, fallen branches on the forest floor
- **NOT on buried wood/roots** (that's jack-o'-lantern's preference)
- **NOT on conifers**

**Where in the region:**
- Forests, woodlots, parks, gardens with dead wood
- Stream banks and disturbed areas with fallen timber

**Fruiting season:**
- **Year-round potential** (species-dependent; eastern *Pleurotus* spp. vary)
- **Primary flush:** fall (September–November)
- **Secondary flushes:** spring (March–April) and summer after rain
- More frequent in moist, mild years

**After what weather:**
- Fruits after rain or high humidity
- Temperature fluctuation (warm day after cold snap) stimulates fruiting
- Can persist on the same log for multiple years

## ⚠ MANDATORY PREPARATION

Oyster is **not safely eaten raw** and requires cooking for digestibility and safety.

**Preparation protocol:**
1. **Harvest tender, young specimens:** select firm, freshly-opened oysters from young fruitings. Discard old, gray, hardened specimens (indigestible).
2. **Clean:** brush dirt; do not soak.
3. **Cut into bite-sized pieces** and blanch in salted boiling water for 3–5 minutes. This softens the texture and reduces chitin load.
4. **Sauté, roast, or simmer** in soups/broths until tender. Do not eat raw or barely-cooked specimens.
5. **First-portion rule:** after full preparation, consume a small portion (2–3 small pieces) with no other new foods or alcohol. Wait 6–8 hours for GI reaction. Only if no upset occurs, continue eating.
6. **Avoid alcohol:** some individuals report GI distress if oyster is paired with beer or wine during the first trial; use water.
7. **Spore print verification:** For any ambiguous specimen, take a spore print before cooking. White/pale lilac = consistent with oyster. Note: *O. illudens* also has a white-to-cream spore print, so spore print alone does NOT rule out jack-o'-lantern. Discard any orange-capped specimen regardless of spore print.
8. **Never mix collections:** only oyster mushrooms in your container; do not combine with other wild mushrooms in a single bag.

## Toxicology of the Confusables

### Jack-o'-lantern (*Omphalotus illudens*) — Illudin Toxins

**The toxins:** Jack-o'-lantern contains **illudins** (sesquiterpene-derived DNA-alkylating cytotoxins) and related compounds. These are thermostable and cannot be cooked out. Illudins are NOT cholinergic agonists — they cause GI damage via cytotoxic mechanisms. Whether any muscarine is also present is unconfirmed; the primary clinical syndrome is severe GI illness.

**Timeline & symptoms:**
- **Onset:** 30 minutes to 4 hours post-consumption (rapid, compared to amatoxins)
- **Phase 1 (early):** excessive salivation, lacrimation, nausea, cramping
- **Phase 2 (peak):** severe vomiting, watery diarrhea (can lead to rapid dehydration), abdominal pain, muscle weakness
- **Duration:** typically 6–24 hours; recovery is usually complete without residual harm
- **Severity:** moderate to severe; dehydration risk in young children, elderly, or those with pre-existing GI conditions

**No antidote; supportive care only:** treatment is hydration (IV fluids if severe) and electrolyte monitoring. Antiemetics may be used to reduce vomiting.

**Lesson:** the rapid onset (within 1–4 hours) and severe vomiting make this a *clearly unpleasant* poisoning, but recovery is typically complete. The danger is dehydration and aspiration risk if vomiting is severe.
