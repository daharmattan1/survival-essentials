## ⚠ POSITIVE-ID SAFETY GATE

> ⚠ **Chicken-of-the-woods: some people react even to the right species, and the wrong host tree or the orange look-alike makes it worse**
>
> **Positive-ID checklist (ALL must hold):**
> - **Shelf or bracket with PORES, not gills.** *L. sulphureus* has bright to dull yellow pores, 2 to 4 per mm, that do not bruise [1].
> - **Bright yellow to bright orange cap when fresh**, overlapping shelving clusters, caps 5 to 25 cm across [1]. (*L. cincinnatus*: pinkish orange cap, whitish pores, rosettes at the base of a tree [1].)
> - **Growing on a hardwood you can name**, most often living or dead oak [1][2].
> - **You can say what tree it is on.** If you cannot name the host, do not eat it.
> - **Young and fresh.** Old specimens turn brittle and sour [3].
>
> **Do NOT use if ANY of these are present (disqualifying traits):**
> - **True gills** underneath, or orange flesh and a dense cluster at a stump or buried root (jack-o'-lantern) [4][5]
> - Growing on **conifer wood** (any conifer): in the East this is *L. huroniensis*, reported to cause poisoning more often [1][3][13]
> - Growing on **eucalyptus** (a western-US warning in the sources) [3]
> - Growing on **yew** (*Taxus*) or any other conifer: a 2026 peer-reviewed review says collecting from conifers, particularly yew, must be avoided [13]. Hard no
> - Old, tough, sour or decayed
> - Anyone eating it has had a reaction to it before, or is allergic [2][3]
>
> **Confusable with:** Jack-o'-lantern (*Omphalotus illudens*) → true gills, orange flesh, dense clusters on hardwood stumps and buried roots, which are the same oaks chicken-of-the-woods grows on → vomiting, cramps and diarrhea lasting hours to days [4][5][7]. Conifer-grown *Laetiporus* → looks "almost identical" and is told apart mainly by habitat and host [3] → reported to cause poisoning more often [3].
>
> **Verdict:** Positive ID on every item, confirmed by a mushroom expert, or do not eat it. **This species must never be eaten on field identification alone.** Even a correct ID does not guarantee you will tolerate it.

## ☠ Deadly Look-Alikes

Missouri says jack-o'-lantern poisoning is not life-threatening to healthy adults but leaves people very sick for a few days [5]. NAMA says most victims recovered within 12 hours [7]. This reference lists it at the top tier because the error is common and costly, and because the same facets apply across the entries that share it.

### Jack-o'-lantern (*Omphalotus illudens*)

| Check | Chicken-of-the-woods | Jack-o'-lantern |
|---|---|---|
| Underside | Pores. Yellow (rarely white), 2 to 4 per mm, no bruising [1] | True gills, close or crowded, running down the stem [4]; flesh pale orange inside [4] |
| Growth | Overlapping shelves on trunks and logs, or rosettes (*L. cincinnatus*) [1] | Large dense clusters on stumps or buried roots of hardwoods, especially oaks [4][5] |
| Color | Bright yellow to bright orange cap [1] | Bright orange to pumpkin orange [4] |

Turn it over. Gills mean stop. Cornell notes jack-o'-lantern gills are sharp and thin-edged [6], and Kuo says it has true gills [4]. Do not use the glow as a test; it fades after picking [6].

### Conifer-grown *Laetiporus* (*L. huroniensis*)

Kuo lists *L. huroniensis* on conifer wood in eastern North America [1]. Cornell's editor's aside says it is morphologically almost identical to *L. sulphureus* and is told apart mainly by habitat and host, and that this conifer species "seems to cause poisoning more often" [3]. A Cornell mycologist's comment adds that she knows of no published toxicity reports and that people are more likely to have a bad experience with the conifer chickens [3]. The cause is not established, so treat the host tree as a hard rule, not a guarantee.

## Identification

**Form:** *L. sulphureus* usually forms overlapping shelving clusters, caps 5 to 25 cm across, whole fruiting body up to 90 cm. Sometimes rosettes on top of a fallen log [1]. *L. cincinnatus* grows in rosettes at the base of the tree or on the ground near buried roots [1][2].

**Color:** Bright yellow to bright orange when fresh, fading to dull yellowish and nearly white [1]. *L. cincinnatus* is pinkish orange with whitish pores [1]. UF says pore layers run white to lemon yellow and *L. cincinnatus* has cream to white pores [2].

**Pores:** *L. sulphureus*: bright to dull yellow, rarely white, 2 to 4 per mm, tubes up to 5 mm deep. The pore surface does not bruise [1]. Do not use bruising as a confirming trait.

**Spore print:** White for *L. sulphureus* [1]. A shelf on a trunk is hard to print, and print color does not separate it from the look-alikes anyway.

**Age:** Older specimens toughen and turn sour [3].

## Habitat, Substrate & Fruiting (this biome)

- ***L. sulphureus*:** parasitic and saprobic on living and dead oaks and sometimes other hardwoods; causes a brown cubical heart rot. Summer and fall are typical, rarely winter or spring [1]. UF names oak and cherry as the most common hosts and says some conifers are also hosts [2].
- ***L. cincinnatus*:** at the butt of the tree or on the ground near its base [1].
- ***L. huroniensis*:** conifer wood, eastern North America [1]. Do not eat.
- **Hosts the sources do not clear:** black locust is not named as a host or warning in any source opened here, so it is not on the positive list. Yew, other conifers and eucalyptus: see the gate. The 2026 review says only fruiting bodies from deciduous trees should be used, because those from conifers "might have been poisoned with toxins from these host trees" [13].
- **Range:** Kuo and UF do not give a Mid-Atlantic range or peak weeks, so none is stated.

## ⚠ MANDATORY PREPARATION

1. **Only after the full gate and an expert's confirmation.** Know the host tree.
2. **Choose young, fresh specimens.** Old ones are tough and sour [3].
3. **Cook thoroughly. Never raw or undercooked.** UF says raw or undercooked specimens "have been reported to cause gastrointestinal problems" [2]. Cornell's post says to cook it fully [3]. NAMA says many edible species need appropriate preparation [8].
4. **Eat a small amount the first time** (general practice; UF says "consumed with caution" [2]). A small first portion cannot clear a bad ID; poisonings can start 1 to 3 hours later or 6 to 24 hours later [7].
5. **Know that a reaction can happen with the right species.** UF says at least some individuals are allergic [2]. Cornell's post lists vomiting, chills and mild hallucinations [3]. The 2026 review lists gastrointestinal problems, allergic reactions and vomiting among reported adverse effects [13]. A past good meal does not guarantee the next one.
6. **Keep one species per container.** Never mix collections.
7. **Stop eating and get help** if a reaction starts (below).

## Toxicology of the Confusables

### Jack-o'-lantern (*Omphalotus illudens*)

- **Toxins:** illudin M and illudin S. The toxin was long thought to be muscarine; NAMA says the symptoms do not match classic muscarine poisoning [7].
- **Onset:** typically 1 to 3 hours [7].
- **Symptoms:** nausea (90% of cases), vomiting (73%), abdominal pain, headache, exhaustion, weakness, dizziness; sweating, salivation and diarrhea less often [7].
- **Duration:** most recovered within 12 hours per NAMA [7]; "a few days" per Missouri [5]; most victims end up in the emergency room per Cornell [6].
- **Cooking:** no source opened says cooking removes it.

### Conifer-grown *Laetiporus* and any *Laetiporus* reaction

Reported effects in the sources opened: stomach upset, vomiting, chills, mild hallucinations, allergy [2][3][13]. The Cornell editor's aside says it has not heard of deaths [3]. No source opened gives a dose, a cooking fix, or a cause. Do not assume boiling, a different cooking method or an alcohol-free meal removes the risk.

### If someone has a reaction or may have eaten a look-alike

This is commonly taught first aid for when help is slow to arrive, not medical advice.

1. **Call Poison Help, 1-800-222-1222, now.** Do not wait for symptoms [9][10].
2. **Call 911** if the person collapses, has a seizure, has trouble breathing, or cannot be woken [9].
3. **Do not induce vomiting** [9].
4. **Save a sample.** Photograph it beside a coin for scale and keep pieces in a paper bag, not plastic [10].
5. Replacing lost fluids is important with vomiting and diarrhea [7]. Get medical care.

## Legal note

Picking is regulated on public land and differs by manager. Shenandoah National Park allows small amounts of edible mushrooms for personal use but says collecting or disturbing "woody shelf mushrooms" is prohibited, so ask the park before taking a shelf fungus [11]. Pennsylvania state forests prohibit removing plants "including fungus, lichen and moss" without written DCNR permission [12]. Check the land manager's rule. No survival exemption was found.
