## ⚠ POSITIVE-ID SAFETY GATE

Read the [mushroom doctrine and look-alike matrix](../_index.md) first. This entry is a draft and has not had its expert safety read. Expert confirmation is required before eating; this entry's checklist is not a substitute.

> ⚠ **Black trumpet: a dark, gill-less vase with a smooth to shallowly wrinkled outer surface; no deadly look-alike is named in the sources opened, but one look-alike is reported to cause stomach illness and another has no edibility information**
>
> **Positive-ID checklist (ALL must hold):**
> - **No gills. The spore-bearing outer surface is smooth to shallowly wrinkled or slightly veined.** Missouri: "no gills," underside "smooth to slightly veined" [1]. Kuo: under/outer surface "smooth or very shallowly wrinkled" [2].
> - **Dark vase or trumpet shape, hollow.** Dark brown to black, funnel-shaped and hollow, with a wavy margin [1]. Kuo's *C. fallax* is pale to dark gray above with a margin often black, and minutely scaly [2].
> - **Underside color:** brown to gray when young, turning salmon-colored as it matures [1]. Kuo: dark gray to black, becoming dull gray with orangish or yellowish tints as spores mature [2].
> - **Spore print yellowish to orange, not white.** Missouri: "ocher-buff to ocher-orange" [1]. Kuo: pale pinkish orange, "a yellowish to orangish print" [2]. Conserving Carolina: yellowish to orange [6]. The colour words vary; none of the three says white.
> - **On the ground under hardwoods,** often in moss, oak and beech named [2][6]; scattered, in groups or loose clusters [1][2].
> - **Summer to early fall** (see habitat for dates).
> - **A person who knows the species has seen it in hand.**
>
> **Do NOT use if ANY of these are present (disqualifying traits):**
> - Real gills, or white gills with a ring or a cup anywhere in your collection (doctrine points 2 and 3).
> - A distinctly wrinkled or veined outer surface with gill-like folds and cross-veins across the upper third, or several fused together in a clump of 2 to 5. Kuo gives these as the signs of *Craterellus foetidus*, a different species [3].
> - A white spore print. Kuo gives a white print for the European *C. cornucopioides* and for *C. foetidus* [3][4]. A white print does not match what the North American sources describe for this species.
> - A bright orange, scaly vase growing with conifers (scaly vase, *Turbinellus floccosus*, reported to cause nausea, vomiting and diarrhea in some people; see below) [8][12][13].
> - Solid, not hollow through the middle; slimy, rotten or insect-damaged flesh.
>
> **Confusable with:** *Craterellus foetidus* (wrinkled folds, white print, thicker, often fused clusters) [3]: the sources opened do not say what eating it does to you, so treat it as unknown. Scaly vase (*Turbinellus floccosus*): orange, scaly, with conifers [8]: reported to cause stomach illness in some people [12][13].
>
> **Verdict:** Positive ID on every checklist item, or do not consume. No opened source names a deadly look-alike for this region, so this entry sets `field_id_not_appropriate: false`, but `expert_id_required: true` stays on. A photo or a description alone is never enough.

## ☠ Deadly Look-Alikes

**No deadly look-alike is named by any source opened for this entry. One named look-alike, the scaly vase (*Turbinellus floccosus*), is reported to cause stomach illness in some people, and the edibility of another (*C. foetidus*) is not given in any source opened.** Missouri's page says "There are no lookalikes in Missouri" [1]. That statement is about Missouri, not Virginia or Pennsylvania, and it comes from one source. Missing information is not a green light. The confusables below are the ones the sources name.

### *Craterellus foetidus*

- **How it differs:** Kuo says it has a veined or prominently wrinkled under/outer surface with gill-like folds and cross-veins across the upper third, and that *C. fallax* does not [3]. It is somewhat thicker, and usually grows in fused clusters of 2 to 5 mushrooms while *C. fallax* only rarely clusters [3]. Its outer surface is gray to lilac gray, sometimes nearly white [3].
- **Spore print:** white [3]. The page for *C. fallax* gives a yellowish to orangish print [2]. Both are Kuo, so this contrast has one author behind it.
- **Pale specimens:** Kuo says young or dry-grown *C. foetidus* can be pale, nearly whitish, and that this can lead to confusion [3].
- **Where it grows:** mycorrhizal with oaks, east of the Great Plains, late spring through early fall [3]. It overlaps the black trumpet in habitat and season.
- **What it does to you:** not stated in any source opened [3].
- **If in doubt, do not eat.**

### The European name, *C. cornucopioides*

Missouri's page lists the black trumpet as *C. cornucopioides* with *C. fallax* in parentheses and gives one spore print color for both [1]. Kuo and the 2010 study split them: *C. fallax* is the eastern North American species and the European *C. cornucopioides* has a white to creamy print [2][4][5]. Kuo reports that research suggests *C. cornucopioides* does not occur in North America, and that a West Coast species is separate [4]. If your guide uses the European name for an eastern mushroom, read it as *C. fallax* and check the spore print yourself.

### Scaly vase (*Turbinellus floccosus*, formerly in *Gomphus*)

- **How it differs:** Kuo describes a vase-shaped fungus with a dark orange to reddish-orange scaly top, shallow forked wrinkles and folds running deeply down the stem, growing with conifers [8]. UBC adds orange scales in concentric rings on a deeply dented cap, a cream stem with yellow-orange tinges, and an ochre spore print [12]. It is not dark gray or black.
- **What it does:** UBC's mushroom guide labels Pacific Northwest and British Columbia specimens "suspect" because they cause nausea, vomiting and diarrhea in some people, with onset that "can be as late as 8-14 hours." It notes that related populations in central Mexico and India are eaten [12]. MykoWeb rates it "Questionable," says some people report stomach upset, and notes that it is eaten in Mexico [13]. Those reports come from western populations. No source opened gives the effect of eastern specimens. Kuo's page carries no edibility information [8].
- **Why it matters:** the confusion risk is low (orange, scaly, with conifers) and the illness reported is not deadly, but the late onset can hide the cause. Keep one specimen aside.
- **If in doubt, do not eat.**

### Chanterelles

Missouri gives "black chanterelle" as a common name [1], and Kuo files both under chanterelles and trumpets [2][8]. No source opened compares black trumpets with chanterelles feature by feature. Do not use either name to carry identification across. Chanterelles have their own entry, their own gate and a deadly look-alike: see [the chanterelle entry](chanterelle-cantharellus.md).

## Identification

- **Size:** Missouri gives 1/8 to 3 1/2 inches wide and 1 to 5 1/2 inches tall [1]. Conserving Carolina says about 1 to 2.5 inches tall [6].
- **Top (inner) surface:** dry, often rough and scaly but sometimes smooth [1]. Kuo: minutely scaly with blackish-tipped scales over a paler gray or gray-brown base [2].
- **Fertile surface (where the spores are made):** the smooth to shallowly wrinkled outer surface, not gills and not teeth [1][2].
- **Flesh:** gray to blackish [2].
- **Spore print:** pale pinkish orange to yellowish-orange or ocher-buff [1][2][6]. Set a piece of the outer surface down on white paper under a bowl for several hours (general practice). The print is faint, so look in good light.
- **Smell:** Conserving Carolina says fruity or earthy [6]; Missouri calls it highly fragrant [1]. Smell is not a deciding trait.
- **Red flags to check every time:** any gills; a clearly wrinkled folded outer surface; a white print; a bright orange body; a solid center.

## Habitat, Substrate & Fruiting (this biome)

- **What it grows on:** Kuo says it is mycorrhizal with oaks and perhaps other hardwoods, growing alone, scattered, in groups or loose clusters, often in mossy areas, east of the Rocky Mountains [2]. The 2010 study found it on the root tips of Virginia pine and, from sequence comparisons, linked it to roots of hemlock and oak and possibly chestnut, which it calls a broad host range [5]. Conserving Carolina says it grows in moist to semi-dry forests under oaks and beech, often in moss [6]. Missouri says rocky, mossy hillsides in deciduous woods and suggests it is probably a saprobe [1]; the research sources say mycorrhizal, which the 2010 peer-reviewed study supports [5].
- **It comes back:** Missouri says it often reappears in the same spot for years [1].
- **When:** Missouri gives June through September [1]. Kuo says spring through fall [2]. A Virginia DWR post describes black trumpets gathered in June on a club outing in the George Washington and Jefferson National Forest [7]. Conserving Carolina reports a peak from mid-August to early October in Polk County, North Carolina [6]. Treat "June to early October" as the pattern the sources support and not as a calendar for your woods; no Maryland, West Virginia or Pennsylvania agency source was opened.
- **Hard to see:** the dark color blends into leaf litter [1][6].

### Picking rules: check the land manager first

Foraging rules differ by land manager and change. Check the current rule before you pick. Nothing here is legal advice, and no source opened gives a survival exemption.

- **Shenandoah National Park:** small amounts of edible mushrooms may be collected for personal use; collecting or disturbing medicinal or woody shelf mushrooms or other inedible mushrooms is prohibited. The park sends readers to the superintendent's compendium [9].
- **Pennsylvania state forests:** DCNR's rules prohibit removing a living or dead plant, including fungus, without written permission, and separately permit gathering edible wild plants or plant parts for personal or family consumption. The page does not say whether the second rule covers mushrooms, so ask DCNR [10].
- **George Washington and Jefferson National Forests and Virginia wildlife management areas:** Virginia DWR says personal gathering is allowed in the national forest and on WMAs, and that commercial gathering needs a permit or is prohibited [7].

## ⚠ MANDATORY PREPARATION

**Do not eat it raw. Cook it.** Virginia DWR says to always cook wild mushrooms before eating [7]. Missouri lists the black trumpet as a choice edible, highly fragrant, whose flavor intensifies when dried, and says to eat only a small amount the first time [1]. No opened source gives a time or a temperature, so none is given here.

1. **Confirmed ID first.** Every checklist item holds, and a person who knows the species has seen it in hand.
2. **Clean carefully.** Shake out soil and leaf bits from the vase; the hollow can hold grit (general practice).
3. **Cook thoroughly** [7].
4. **First-portion rule.** Eat a small amount the first time and wait for any reaction [1][7]. Waiting several hours is common practice (general practice). Conserving Carolina warns that some people react badly even to edible mushrooms [6].
5. **Keep one piece for identification later** [7]. Never try two new species at once and never mix collections in one bag (doctrine points 4 and 7). Virginia DWR also says some people get stomach trouble even from edible varieties, so start small [7].
6. **No source opened ties the black trumpet to a problem with alcohol.** The doctrine's no-alcohol point is for inky caps. Skipping alcohol on a first trial is general practice.

## Toxicology of the Confusables

The sources opened name no toxin and no symptom list for *C. foetidus* or any other black trumpet look-alike. For the scaly vase, two sources report nausea, vomiting and diarrhea, with onset as late as 8 to 14 hours [12][13]. This entry will not invent more. What the sources do say:

- Some people react to edible mushrooms [1][6]. Start small.
- Virginia DWR says numerous poisonous look-alikes of edible mushrooms exist, and that *Amanita* species are especially harmful and common in Virginia [7]. NAMA describes the amatoxin syndrome as a 6 to 24 hour wait with no symptoms while the liver and kidneys are damaged, a possible apparent recovery around day two or three, then kidney and liver failure that often leads to death [11]. A black trumpet does not look like an *Amanita*, but a dark mushroom in leaf litter is easy to bag with other finds. That is why doctrine says no mixed collections.

**If someone ate a wild mushroom and may be sick:** call Poison Control at 1-800-222-1222 at the first suspicion, call 911 for collapse, seizure or trouble breathing, do not make the person vomit, and save a sample. Details are in [the food guide](../../../../05_food.md#if-someone-ate-something). NAMA says not to wait for symptoms after a suspected amatoxin ingestion [11].
