## Zone Fit & Why It Earns Space

Potatoes are the starch crop of the cool seasons. Maryland puts the yield at 6 to 15 pounds per 10-foot row [1]. Potatoes can be grown as an early crop for new potatoes and a late crop for storage [1]. Stored at the right temperature they keep for 6 to 8 months [1].

The same plant that gives you food also makes a toxin. Light turns tuber skin green and raises levels of a glycoalkaloid (solanine) [3][4]. That is handled in the preparation section below, and it matters for how you store potatoes.

## Planting Windows (Zone 7a)

Potatoes go in **before** the last frost, not after. Maryland calls them a half-hardy annual that can withstand light frosts [1]. Virginia gives the 50 percent-chance last spring frost for zone 7a as April 15 to 25 [6]. Penn State says to plant when the soil warms to 45 degrees F [2]. Maryland says hard frosts slow growth, and seed pieces languish or rot if planted in prolonged cold weather [1].

| Source | Spring planting |
|---|---|
| University of Maryland Extension, home garden guide [1] | March 15 to May 1 (early-maturing varieties can go in through June 1) |
| University of Maryland, central Maryland [5] | March 20 to May 10 |
| Virginia Cooperative Extension, zone 7a [6] | March 10 to May 20 |

**Fall crop.** Maryland lists June 15 to July 10 [5]. Virginia does not recommend a fall planting for zone 7a [6]. Storage potatoes need 90 to 120 days [1], and the first fall frost in zone 7a averages October 15 to 25 [6]. A planting after about July 10 will not reach full size before frost, by simple counting (general practice). Treat fall potatoes as a risk.

## Growing

- **Seed.** Buy certified disease-free seed potatoes. Maryland says supermarket potatoes and last season's garden tubers can carry disease without symptoms [1]. Seed should be firm and unsprouted [1]. Penn State says not to replant garden potatoes, and not to plant where potatoes or other nightshades grew in the last 2 to 3 years [2].
- **Cutting.** Maryland: seed pieces of 1.5 to 2 ounces with 1 to 3 buds (eyes), or small whole potatoes. Let cut pieces dry for a few days before planting [1].
- **Depth and spacing.** Maryland: 3 to 5 inches deep (5 to 6 inches for later crops), 8 to 12 inches apart in rows 24 to 36 inches apart [1]. Penn State: a trench 4 inches deep, seed 8 to 12 inches apart [2].
- **Soil.** Well-drained and high in organic matter, pH 6.0 to 6.5. Where common scab is a lasting problem, Maryland recommends pH 4.8 to 5.8 [1].
- **Hilling.** Penn State says to hill soil when stems are 8 to 12 inches tall [2]. Maryland says to hill when the tops are fully grown, to keep tubers from the sun, which can cause greening or scalding, and that hill tops should not be more than 6 inches above grade [1]. Light is the cause of greening, so keep tubers covered [1][3].
- **Water.** Keep plants watered, especially while tubers form. Avoid overhead watering, which spreads fungal disease. Too much or too little water causes cracks, blackheart, and misshapen tubers [1]. A thick straw mulch after hilling keeps soil cool and deters Colorado potato beetles [1].
- **Problems Maryland lists:** Colorado potato beetle, common scab, potato leafhopper, and potato tuberworm [1].

## Harvest & Storage

**New potatoes.** Dig 6 to 8 weeks after planting, usually once plants have flowered [1]. Penn State says about 8 weeks [2].

**Storage crop.** Maryland says leave tubers in the ground for 2 weeks after the plants die back so the skin thickens. Do not dig in wet soil. Do not wash tubers before storage. Bruises and cuts let in rot, so dig gently with a fork [1]. Penn State says to harvest mature potatoes after the plants have died and before a hard frost [2].

**Curing.** Maryland: 10 to 14 days at 50 to 60 degrees F and high humidity [1]. Penn State: 2 to 3 weeks in a well-ventilated place at 50 to 60 degrees F with 85 to 90 percent humidity [2].

**Storing.**

| Source | Conditions |
|---|---|
| Penn State, Potatoes in the Garden and the Kitchen [2] | dark, 40 to 46 degrees F, about 90 percent humidity |
| University of Maryland Extension [1] | dark, 40 to 50 degrees F, 90 percent humidity, 6 to 8 months |
| Oregon State [4] | dark, 41 to 46 degrees F, humidity over 90 percent |

- **Do not store below 38 degrees F.** Penn State says temperatures below 38 degrees F can cause sugar to build up [2].
- **Not too warm either.** Maryland says sprouting and rots are a problem at higher temperatures [1]. Oregon State says above 46 degrees F (8 degrees C) potatoes may sprout after two or three months, which can raise glycoalkaloid levels [4].
- **Keep them in the dark.** Light causes greening [1][2][3]. Maryland suggests ventilated bags or boxes in a cool basement [1].
- **Check often.** Remove soft or rotting tubers (general practice).

## Seed Saving

Potatoes are grown from tubers, which are clones, not from seed. The plant sometimes makes small potato-like fruits. Maryland says their seed is extremely small and, if planted, will not come true to the variety [1]. Maryland also says saving your own seed potatoes is generally not worthwhile, because viruses and diseases often show up the next year [1]. Penn State says to use certified seed rather than replanting garden potatoes [2].

If you cannot get certified seed, small whole tubers from your own garden crop are what people replant. The extension warning is about disease build-up over seasons, not that they will not grow (general practice). Rotate to a bed where nightshades have not grown for 2 to 3 years [2].

## ⚠ MANDATORY PREPARATION

**Green, bitter, or heavily sprouted potatoes contain more glycoalkaloid (solanine), which cooking does not remove.**

- **What causes it.** Light on tubers, before or after harvest, makes them turn green and form solanine, a glycoalkaloid toxin. It tastes bitter and can irritate the gut, and enough of it can cause vomiting and diarrhea. Because of the bitterness, Penn State says it is rare for someone to eat enough to get sick [3].
- **How much green is too much (Penn State).** "Small green spots can be trimmed off." If more extensive greening occurs, throw the potato out [3]. Maryland says green portions should be cut out and discarded [1].
- **Peeling helps.** Oregon State says peeling slightly green potatoes lowers the glycoalkaloid content, and that potatoes with pronounced greening or damage must not be eaten [4].
- **Cooking does not fix it.** Oregon State says boiling and microwaving have a negligible effect on glycoalkaloid content [4]. Boiling peeled potatoes cut it by about 39 percent, which still leaves a green tuber unfit to eat [4]. Frying at 210 degrees C (410 degrees F) cut it by about 40 percent in one study, while frying at 150 to 180 degrees C changed it little [4]. Do not count on cooking.
- **Bitter or burning.** Oregon State says levels above about 140 mg per kg make potatoes bitter, and levels above 200 mg per kg can cause a burning sensation in the mouth and throat [4]. **If a potato tastes bitter or burns your throat, stop eating it and throw it out.** Oregon State gives about 12 to 20 mg per kg for a normal tuber, 250 to 280 for a green tuber, and 1,500 to 2,200 in the skin of a green tuber [4].
- **Sprouts and eyes.** A university poisonous-plant database says sprouted eyes can hold high levels of solanine [7]. Cut sprouts and eyes out deeply from a firm tuber. Throw out soft, shriveled, or heavily sprouted or green tubers (general practice).
- **Plants and fruits.** The same database says all green parts of the potato plant are poisonous, particularly the berries [7]. Do not eat potato leaves, stems, or the small green fruits. This rests on one source and is stated in animal-poisoning terms.

**Symptoms of too much.** Oregon State lists abdominal pain, diarrhea, sweating, and vomiting at lower doses, and neurological problems, rapid pulse, low blood pressure, coma or death at severe doses [4]. If someone has stomach pain, vomiting, confusion, or a racing pulse after eating green or bitter potatoes, call America's Poison Centers at 1-800-222-1222, and call 911 for severe symptoms (general practice).

**Always cook potatoes** before eating them. This entry gives no raw-potato advice and no cooking time, because the sources opened give none (general practice: cook until tender).
