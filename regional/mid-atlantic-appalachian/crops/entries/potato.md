## Zone Fit & Why It Earns Space

Potatoes are the **starch backbone** of survival gardening in 7a: a single 100 sq ft bed yields 50+ lbs of storable carbs. They thrive in cool seasons (spring + fall) and deliver calories and vitamin C with minimal pest pressure compared to warm-season crops. In Baltimore's climate, both spring and fall plantings are viable — spring harvest before summer heat, fall harvest before October frost. Potatoes store for 4-6 months in cool/humid conditions.

## Planting Windows (Zone 7a)

**Spring crop:** Plant 2-3 weeks after last frost (early-to-mid April). Final spring frost in the Baltimore region ≈ mid-April. Potatoes planted then will mature 70-90 days later, ready for harvest by late June–mid-July (before summer stress). 

**Fall crop:** Plant late July–early August (high heat is gone; soil still warm enough for sprouting). Harvest by late September–early October, before the first hard frost (late October ≈ 28°F). Fall potatoes often have better flavor and storage due to cooler finish.

## Growing

- **Spacing & depth:** Plant seed pieces 4 inches deep, 12 inches apart in rows 30 inches apart. Pieces should be 1.5-2 oz, each with 1-2 eyes.
- **Soil:** Loose, well-draining, acidic (pH 5.0-6.8). Avoid heavy clay; work in compost or aged manure. Potatoes prefer consistent moisture but not waterlogged soil.
- **Water:** 1-1.5 inches per week during growing season. Reduce once foliage yellows (late growth).
- **Hilling:** Mound soil over emerging stems when plants are 6-8 inches tall. Repeat when new stem growth appears. This prevents green exposure (solanine — see Cautions) and loosens soil for tuber formation.
- **Pests (7a region):** Colorado potato beetle (hand-pick early; row covers for seedlings); flea beetles (can defoliate young plants); late blight fungus (worst in cool/wet springs). Scout weekly. Remove beetle egg clusters (orange clusters on leaf undersides). Fungicide spray may be needed in wet springs.
- **Diseases:** Late blight (Phytophthora infestans) is the biggest threat in this region during cool, wet springs. Plant certified disease-free seed. Improve air circulation (wider spacing); remove lower leaves as plants mature.

## Harvest & Storage

**Timing:** Foliage yellows and dies back (8-10 weeks after planting). Dig carefully with a fork to avoid bruising. Spring crop ready by late June–mid-July; fall crop by late September–early October.

**Curing (for storage):** Cure harvested potatoes in a cool (50-60°F), dark, well-ventilated space for 1-2 weeks. This hardens the skin for storage and heals minor cuts. Store at 35-40°F, high humidity (80-90%), in darkness (to prevent greening). Ideal storage: root cellar or unheated basement. Check monthly; remove any sprouting or soft tubers to prevent rot spread.

**Shelf life:** 4-6 months in ideal storage; 2-3 months in warmer conditions.

## Seed Saving

Potatoes reproduce from tubers (clones), not seed. **Do NOT save from commercial potatoes** — they may carry latent disease.

**For self-sufficiency:**
1. Choose open-pollinated heirloom varieties (e.g., Russet, Red Norland, fingerling types).
2. Harvest carefully; set aside small, unblemished tubers (walnut-size or smaller) for seed.
3. Cure and store seed potatoes separately at 35-40°F in high humidity, in the dark.
4. Inspect monthly; discard any soft or sprouting tubers showing disease.
5. Plant saved seed potatoes in spring (or fall) per the planting window above. Yield degrades after 2-3 years of replanting without disease monitoring; periodically buy certified seed to refresh the line.

## ⚠ MANDATORY PREPARATION

**Green tubers and sprouts contain solanine (a toxic alkaloid). Do NOT eat them.**

Solanine accumulates in tuber skin and flesh when potatoes are exposed to sunlight or stored in warm conditions. It tastes bitter, causes mouth tingling, and in large amounts can cause gastrointestinal upset and worse.

**Preparation rules:**
- Cut away all green portions, including the stem end and any sprouts, before cooking.
- Discard any tuber with extensive green skin or sprouting. Cook tubers thoroughly (boiled, baked, fried until soft). Solanine is heat-stable but mostly in the skin and upper layer; peeling reduces exposure.
- Store in darkness to prevent greening. If tubers begin to green or sprout, they are no longer safe.
