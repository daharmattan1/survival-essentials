## Zone Fit & Why It Earns Space

Kale and collards stretch the garden season at both ends. Maryland Extension says they tolerate cooler temperatures, and that kale and collards can take heavy frosts in spring and fall [1]. They do best as a fall crop except collards, because frost raises the sugar content and flavor of the leaves [1]. Maryland says all greens can be grown well into the fall in the mid-Atlantic using a protected spot, floating row cover, or cold frame, and that if winter is not too severe, kale will re-sprout from the stems in spring [1]. Maryland puts the yield at 5 to 10 pounds per 10-foot row [1]. Greens are relatively high in antioxidants, vitamins, and minerals [1].

Kale and collards are cultivar groups of one species, *Brassica oleracea*, the same species as cabbage, broccoli, cauliflower, and kohlrabi [6].

## Planting Windows (Zone 7a)

Virginia gives the 50 percent-chance last spring frost for zone 7a as April 15 to 25 and the first fall frost as October 15 to 25 [3]. Cool-season greens go in **before** the last frost in spring, not after it.

| Source | Crop | Spring | Fall |
|---|---|---|---|
| University of Maryland, central Maryland [2] | kale | April 1 to 20 | July 10 to August 10 |
| University of Maryland, central Maryland [2] | collards | April 1 to June 1 | July 10 to August 10 |
| Virginia Cooperative Extension, zone 7a [3] | collards and kale | March 1 to April 20 | August 1 to September 10 |

Maryland's general advice: in spring, plant seed as soon as soil can be worked, about 3 to 4 weeks before the frost date, or set out 3 to 5 week old transplants. To keep greens going all summer, plant in early spring and again in late summer [1]. For fall, Maryland says to take the days to maturity, add 10 to 14 days for the short fall days, and plant that many days before the first fall frost [1].

## Growing

- **Spacing.** Maryland: 12 to 24 inches apart in rows 30 to 36 inches apart for large plants. Closer spacing, 2 to 4 inches apart, gives small plants you can cut again and again [1].
- **Soil and feeding.** Work compost into the soil before planting. Medium nutrient need [1].
- **Light.** Greens tolerate partial shade, 4 to 6 hours of direct light [1].
- **Water and mulch.** Water regularly. Organic mulch keeps weeds down and holds moisture [1].
- **Cold.** Kale and turnip greens tolerate 15 to 20 degrees F. Collards are less cold-tolerant than kale [1].
- **Problems Maryland lists:** bolting, cabbage looper, harlequin bug, imported cabbageworm, and whiteflies [1]. If you use any pesticide, follow the label exactly (general practice).

## Harvest & Storage

**Harvest.** Pick outer leaves, young leaves, or whole plants. For kale and collards the outer leaves are usually picked. Yellowing or browning leaves are over-mature and go to the compost [1]. Alternatively, sow thickly, thin to 8 inches once plants are 6 to 10 inches tall, and eat the thinnings first [1].

**Fresh storage.** Maryland: very cold (32 degrees F), moist (95 percent relative humidity) conditions, 2 to 3 weeks [1].

**Freezing.** The National Center for Home Food Preservation says freezing greens is recommended only for use as a cooked vegetable, because their water-filled cells burst when frozen. It also says blanching is important to slow or stop the enzyme action that spoils them [4].
- Water-blanch **most greens 2 minutes**, and **collards 3 minutes** (1 extra minute) [4].
- The page does not name kale. Kale is a leafy green of the same sort, so 2 minutes is the figure for "most greens"; blanching it 3 minutes like collards is a reasonable choice (general practice).
- After blanching, cool the leaves quickly, drain, pack, and freeze (general practice). The NCHFP page does not give a freezer shelf life.

**Canning.** Spinach and other greens can be canned only in a pressure canner. The greens should be soft and spinach-like, not firm leaves like cabbage [4]. A boiling-water bath is not enough for low-acid vegetables, and a jar of unknown origin goes in the trash (see `05_food.md`).

## Seed Saving

Kale and collards are biennial: they flower and set seed in the second year [1]. They are insect-pollinated and cross with each other and with cabbage, broccoli, cauliflower, and kohlrabi, because these are all *Brassica oleracea* [5][6]. Illinois Extension says insect-pollinated crops should be about 200 yards apart for pure seed, and that if that is not possible you can stagger planting times or bag the flowers [5].

To keep a line going: let a few plants live through the winter (Maryland says kale re-sprouts from the stems if winter is not too severe [1]) and let them flower the next spring. When pods are dry, collect the seed, dry it further, and store it cool and dry in paper or in paper in a glass jar [5]. In most cases it is easier to buy seed than to isolate a Brassica (general practice). The sources opened give no germination life for Brassica seed, so test a few seeds before you plant.
