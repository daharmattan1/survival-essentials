# Garlic (Allium sativum)

> ⚠ **Never keep fresh or unacidified garlic in oil at room temperature.** The only tested exception is the citric-acid method in Rule 2 below, followed exactly. Garlic in oil can grow the bacterium that makes botulism toxin. Read `## ⚠ MANDATORY PREPARATION` below before you put garlic in oil, jar it, or give it away. The garlic bulb itself is not the danger. The oil is.

## Zone Fit & Why It Earns Space

Garlic goes in the ground in fall and comes out in early summer, and the bulb keeps for months. The University of Maryland says bulbs usually keep 6 to 9 months in a cool, dry basement [1]. That is the best case, and hardneck keeps shorter (see Store below). Hardneck garlic also gives you scapes, the curled flower stalks, which Penn State lists as edible [3].

**Hardneck or softneck.** The two types matter for this region.

- **Hardneck** (also called stiffneck) makes a hard stem up the middle of the bulb and a scape in June. Penn State says hardnecks grow best in Pennsylvania because they are more cold-hardy [3]. NC State says the "rocambole" group peels easily and is the most pungent but does not store as long as the "continental" group [7].
- **Softneck** is the type usually sold in grocery stores. It has smaller cloves and a long storage life [1]. NC State says softnecks lack the hardnecks' cold requirement and do better in warmer climates [6].

## Planting Windows (Zone 7a)

Plant cloves in fall. Virginia gives 50 percent-chance frost dates for zone 7a of April 15 to 25 and October 15 to 25 [5].

Fall windows: Virginia zones 7a and 7b Oct 1 to Oct 30, zones 6a and 6b Sept 1 to Oct 1 [5]; Maryland and Penn State mid to late October [1][3]; NC State late October to November, at least 4 to 6 weeks before the ground freezes [6][7].

Fall planting gives the cloves the cold they need. The University of Maryland says cloves must be exposed to 32 to 50 degrees F for two months before bulbs start to swell, and spring-planted garlic makes smaller bulbs [1]. Virginia Cooperative Extension says you may plant in March or April, but recommends fall planting for full development [4].

## Growing

- **Planting stock.** Buy certified, disease-free bulbs. The University of Maryland says never plant grocery-store garlic because it may carry disease without showing it [1]. Split the bulb just before planting, since early splitting lowers yield [4]. Larger cloves make larger bulbs [1][4].
- **Planting.** Point the tip up [1][3]. Depth: 1 to 3 inches depending on source (Virginia 1, Maryland and Penn State 1 to 2, NC State 2 to 3) [1][3][4][6]. Spacing in the row: 3 to 8 inches depending on source (Virginia 3 to 5, Maryland 3 to 6, Penn State 6 to 7, NC State 6 to 8) [1][3][4][6]. Use a middle value and follow your local office.
- **Soil and sun.** Deep, well-drained soil with plenty of organic matter. Best pH is 6.0 to 6.5 (Penn State [2]; Virginia says 6.2 to 6.5 [4]). Full sun, at least 6 hours a day [1].
- **Water.** Penn State says about 1 inch of water a week in dry periods through mid-June, then stop so the bulbs mature and rot is less likely [2].
- **Scapes.** On hardnecks, snap off the young scape to increase bulb size [1][2][3].
- **Problems in this region.** Allium leafminer and onion thrips are the main insect pests in the eastern United States [2].

## Harvest & Storage

**When to dig.** In central Maryland the tops start to die back in late June to early July, and harvest is around July 1 [1]. Penn State says early to mid July [3]. Lift the plants when about half the foliage is brown (Maryland [1]) or 40 to 60 percent of leaves are yellowed with at least 4 still partly green (Penn State [2][3]). Too early gives small bulbs and poorer storage. Too late and the bulbs split [2][3].

**How to dig.** Do not pull the stem. Lift the bulb with a fork or spade [1][3]. Brush off soil, and do not wash the bulbs [4].

**Cure.** Dry the bulbs with the tops on, in a shaded, well-ventilated spot out of the sun and rain.

Maryland: 7 to 14 days with tops on, then trim tops and roots [1]. Penn State: a few weeks [3], or about three weeks until the outer two leaves are completely dry [2]. NC State: until the leaves are completely dry [6][7].

**Store.** Keep cured bulbs in mesh bags or open boxes. The sources split on temperature:
- Maryland says 32 to 38 degrees F and 65 percent relative humidity [1]. Penn State says 32 to 35 degrees F and 65 to 75 percent [2]. UC ANR describes near 32 degrees F as commercial storage [15].
- NC State's hardneck page says "do not refrigerate" [7]. UC ANR says a home refrigerator (about 40 degrees F) is not suitable, because that temperature stimulates sprouting [15]. Cornell says below 50 degrees F garlic is likely to sprout and above 66 degrees F it shrivels [16].
- **What to do.** Use 32 to 38 degrees F only in a cellar or cold room that holds it without freezing and stays dry. In an ordinary home, keep cured bulbs cool (about 60 degrees F), dry, dark, and ventilated, and do not put whole bulbs in the refrigerator. All sources agree on dry and ventilated.

**How long.** UC ANR: 3 to 5 months at about 60 degrees F for both types. Under commercial cold storage near 32 degrees F, softneck up to 9 months and hardneck up to 6 months. At 68 to 86 degrees F, softneck only 1 to 2 months [15]. Cornell: rocambole hardnecks about 3 to 4 months, silverskin softnecks often over 12 months [16]. NC State: softneck up to 12 months [6]. Plan to eat or plant hardneck by about midwinter. Sprouted or shriveled cloves are not harmful but are past peak; cut a sprouted clove in half and discard the green center [15].

**Drying.** Penn State describes drying peeled, sliced or chopped garlic in a dehydrator until crisp, about 6 to 8 hours, and storing it airtight [3].

**Canning.** Penn State says there is no tested method for canning garlic [3]. UC ANR says adequate processing times have not been determined [15].

## Seed Saving

Garlic is not grown from seed. It is grown from cloves, so each plant is a copy of the one before it.

1. At harvest, set aside the largest, most uniform, unblemished bulbs for replanting [3]. Discard planting stock with blemishes or signs of disease [2].
2. Cure and store them like the eating crop. Do not split the bulb until planting day [4].
3. Plant in the fall window above.
4. Hardneck scapes end in bulbils. Penn State notes these can grow small bulbs, but removing the scape gives bigger bulbs [2].

## ⚠ MANDATORY PREPARATION

**Garlic in oil can cause botulism. Never keep it at room temperature, except by the citric-acid method in Rule 2, followed exactly.**

Garlic is a low-acid food and can carry spores of *Clostridium botulinum*. Oil removes the air. At room temperature, the spores can grow in that airless, low-acid mix and make botulism toxin. Penn State says the result is serious illness or death [9]. NCHFP research confirmed that garlic in oil at room temperature is at risk, and lists it among foods linked to botulism outbreaks [8][13]. The toxin is among the most toxic substances known, and symptoms have started as early as 4 hours after eating and as late as 8 days [13].
**Rule 1: garlic in oil with no acid treatment, including roasted garlic.** UC ANR says roasted garlic in oil has the same hazard [15].
- Make it fresh. Refrigerate at 40 degrees F or lower for no more than 4 days [8]. The CDC says to refrigerate homemade oils made with garlic or herbs and throw them away after 4 days [11]. UC ANR also says 4 days [15]. Idaho says 2 to 4 days [14], and Penn State says three [12]. Four days is the outer limit; use it in two to three.
- To keep it longer, freeze it. NCHFP says it may be frozen for several months, packed in glass freezer jars or plastic freezer boxes with half an inch of headspace, labeled and dated [8].
- Heating the oil first does not remove this rule. UGA says oil heated to 180 degrees F before adding garlic is still refrigerated and thrown out after four days, or frozen. Dried garlic is not a guarantee: even a very small pocket with enough moisture can let bacteria grow and make toxin [14], and UGA says dried ingredients may not be dry enough [10].

**Rule 2: the only tested way to hold garlic-infused oil at room temperature.** Researchers at the University of Idaho developed an acid method for raw garlic, basil, oregano, and rosemary [14]. Penn State and UGA relay it [9][10]. Use it only for those items, and only with raw garlic, not roasted.
1. Make a 3 percent citric acid solution: stir 1 level tablespoon of granular citric acid into 2 cups of water [14]. Citric acid is sold where canning supplies are sold [9]. It is not ascorbic acid (vitamin C), which does not acidify the same way [14].
2. Use chopped, peeled raw garlic only, in pieces no larger than 1/4 inch. Whole cloves acidify much more slowly and do not reach the needed acidity in 24 hours [14]. Use 2/3 cup chopped garlic for 2 cups of solution, 1 part garlic to 3 parts solution. Changing the ratio may give a dangerous product [9][10].
3. Keep the garlic fully under the liquid with a weight. Soak at least 24 hours at room temperature; a few hours longer is fine, much longer spoils quality [9][10][14]. UGA reports this brings the garlic to a pH of 4.2 or lower within 24 hours [10].
4. Drain. Add 1 part acidified garlic to 10 parts oil. Infuse at room temperature for 1 to 10 days, or heat the oil to 140 degrees F for 5 minutes for a quicker infusion [9][10].
5. Remove the garlic when the flavor is right. Do not add fresh garlic or herbs afterward, because they will not be acidified [9].
6. Do not swap vinegar or lemon juice for citric acid. UGA says they have not been tested and may give a dangerous product [10]. A weaker solution or shorter soak can also do this [9].
7. Acidify garlic in its own soak, separate from herbs, because the ratios differ. After draining, you may combine garlic and herbs in the oil [14].
8. Store away from heat and light. Penn State says it keeps better refrigerated or frozen [9], and UGA recommends refrigerating or freezing for long-term storage [10].

**Store-bought infused oils.** Check the label for acidification or another inhibitor [9]. **Do not can plain garlic** [3].

**If someone gets sick.** Botulism is a medical emergency [17]. If anyone who ate garlic in oil (or any home-preserved low-acid food you doubt) has double or blurry vision, drooping eyelids, slurred speech, trouble swallowing, muscle weakness, trouble moving the eyes, or trouble breathing, **call 911 or go to an emergency room immediately. Do not wait** [17]. CDC says people might not have all these signs at once, and that vomiting, nausea, stomach pain, and diarrhea can also occur with foodborne botulism [17]. Call America's Poison Centers at 1-800-222-1222 for advice. Do not make the person vomit. Keep the jar and what remains of the food, and tell responders it contained garlic in oil. Symptoms have shown up anywhere from 4 hours to 8 days after eating [13], so report what was eaten even if the person feels fine.
