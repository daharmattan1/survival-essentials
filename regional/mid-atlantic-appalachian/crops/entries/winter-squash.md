## Zone Fit & Why It Earns Space

Winter squash is eaten as a mature fruit, once the seeds have fully matured and the skin has hardened into a tough rind. At that stage most varieties can be stored for use through the winter [1][2]. Maryland gives a yield of 10 to 20 pounds per 10-foot row [1]. Maryland says it is generally cooked before eating [1].

Storage length depends on the type. Illinois says large hard-rind winter squash can be stored up to six months, and the smaller acorn and butternut only up to 3 months [2]. Plan to eat acorn and delicata first.

**One hazard sits behind this easy crop: bitter squash.** A squash that tastes bitter can make you seriously ill, and cooking does not fix it. Read the preparation section before you eat any squash you grew, especially one grown near ornamental gourds or from saved seed.

## Planting Windows (Zone 7a)

Squash seed does not germinate in cold soil and seedlings are injured by frost. Do not plant until all danger of frost is past and the soil is thoroughly warm [2]. Virginia gives the 50 percent-chance last spring frost for zone 7a as April 15 to 25 and the first fall frost as October 15 to 25 [5].

| Source | Planting window |
|---|---|
| University of Maryland, central Maryland [4] | May 15 to June 15 |
| Virginia Cooperative Extension, zone 7a [5] | April 20 to July 10 |
| University of Maryland Extension, home garden guide [1] | a second crop between June 15 and July 1 |

Maryland says winter squash often needs more days than summer squash to ripen [1], so count days to maturity back from your first fall frost.

## Growing

- **Seed.** Maryland: sow 2 to 3 seeds at a depth of 4 times the seed's thickness, then pinch out all but the strongest seedling. Transplants started indoors 3 weeks ahead are an option. Black plastic or landscape fabric can hasten maturity [1].
- **Spacing.** Maryland: every 3 to 4 feet in rows 6 to 8 feet apart [1]. Illinois: vining types need at least 50 to 100 square feet per hill; bush types one plant every 3 feet with 5 feet between rows [2].
- **Sun and feeding.** At least 6 hours of direct light, 8 to 10 preferred. Medium to high nutrient need; work in compost and side-dress when fruit begins to form [1].
- **Water.** Water deeply and regularly at the base, especially in hot, dry weather and once fruit starts to form [1]. Drought and heat also raise the risk of bitter fruit (see below) [3].
- **Pollination.** Illinois says bees are necessary for pollinating squash and are killed by insecticides, so spray only in the late afternoon or evening and avoid the flowers [2].
- **Problems Maryland lists:** cucumber beetles, squash bugs, squash beetle, squash vine borer, powdery and downy mildew, viruses, and pollination problems [1]. Illinois says a vine hit by the borer can be kept alive by rooting the stem every foot or so along the vine [2].

## Harvest & Storage

**Harvest.** Cut the fruit from the vine with a knife or shears, leaving about 2 inches of stem (pumpkins 3 to 4 inches). Avoid cuts and bruises. The rind must be hard and firm for the squash to store [1]. Illinois says harvest when the fruit is a deep, solid color and the rind is hard [2]. Fruit that is not fully mature, is injured, has lost its stem, or has been through a heavy frost does not keep; use it first [2].

**Curing.** Illinois says most winter squash benefits from curing, except acorn, sweet dumpling, and delicata. Curing is holding the squash at about 70 degrees F for 10 to 20 days [2]. Do not cure acorn or delicata; eat them first.

**Storage.** Maryland: a dry area at 50 to 55 degrees F, in a single layer with fruit not touching, to reduce the spread of rot [1]. Illinois: after curing, a cool (45 to 50 degrees F), dry place, never freezing. Warmer temperatures mean shorter storage [2]. Illinois says refrigerators are too humid for whole squash. Keep cut pieces in the refrigerator [2].

| Type | Illinois storage figure [2] |
|---|---|
| Large hard-rind winter squash | up to 6 months |
| Acorn and butternut | up to 3 months |

Check stored fruit often and use any with soft spots first (general practice).

## Seed Saving

**Read this before you save a single seed.** Squash can carry cucurbitacin, a bitter and toxic compound. Poison Control says wild toxic gourds sometimes accidentally cross-pollinate with domestic gourds, and tells gardeners to buy new seed each year and not to plant edible and non-edible varieties of squash close to each other [3]. Decorative squashes such as colocynths are not edible [3]. So:

- **Do not save seed from squash grown near ornamental gourds or wild gourds.** Buy new seed instead [3].
- **Do not save seed from any fruit that tasted bitter.** Throw the fruit away [3].
- If you do save seed in an emergency, taste-test every fruit from the next crop before you eat it (general practice).

How crossing works, from Illinois Extension [6]:
- Different **species** do not cross, even though squash, cucumber, and watermelon share a family.
- Different **varieties of the same species** do cross, for example summer squash and pumpkin. They must be isolated if you are saving seed.
- Insect-pollinated plants should be about 200 yards apart for pure seed. If that is not possible, stagger planting times or contain the flowers with bags or hand-pollination [6].
- Hand-pollination: use a cotton swab or small paintbrush to pick up pollen from the male flower and brush it on the female stigma [6].
- Cross-pollination does not affect the current crop, but the seed does not come true the next year [2].

**Cleaning seed (wet harvest).** Scoop out seeds, put them in a jar with a little water, and ferment 2 to 4 days, stirring daily. Good seed sinks; pulp and bad seed float. Pour off the top, spread the seed on a paper towel or fine screen to dry, and store cool and dry in paper, labeled with name, variety, and date [6]. The sources opened give no germination life for squash seed.

## ⚠ MANDATORY PREPARATION

**Step 1 for every squash you grew: taste a very small piece of the raw flesh.** Poison Control says squash, pumpkin, zucchini, and cucumber with high cucurbitacin look and smell normal but taste very bitter, and that toxic squash syndrome comes from eating them [3].
- **If it is bitter, spit it out and throw the whole squash away.** Do not cook, boil, or bake bitter-tasting squash. **Cooking does not destroy the toxin; heat does not inactivate it** [3].
- Do not eat wild squash you did not plant in your garden [3]. Volunteer seedlings from a compost pile are squash you did not plant (general practice).
- Stress (high heat, drought, poor soil, insect damage) raises cucurbitacin levels [3].

**What bitter squash does.** Symptoms are mostly stomach-related: diarrhea, nausea, vomiting, and abdominal pain, and these can lead to dehydration, low blood pressure, fast heart rate, headache, and dizziness. Rarely, temporary hair loss follows about five days later [3]. Poison Control says treatment is supportive, usually fluids by mouth and sometimes by vein. Seek medical care if you cannot keep fluids down, if symptoms are severe, or if they last more than a day. **Call America's Poison Centers at 1-800-222-1222 (free, confidential, 24 hours)** [3]. Save a piece of the squash for the poison center or doctor (general practice).

**Step 2: cook it.** Winter squash is normally cooked before eating [1]. This entry gives no oven or boiling times, because the sources opened give none. Cut the squash carefully; the rind is hard and a knife can slip (general practice). Cook until the flesh is soft (general practice).

**Step 3: seeds.** Squash seeds you save for planting follow the rules in Seed Saving above.
