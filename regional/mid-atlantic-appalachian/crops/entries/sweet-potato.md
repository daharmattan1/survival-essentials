# Sweet Potato (Ipomoea batatas)

## Zone Fit & Why It Earns Space

Sweet potato is a warm-season root crop. The University of Maryland calls it a very tender warm-season annual: frost injures the top growth and the plant needs warm weather [1]. In return you get a heavy root crop. Maryland gives a yield of 15 to 30 pounds per 10-foot row [1]. Roots store through the winter [1][2]. The vines also cover the ground quickly and shade out most weeds [1][2]. 

The leaves are food too. Maryland says the foliage is edible and very nutritious, and can be eaten fresh in a salad or sauteed [1]. Penn State says the young leaves and shoots are edible [2]. NC State's plant database lists edible leaves, stems, and roots, and says the roots can be eaten raw or cooked [6].

**Sweet potato is not a yam.** Penn State and Maryland both say sweet potatoes are storage roots, not tubers [1][2].
- A true yam (*Dioscorea*) is an unrelated plant grown mainly in the tropics [1]. NC State Franklin County describes yams as native to Africa and Asia with a thick, rough, bark-like skin and white or yellow flesh, and says many products sold as "yams" are sweet potatoes [4]. Penn State says yams have a dark brown skin and white flesh that is not so sweet [2].
- Moist, soft-fleshed cultivars such as 'Georgia Jet', 'Centennial', and 'Vardaman' are sometimes called yams. They come from 'Porto Rico' [1].

## Planting Windows (Zone 7a)

The vine will not grow in cold soil. Maryland says to set out slips after the soil has warmed to 65 degrees F, and to plant in ridges so the soil warms faster [1]. Penn State says plants are very sensitive to cool soil, and to plant three to four weeks after the frost-free date [2]. Virginia Cooperative Extension gives the 50 percent-chance last spring frost for zone 7a as April 15 to 25 and the first fall frost as October 15 to 25 [7].

| Source | Planting window |
|---|---|
| Virginia Cooperative Extension, zone 7a | May 1 to July 10 [7] |
| Virginia Cooperative Extension, zone 7b | May 1 to July 20 [7] |
| Virginia Cooperative Extension, zone 6a / 6b | May 20 to July 1 / May 15 to July 1 [7] |
| NC State, Franklin County (North Carolina) | May 1 to July 15 [4] |
| Penn State, most of Pennsylvania | end of May or beginning of June, when soil is 65 degrees F [2] |

Start your own slips about 6 to 8 weeks before the planting date. Maryland says bedding to planting takes about six weeks [1]; Penn State says six to eight weeks [2]. Count back from the day you plan to plant.

**Do not plant to the end of the window.** The roots need 85 to 120 days [1], and the first fall frost in zone 7a averages October 15 to 25 [7]. A July 10 planting would finish between October 8 and November 7. Count back from your first fall frost: for a 110-day crop in zone 7a, plant by about late June (general practice). Penn State's rule of 3 to 4 weeks after the frost-free date points to mid-May [2][7].

## Growing

- **Slips.** You do not grow sweet potatoes from seed. You grow them from slips, which are sprouts from the previous year's roots [2]. Buy certified slips or grow your own (see Seed Saving).
- **Hardening and planting.** Pull slips from the bedded root and plant outdoors after frost danger has passed. Slips have no roots when pulled, so keep them well watered [1]. Penn State says slips do not need visible roots, and to cut off the bottom inch [2].
- **Spacing.** Maryland: 12 inches between plants and 40 inches between rows [1]. Penn State: 1 foot apart on ridges 8 to 12 inches high, rows 3 feet apart, planted 6 inches deep with at least two nodes underground [2]. NC State Franklin County: 16 to 18 inches apart in rows 3 to 4 feet apart, set 3 to 4 inches deep with several nodes below ground [4].
- **Soil.** Light, sandy soil is best, but heavier soil works if it is amended with organic matter [1]. Penn State says soil too rich in nitrogen makes many leaves and few roots, and compacted or waterlogged soil rots roots [2]. NC State Franklin County gives a pH near 6.0 [4].
- **Water.** Maryland says plants are sensitive to drought for the first 50 to 60 days after planting, and that saturated soil can cause root rot and splitting [1]. Penn State says to water newly planted slips every day for the first two weeks, then about half an inch a week [2].
- **Pests and disease.** Voles (Maryland [1]), and voles, mice, and deer (Penn State [2]). Scurf shows as dark spots on the skin. Penn State says it stays on the skin, the root can still be eaten, and it can spread in storage. A four-year rotation is its remedy [2].

## Harvest & Storage

**When.** Sweet potatoes generally mature 85 to 120 days after planting [1]. Penn State and NC State Franklin County say 90 to 120 days [2][4]. Maryland says check root size after 80 to 85 days, because roots keep growing and can split if overgrown [1]. Dig before frost. Penn State says to dig when the leaves begin to yellow, before a light frost [2].

**If frost hits the vines.** Do not wait. Maryland says dig the roots immediately, because decay in dead vines moves into the roots. If you cannot dig right away, cut the vines and throw loose soil over the rows [1]. Penn State says to cut the vines if harvest has to wait [2].

**Cold soil.** Maryland says soil below 50 degrees F can cause chilling injury [1]. Penn State's curing guide says to lift roots before soil temperatures drop below about 55 degrees F [3].

**How.** Cut the vines first to make digging easier [1]. Loosen soil with a fork or spade. The roots sit in the top 8 to 10 inches [2]. Handle them gently because they bruise easily and keep less well if cut or bruised [1][2]. Do not rub the skin and do not wash the roots before storing [1][3]. Let them dry a couple of hours in the garden if the weather allows [2].

**Cure.** Curing heals skin wounds, sets the skin, and turns starch into sugar [3]. The sources give a range of numbers:

| Source | Temperature | Humidity | Time |
|---|---|---|---|
| Penn State curing guide [3] | 80 to 85 degrees F | 85 to 95 percent | 4 to 7 days (up to 10 if heavily skinned) |
| NC State Burke County [5] | about 85 degrees F | 85 to 95 percent | 5 to 7 days |
| Univ. of Maryland, commercial [1] | 85 degrees F | 80 to 90 percent | 5 to 10 days |
| NC State Franklin County [4] | 80 to 85 degrees F | about 90 percent | about 10 days |
| Penn State, home method [2] | 80 to 85 degrees F | 85 to 90 percent | about two weeks |

Penn State's curing guide says to keep roots in dark or very low light with gentle air movement. For a small setup it suggests a closet, tent, or insulated cabinet with a thermostat-controlled heater, wet towels or a humidifier, a small fan, and a thermometer and hygrometer. Keep heaters away from anything that burns [3]. Penn State's home method puts roots in plastic bags, closed tightly with slits cut for airflow, in a sunny part of the house that reaches 80 to 85 degrees F [2]. That conflicts with the curing guide's dark cure [3]. If the bags get hot or wet, open them and check for rot.

Maryland says a curing room is very hard for home gardeners to copy. You can partly cure roots for about a week on a covered porch or in a garage, on a mesh screen in a single layer, protected from rain and animals. Maryland says roots usually store even if the cure is skipped [1], but Penn State says you need to cure sweet potatoes to keep them longer and heal wounds [2]. If you can hold 80 to 85 degrees F and high humidity for a week, do it.

**Store.** After curing, store in a cool, dark, well-ventilated place in boxes, bins, or baskets that let air move [1][3].

| Source | Storage conditions |
|---|---|
| University of Maryland Extension | 55 to 60 degrees F, 85 to 90 percent relative humidity [1] |
| Penn State curing guide | 55 to 60 degrees F, 85 to 90 percent relative humidity, 3 to 6 months [3] |
| NC State Burke County | 55 to 65 degrees F [5] |
| Penn State, home method | dark, 50 to 60 degrees F, keeps until spring [2]. The low end overlaps the chilling-injury range in [3]; aim for 55 to 60 |

**Temperatures to avoid.**
- **Too cold.** Penn State says storage below 50 to 55 degrees F causes chilling injury, with hard cores and off-flavor. Do not refrigerate fresh roots [3].
- **Too warm.** Penn State says above 65 degrees F causes sprouting and moisture loss [3]. Maryland says roots shrivel and sprout if temperatures are too high [1].
- **Overlap.** The range where the sources overlap is 55 to 60 degrees F.
- **Ethylene.** Penn State says do not store near apples or onions, because ethylene speeds sprouting [3].

**Check and discard.** Check weekly. Discard roots with severe mold, rot, or off odors [3].

## Seed Saving

Sweet potato is not saved as seed. It is a clone: each plant grows from a slip cut from a root, so a saved line stays the same.

1. At harvest, set aside small to medium roots, about 1.5 inches across and free of blemishes [2].
2. Cure and store them like the eating crop (above).
3. Six to eight weeks before planting, bed the roots in 2 to 3 inches of moist potting soil or sand, lay them on their sides and half buried, and keep them warm. Penn State says about 75 degrees F [2]. Maryland says 75 to 85 degrees F, covered with plastic until shoots appear, then under lights for 14 to 16 hours a day [1].
4. Each root gives three to five slips [2]. When slips are about 10 inches long, harden them off outdoors for a week or two [2].
5. Bought roots: Maryland says to wash off sprout-prevention chemicals before bedding them [1]. Penn State says roots treated with sprout inhibitors are harder to sprout [2]. Both advise buying certified slips or stock from a reputable source [1][2]. Penn State says slips from reputable nurseries are free of disease [2].

## Ornamental Sweet Potato Vines

Ornamental sweet potato vines are the same species, *Ipomoea batatas*, bred for showy leaves rather than for eating [6][8].

- **Taste.** NC State says cultivars bred for fancy foliage rarely produce good edible roots, and that ornamental cultivars are not as tasty [6]. Purdue Extension says the roots of ornamental cultivars are edible, but breeders choose them for foliage rather than eating quality, so flavor and texture are likely worse [8].
- **Pesticides.** Purdue advises gardeners to note whether plants grown as ornamentals have been treated with pesticides that are not labeled for use on edible sweet potatoes, and says this is typically not a problem in the home garden [8].
- **What to do.** If you do not know what a nursery sprayed on a plant, do not eat the roots or the leaves of that plant (general practice). The sources speak to roots only.
