## Zone Fit & Why It Earns Space

Dry beans give you protein and starch in a form that keeps. Penn State says one pound of dried beans makes 5 to 6 cups of cooked beans, and calls them low-cost with a long shelf life [3]. USDA FoodData Central lists cooked pinto beans, no salt, at 143 kcal and 9.0 g of protein per 100 g, and one cup is 171 g [4]. That is about 245 kcal and 15 g of protein per cup, so one pound of dry beans comes to roughly 1,200 to 1,500 kcal and 75 to 90 g of protein (worked out from [3] and [4]; other bean types differ).

Beans are legumes. Maryland says they fix nitrogen once a good root system is established [5]. Iowa State says dry beans are easy to store for long periods in sealed jars indoors [8].

## Planting Windows (Zone 7a)

Plant after the danger of frost is past [5]. Virginia gives the 50 percent-chance last spring frost for zone 7a as April 15 to 25, and the first fall frost as October 15 to 25 [6]. The guides give dates for snap, pole, and lima beans, not for dry beans [6][7]. Use them as a guide, not a rule:

| Source | Crop listed | Spring planting |
|---|---|---|
| Virginia Cooperative Extension, zone 7a [6] | snap beans | April 20 to June 10 |
| Virginia Cooperative Extension, zone 7a [6] | pole beans | April 20 to June 20 |
| University of Maryland, central Maryland [7] | snap beans | May 5 to June 30 |

Dry beans need to finish and dry down, not just make a green pod. Iowa State says pods must dry completely before harvest and that dry beans may not finish drying before frost in a short season [8]. Work back from your first fall frost using the days to maturity on your seed packet. Iowa State gives 70 to 110 days to harvest for dry beans [8]. That is a Midwest source; your variety's number matters more.

## Growing

- **Spacing.** Maryland gives 2 to 4 inches apart in rows 24 to 30 inches apart for bush snap beans, and 4 to 8 inches apart in rows 24 to 36 inches apart for pole beans [5]. It gives no separate figure for dry beans. Pole beans need a sturdy trellis 6 to 8 feet tall [5].
- **Sun.** At least 6 hours of direct light; Maryland says 8 to 10 is preferred [5].
- **Soil and feeding.** Beans need medium nutrients. Maryland says too much nitrogen delays flowering [5].
- **Water.** Keep the root zone moist by watering deeply and regularly in dry spells, and more often once pods begin to develop [5].
- **Weeds.** Pull young weeds by hand or hoe, and mulch on each side of the row [5].
- **Problems Maryland lists:** bacterial blight, Cercospora leaf spot, Mexican bean beetle, spider mites, powdery mildew, and poor blossom and fruit set [5]. Maryland also says row covers can protect new seed from birds [5].

## Harvest & Storage

**When.** Maryland says that for dry beans of all types, the pods should stay on the bush until dry and brown [5]. Iowa State says that when the pods are dry and rattle a little when shaken, the seeds inside are mature and ready to harvest [8].

**Drying and shelling.** Dry the seed further on a screen in a single layer in a cool, well-ventilated place [9]. Discard shriveled or discolored beans when you sort them [3].

**Storage.** Keep dry beans in sealed jars indoors [8]. Check stored beans now and then for insects, and throw out beans with small holes in them (general practice).

## Seed Saving

Illinois Extension lists beans as self-pollinating annuals that do not need isolation and need only a few plants for good seed [9]. Choose open-pollinated varieties, which give seed like the parent plant [9].

1. Leave your best plants to dry fully on the vine until the pods are brown and dry [5][8].
2. Shell the pods and dry the seed further on a screen in a cool, well-ventilated place [9].
3. Keep only plump, whole seed (general practice).
4. Store the seed cool and dry in a paper envelope or a paper bag in a glass jar, and label it with the name, variety, and date [9].
5. Grow enough plants that you can eat some and still save seed [9].

The sources opened give no germination life for bean seed, so test a few seeds on a wet paper towel before you plant old seed (general practice).

## ⚠ MANDATORY PREPARATION

**Raw or under-cooked dry beans can make you sick.** They contain a protein called phytohaemagglutinin (a lectin). The FDA says as few as four or five raw beans can trigger symptoms [1]. Illness starts with extreme nausea and vomiting within 1 to 3 hours, and diarrhea follows. Some people also have abdominal pain. Recovery is usually fast, within 3 to 4 hours after symptoms start, but some cases have needed hospital care [1].

Red kidney beans hold the most toxin. The FDA measures raw red kidney beans at 20,000 to 70,000 hemagglutinating units and fully cooked ones at 200 to 400. White kidney beans have about one third as much as red, and broad beans 5 to 10 percent as much [1]. The FDA says the toxin is found in many species of beans [1]. This entry applies the kidney-bean rule to all dry beans. The FDA gives no separate figure for other types.

**The FDA rule for kidney beans [1]:**
1. Soak the beans in water for **at least 5 hours**.
2. **Pour the soaking water away.**
3. Boil the beans in **fresh water for at least 30 minutes**. In the study the FDA cites, boiling for 10 minutes at 100 degrees C destroyed the toxin, and the FDA advises 30 minutes to be sure [1]. Illinois Extension says the toxin is destroyed at 212 degrees F held for at least 10 minutes, and says to boil briskly [2].
4. Keep cooking until the beans are soft. Soft texture is the sign they are done; it does not replace the 30 minutes (general practice).

**Do not use a slow cooker for dry kidney beans, or for dishes that contain them.** The FDA says slow cookers should not be used for these beans. Several outbreaks were linked to beans cooked in slow cookers, and casseroles in slow cookers often reached only 75 degrees C (167 degrees F) or less, which is not enough to destroy the toxin [1][2]. For other dry beans the FDA gives no separate slow-cooker statement. Boil them as above before they go into any slow cooker (general practice).

**Other steps:**
- **Sort first.** Remove stones and foreign matter, and discard beans that are discolored or shriveled. Rinse under cold water [3].
- **A quick soak is not the FDA rule.** Penn State describes a quick soak (boil 2 to 3 minutes, then stand covered for 1 hour) and says to discard the soaking water [3]. The FDA names a soak of at least 5 hours. If you use a quick soak, still pour the water away and boil in fresh water for at least 30 minutes.
- **Simmering is not boiling.** A low simmer or a slow cooker does not reach the temperature the FDA describes. Boil briskly [1][2].

**Common mistakes that leave the toxin active:**
- Cooking dry kidney beans in a slow cooker on any setting [1][2].
- Soaking the beans and then eating them, or adding them to a salad or casserole without boiling them. The FDA says the illness usually comes from raw, soaked kidney beans, alone or in salads or casseroles [1]. Soaking alone does not destroy the toxin.
- Boiling in the soaking water instead of fresh water [1].

**If someone ate under-cooked beans:** the usual picture is vomiting within 1 to 3 hours that passes within hours [1]. Call America's Poison Centers at 1-800-222-1222 if the vomiting is severe or will not stop, and call 911 for severe symptoms (general practice).
