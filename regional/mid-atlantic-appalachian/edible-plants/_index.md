## ☠ Look-alike matrix (read BEFORE the edible exemplars)

Every plant below that has a dangerous look-alike in this biome is paired here. The discriminators
are **necessary but not sufficient**. Positive ID needs *several* matching traits, and each entry's
`## ⚠ POSITIVE-ID SAFETY GATE` is the real test. Rows run from the most dangerous to the least.
The wild onion / death camas pair and the two hemlocks can kill: death camas can slow the heart and drop blood pressure and can be fatal (NC State), and water hemlock
brings violent seizures. If you suspect someone ate any of these, call Poison Help at **1-800-222-1222**
(call 911 for a seizure, collapse, trouble breathing or if they cannot be woken; do not induce vomiting).

| **Wild onion / garlic / ramps** (*Allium* spp.) — [entry](entries/wild-onion-garlic-allium.md) | **Eastern death camas group** (*Amianthium muscitoxicum*, *Stenanthium densum*, *Zigadenus glaberrimus*; possibly *Anticlea glauca*), plus star-of-Bethlehem, false garlic (*Nothoscordum*), autumn crocus, false hellebore and lily of the valley | An onion or garlic smell from the **individual plant in your hand** is necessary but **never sufficient**: the smell of a patch, or on your hands, proves nothing, and death camas can grow in the same patch. No smell means stop. Spring leaves look alike, so never use bulb shape, leaf feel or flower color to clear a plant. **Never eat on field ID alone**; have someone who knows the plants confirm it in person | Toxic alkaloids (NC State: high severity, "can be fatal"): heavy salivation, persistent vomiting, weakness, low blood pressure, irregular heartbeat, convulsions, coma, usually 1 to 8 hours after eating (a source on a western species). Can be lethal |
| **Wild carrot (Queen Anne's lace) and other edible-looking umbels** — see [toxic plants entry](../hazards/entries/toxic-plants-hemlock-poison-ivy.md) | **Poison hemlock** (*Conium maculatum*) | Poison hemlock: hollow, smooth, hairless stem with small purple spots; finely divided parsley-like leaves; a foul odor when crushed (NC State), which is not a test to run. Wild carrot: coarse hairs on the stem, often one dark purple floret in the center of the flower cluster. **Purple spots do not separate the hemlocks, and a hairy or green stem does not clear a plant.** Do not eat any wild white-flowered umbel unless an expert has identified the exact plant in person | Alkaloids including coniine; all parts poisonous (NC State): salivation, vomiting, diarrhea, muscle weakness, paralysis, tremors, dilated pupils, convulsions, coma. Eating it may be fatal |
| **Wild parsnip and other water-edge umbels** — see [water hemlock entry](../hazards/entries/water-hemlock-cicuta.md) | **Water hemlock** (*Cicuta maculata*) | Hairless, hollow stem, often purple-streaked or spotted (color ranges from green to purple); wet ground (ditches, stream banks, marshes); chambered root; **the root has the most toxin**. Sources word the leaf-vein feature differently and it is hard to judge in the field. Smell and taste are unreliable. Do not dig, taste or eat any wild white umbel | Cicutoxin, a violent convulsant. CDC: mild symptoms (nausea, abdominal pain) within 15 to 90 minutes, then seizures (in a Maine case convulsions began within 30 minutes, and one man died about 3 hours after eating a root). Forest Service: vomiting and convulsions within 60 minutes, often leading to death. **No antidote.** Any suspected eating is an emergency now |
| **Acorn / oak** (*Quercus*) — [entry](entries/acorn-oak.md) | **Buckeye and horse chestnut** (*Aesculus* spp.): nuts that look like acorns | Oak: nut sits in a **scaly cap**; leaves **alternate**, lobed. Buckeye / horse chestnut: **no scaly cap**; a leathery capsule (smooth in yellow buckeye, prickly in horse chestnut); large shiny nut with a pale scar; leaves **opposite**, hand-shaped with 5 or more leaflets. Never take loose nuts from the ground without knowing the tree. Acorns are also **bitter and must be leached** of tannins before eating | Buckeye / horse chestnut: aesculin and aescin; muscle weakness, paralysis, dilated pupils, vomiting, diarrhea, stupor. NC State rates poison severity **high** and says it may be fatal. Raw acorns: tannins, abdominal pain, constipation then diarrhea, and possible gut blockage |
| **Cattail** (*Typha* spp.) — [entry](entries/cattail.md) | **Blue flag iris** (*Iris versicolor*) and **yellow flag iris** (*Iris pseudacorus*) | Cattail leaf bases are **round**; iris leaves **fan out** from the base. This is a cautious screen, not a clean separator: cattail leaves also sit in two ranks, so a fan means stop even if it may reject a real cattail. Cattail has a brown sausage-shaped head with the male section **above** the female; iris has a three-up, three-down flower in spring. Do not rely on a midrib alone: sources describe one for yellow flag only. Harvest only from clean water | Iris rootstock: nausea, salivation, vomiting, abdominal pain, diarrhea, lethargy; sap irritates skin. NC State rates severity low. Cattail also takes up metals and other contaminants from polluted water |
| **Black walnut** (*Juglans nigra*) — [entry](entries/black-walnut-juglans.md) | **Kentucky coffeetree** (*Gymnocladus dioicus*; poisonous). Also on source lists: butternut and hybrids, pecan, English walnut, tree-of-heaven | Coffeetree: **twice-divided** leaves, flat reddish-brown pods up to 10 inches, no round husked nut. Walnut: once-divided leaves, round non-splitting husk, light-brown pith. Butternut pith is very dark brown, but **hybrid pith is often medium or light brown**, so the pith test does not clear hybrids. Hickory and pecan husks split along four seams. **Throw out any nut with mold, discoloration or shriveling** | Coffeetree leaves, seeds and pulp are poisonous (NC State names cytisine and rates severity low; USDA says cytisine is unverified): stomach pain, nausea, vomiting, diarrhea, irregular pulse, coma. Moldy walnuts: aflatoxin and tremorgenic mold toxins (one human case report; dogs get tremors and seizures). Walnut allergy: hives to **anaphylaxis** |
| **Violet / chickweed** (*Viola* / *Stellaria*) — [entry](entries/violet-or-chickweed.md) | For violet leaves: **lesser celandine** and **marsh marigold**. For chickweed: **scarlet pimpernel** and **spurges** | Heart-shaped leaves clear nothing. Pick violet only **in bloom**: five unequal petals with a spur; **a yellow flower is never a violet**. Chickweed: opposite smooth oval leaves, white flowers with five deeply split petals; **milky sap, a square stem, red or pink flowers or a maroon leaf spot mean stop**. **No taste test** | Lesser celandine and marsh marigold: protoanemonin (vomiting, diarrhea, mouth ulcers). Scarlet pimpernel: saponins. Spurge sap: blistered lips and mouth, eye injury |
| **Dandelion** (*Taraxacum officinale*) — [entry](entries/dandelion.md) | Catsear, chicory, sow thistle, mouse-ear hawkweed, **common groundsel**, **arnica** and **spotted spurge** (sap looks like dandelion's) | One head on a hollow, unbranched, leafless stem from a rosette of hairless leaves. Hairy leaves or a branched stalk is catsear; leaves up the stem is chicory or sow thistle; felty silver leaves is hawkweed. A branched leafy stem with petal-less clustered heads is groundsel; opposite leaves up a hairy stem with one head is arnica. **Milky sap clears nothing**; opposite leaves, a mat form or a maroon leaf spot means spurge: stop | Common groundsel (NC State: medium severity): delayed toxicity, vomiting, lethargy, signs of liver failure. Arnica (NC State: high severity, helenalin): heart palpitations, vomiting, drowsiness, shortness of breath. Spotted spurge sap: skin rash, corneal damage, blistering of lips and mouth if eaten. Catsear, chicory, sow thistle and hawkweed are not listed as human hazards in the sources opened |
| **Pawpaw** (*Asimina triloba*) — [entry](entries/pawpaw-asimina.md) | **No toxic look-alike named** by the sources opened (NC State lists dwarf pawpaw, *A. parviflora*, as often confused with it; neither source calls it toxic). The hazard is the plant's own parts: **skin, seeds, leaves and stems** | Simple, alternate, smooth-edged leaves up to about a foot long, hanging in two rows; naked red-brown hairy buds; large green berry that turns yellowish, then black. **Eat only the ripe flesh; do not eat the skin or seeds; do not eat leaves or stems** | Seed and peel alkaloids (emetic): stomach and intestinal pain, vomiting; skin irritation from handling fruit. Annonacin in the pulp is neurotoxic in lab studies, and one 2020 case report describes a man with possible progressive supranuclear palsy who ate large amounts of raw fruit for years (cause not shown) |
| **Wild blackberry, black raspberry, wineberry** (*Rubus* spp.) — [entry](entries/wild-blackberry-raspberry-rubus.md) | **No poisonous look-alike named** for the ripe fruit. Plants named as confusable: multiflora rose (*Rosa multiflora*) and smooth blackberry (*Rubus canadensis*, edible). Hazards are **where you pick** (sprayed roadsides) and **ticks** | Cluster of tiny bumps that goes red, then black (wineberry stays bright red). **Hollow cup when picked = raspberry type; spongy core that comes with the fruit = blackberry.** Berry color is not a test. Multiflora rose: **5 to 11 leaflets, fringed stipules**, small red hips; stem colour is not reliable. Wineberry: dense wine-red hairs, white woolly leaf undersides; invasive | No toxin reported in the sources opened. Herbicide residue on sprayed ground (a Colorado source; a Penn State module shows PennDOT roadside programs); tick-borne disease from brushy ground |

> **Rows for pawpaw, dandelion and the brambles differ from the rows above them.** Their sources name no
> look-alike that is deadly for the part you eat, or none at all. The hazards there are the wrong plant
> part (pawpaw), two poisonous look-alikes and a toxic spurge in the same lawns (dandelion) and the place you pick (brambles). Missing data
> on look-alikes is not a promise that none exists.

> **No onion smell = do not eat. An onion smell does not make it an onion.** The *Allium* / death-camas
> pair is the deadliest field mistake in this biome. **Two umbel killers:** poison hemlock and water
> hemlock make every wild white-flowered umbel a do-not-touch unless an expert confirms it. And **berry
> color and "animals eat it" are not tests**: see the wild-plants rules in the
> [food guide](../../../05_food.md) (red baneberry and pokeweed berries are poisonous).

## Synthesis & load order

No plant here is cleared by a glance. Even the entries rated lowest for hazard (dandelion, violet, chickweed,
cattail, brambles) have a toxic plant in the same habitat, and each has its own gate. The most dangerous are the
**onion** group (death-camas twin) and the **umbel/carrot** group (the two hemlocks). Calorie reality: greens
are low-calorie; **acorns and nuts are the real wild calories here**, but acorns demand tannin leaching and
have a potentially fatal look-alike nut (buckeye and horse chestnut). Fruit and nut entries have their own traps:
[`pawpaw-asimina`](entries/pawpaw-asimina.md) (eat only the ripe flesh, never the skin, seeds, leaves or stems),
[`black-walnut-juglans`](entries/black-walnut-juglans.md) (hull and dry the nuts, throw out moldy ones, a
poisonous coffeetree look-alike, tree-nut allergy) and
[`wild-blackberry-raspberry-rubus`](entries/wild-blackberry-raspberry-rubus.md) (hollow vs solid core,
invasive wineberry, sprayed roadsides, ticks). Public land is regulated: plants in Shenandoah National Park
are protected apart from small amounts of fruit, common nuts and edible mushrooms, and Virginia state parks allow only edible fruits, berries,
fungi or nuts for personal use. Check the land manager's rule before you pick. Load order:

```
1. LOOK-ALIKE MATRIX (above)   — "what could kill me" first; internalize the onion-smell + umbel rules
2. This manifest              — find the entry + read its facets
3. The plant entry            — ⚠ POSITIVE-ID SAFETY GATE (multiple concurrent traits) + ⚠ MANDATORY PREPARATION (where present)
4. Cross-check ../hazards/    — toxic plants that share the habitat (hemlocks, poison ivy)
5. CITE the entry's sources (T1/T2) and triangulate ≥2 refs at safety-read
Positive ID or do not consume. Universal Edibility Test is a LAST resort only (and useless for fungi).
```
