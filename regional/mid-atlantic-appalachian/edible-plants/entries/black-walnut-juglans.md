## ⚠ POSITIVE-ID SAFETY GATE

> ⚠ **BLACK WALNUT: the kernel is the food; moldy nuts and nut allergy are the real dangers**
>
> **Positive-ID checklist (ALL must hold):**
> - A large tree, 50 to 90 feet when mature [1].
> - Leaves are alternate and pinnately compound (many leaflets on one stalk), 12 to 24 inches long, with about 15 to 23 leaflets [1]. Virginia Tech gives 10 to 24, with the end leaflet often poorly formed or missing [3].
> - Bark is brown, ridged and furrowed in a rough diamond pattern [1][3].
> - Twigs have chambered pith (the center is divided into thin plates) [1][2]. Black walnut pith is light brown or buff [2][3].
> - The leaf scar is three-lobed, like a "monkey face", with no band of dense hairs above it [1][2].
> - The leaves are **once-divided** (one stalk with leaflets), not twice-divided like Kentucky coffeetree [1][13].
> - Fruit is round, 2 to 2.5 inches across, a thick green husk that does not split open, with a hard, furrowed nut inside [1][3].
> - The husk stains skin and clothing heavily [1].
>
> **Do NOT use if ANY of these are present (disqualifying traits):**
> - The husk splits along four seams (hickory or pecan, not walnut) [2].
> - The nut is elongated and sharply ridged in a sticky hairy husk, the pith is dark brown, or a "moustache" of dense hairs sits above the leaf scar (butternut or a hybrid). Hybrid pith is often medium or light brown, so light pith does not rule a hybrid out [2].
> - The tree has twice-divided leaves and flat pods up to 10 inches long instead of a round husked fruit (Kentucky coffeetree, poisonous) [13].
> - The fruit is a dry, winged seed, not a husked nut (tree-of-heaven, not a nut tree) [1][3][14].
> - The shell or kernel shows mold, discoloration or shrivelling [5][8].
> - You have a tree-nut allergy [9].
>
> **Confusable with:** Kentucky coffeetree (*Gymnocladus dioicus*) → twice-divided leaves, flat seed pods → NC State says its leaves, seeds and pulp are poisonous to humans (stomach pain, nausea, vomiting, diarrhea, irregular pulse, coma), rated low severity; USDA agrees they are poisonous [13][16]. Also on NC State's and Virginia Tech's lists: butternut and hybrids, pecan, English walnut and tree-of-heaven [1][3]. No source opened says butternut, hickory, pecan or English walnut nuts are poisonous.
>
> **Verdict:** Positive ID on every checklist item, or do not consume. Even with a sure ID, a moldy nut is a nut to throw away.

## Deadly / Toxic Look-Alikes

**Kentucky coffeetree (poisonous).** NC State lists it as often confused with black walnut [1], and Virginia Tech lists it under "Looks like" [3]. NC State says "the leaves, seeds, and pulp of this tree are poisonous to livestock, humans, and pets" and that the seeds are toxic raw; NC State names the alkaloid cytisine as the toxin, with stomach pain, nausea, vomiting, diarrhea, irregular pulse and coma, at a rating of low severity [13]. USDA's plant guide agrees the seeds and pods are poisonous but says researchers at Indiana State University could not find experimental data verifying cytisine, and that hydrocyanic acid has also been suggested as a toxin [16]. Treat the tree as poisonous whatever the toxin. It is told apart by its very large **twice-divided** leaves (5 to 9 pairs of leaflets per branch) and its reddish-brown flat pods up to 10 inches long [13]. It makes no round husked nut. If you are holding a flat pod with hard seeds, put it down.

**Tree-of-heaven.** Listed by Virginia Tech as a look-alike [3]. Its leaves have 10 to 41 leaflets with a glandular, notched base on each, can smell foul when crushed, and it bears winged fruits instead of nuts [14]. It is an invasive tree. NC State tags it for contact dermatitis [14]. No nut is made.

**English walnut (*Juglans regia*).** Listed as a look-alike by NC State and Virginia Tech [1][3]. The sources opened give no discriminators for it and do not call it poisonous. A cultivated walnut tree you do not know is not a wild black walnut; use the full checklist.

**Butternut and hybrids.** Purdue Extension says butternut is "relatively easy to distinguish" from black walnut [2]. All walnuts have chambered pith, but pure butternut pith is very dark brown and a thick band of hairs (the "moustache") sits above its leaf scar. Black walnut pith is light brown and has no moustache [2]. Butternut nuts are elongated, sharply ridged and held in sticky hairy green husks [2]. Butternut bark is platy and ash-gray when mature, though some trees are dark and look like black walnut [2]. Leaf counts do not settle it: butternut has 9 to 17 leaflets [2] and black walnut about 15 to 23 [1], so the ranges overlap. Butternut is now threatened across its range by a fungal canker and is rare in many places [2]. It has also crossed with Japanese walnut, so hybrids are common and hard to read [2]. **The pith test fails for hybrids:** Purdue's table gives hybrid pith as "variable, dark brown, but more commonly medium brown or even light brown" and the hybrid leaf scar top edge as almost always notched. Purdue says no single trait separates butternut from a hybrid, and genetic testing may be the only reliable way for some trees [2]. Purdue notes butternuts were collected and eaten by Native Americans and settlers, and no source opened calls butternut or hybrid nuts harmful [2]. The practical risk is a wasted effort, not poisoning.

**Hickories and pecan.** Hickory leaflets grow larger toward the tip of the leaf, and the nuts sit in woody husks that split along four seams. Hickories lack the moustache above the leaf scar [2].

## Identification

**Tree.** Massive, 50 to 90 feet tall with a trunk 3 to 6 feet across when mature. Leaves emerge late and drop early. They turn bright yellow in fall [1].

**Leaf.** Pinnately compound, 12 to 24 inches long, leaflets egg-shaped to lance-shaped with fine teeth [1]. More leaflets and less toothed margins than pecan [1].

**Twig.** Conspicuous lenticels (small pores) and chambered pith [1][3]. The three-lobed leaf scar looks like a monkey face [1].

**Fruit.** A brown to black nut inside a fleshy, non-splitting, yellow-green husk, 1 to 3 inches, appearing in October [1]. The nut is hard and irregularly furrowed [3]. The meat is sweet and oily [3]. Sources say the nut is difficult to remove from the husk [1].

## Habitat & Range (this biome)

Black walnut is native across central and eastern North America and grows well on rich bottomlands, in moist fertile coves and on lower slopes [1]. It prefers moist, organically rich, well-drained soil and full sun [1]. NC State gives October for the fruit [1]. Higher in the Appalachians, expect later (general practice).

**Juglone.** Leaves, stems and roots contain juglone, which inhibits the growth of some plants under the tree, including tomatoes, apples and blueberries [1]. NC State tags the tree as a "problem for dogs" and for horses and does not say what the effect is [1].

## ⚠ MANDATORY PREPARATION

> From University of Illinois and University of Maine Extension. They are not cooking steps.
>
> 1. **Wear gloves.** The outside husks "will stain just about anything" [4]. Illinois also suggests a helmet when driving over nuts [4].
> 2. **Hull soon after the nuts fall.** Iowa State: hull "immediately" after harvest, because hull juice left on will discolor the kernel and make it strong-tasting [15]. Illinois describes two methods: spread nuts on a gravel driveway and drive over them a few times, or force each nut through a 1-5/8 inch hole drilled in thick plywood with a heavy hammer to shear off the husk [4].
> 3. **Wash the hulled nuts** in a bucket to remove juice and debris. Nuts that float are unfilled and should be removed [4].
> 4. **Dry and cure.** Spread unshelled nuts in thin layers (no more than three nuts deep) in a cool, dry, well-ventilated place out of direct sunlight. Illinois and Maine say several weeks [4][5]; Iowa State says two or three weeks [15].
> 5. **Store dry.** Keep cured, unshelled nuts in mesh or burlap bags in a cool, dry place, for up to a year [4][5]. Shelled kernels can be kept refrigerated or frozen [4][5].
> 6. **Throw out any nut with mold, discoloration or shrivelling** (see next section) [5][8].

## Moldy nuts: people and dogs are not the same case

**What the extension and health sources say about people.** Maine Extension answered a reader whose dried black walnuts had white mold on the shells. It advised discarding them: molds on nuts are more likely to be aflatoxin producers, aflatoxins can be cancer-causing, and mold may be present on the kernel even when you cannot see it inside the shell [5]. MedlinePlus says aflatoxins are made by a mold that grows on nuts, seeds and legumes, that high doses can cause acute liver damage, and recommends discarding nuts that look moldy, discolored or shrivelled [8]. Neither source opened separates husks from shells. Treating a moldy husk like a moldy shell and not eating the nut is this guide's judgment.

**One reported human case.** A 2019 case report describes one adult man who ate about eight fungus-infected walnut kernels. About 12 hours later he had tremors, widespread pain, poor coordination, confusion, anxiety and sweating. Hospital supportive care followed and he recovered fully [6]. The dominant mold was *Penicillium crustosum*. Penitrem A (low level) and roquefortine C (much higher) were found on the kernels [6]. The title calls it "putative" (probable, not proven). The walnut species is not given in the summary opened. This is a single case.

**Dogs (a separate source, not about people).** The Merck Veterinary Manual lists moldy walnuts among the foods that cause tremorgenic mycotoxicosis in dogs. Signs can start within hours: vomiting, tremors, loss of balance and seizures. Most dogs recover over days to weeks, but some stay unsteady for months to years [7]. That page says nothing about humans. Do not apply dog findings to people, or the reverse.

**If you feel ill after eating a questionable nut.** Call Poison Help at 1-800-222-1222. Call 911 if the person collapses, has a seizure, has trouble breathing or cannot be woken. Do not induce vomiting [10]. Keep a sample.

## Uses & Nutrition

NC State calls the nut sweet and edible. An edible oil can be pressed from the seed but goes rancid quickly [1]. The husk can be used as a dye [1]. No calorie figure was found in the sources opened.

## Cautions

1. **Tree-nut allergy.** The FDA lists tree nuts, with walnuts named as an example, among the nine major food allergens. Reactions can include hives, rash, mouth tingling, swelling of the face, tongue or lips, vomiting, diarrhea, cramps, wheezing, dizziness and throat swelling. Any allergic reaction can lead to anaphylaxis, which can be life-threatening [9]. A person with a known tree-nut allergy should not eat this nut. Trouble breathing, throat swelling or fainting is an emergency: call 911 (general practice).
2. **Mold.** See the section above.
3. **Look-alike pods and seeds.** Kentucky coffeetree seeds are poisonous [13]. Do not crack or roast any seed you cannot tie to a round husked walnut on a pinnately compound tree.
4. **Do not confuse the husk with food.** Only the kernel is the food part named in sources [1][3].
5. **Public land.** Rules differ by land manager. Shenandoah National Park lists walnuts and hickory nuts among the nuts that may be gathered, with amounts set in the superintendent's compendium, and says plants in the park are protected [11]. Virginia state parks allow edible fruits, berries, fungi or nuts for personal use only [12]. Check the exact land manager's rule (not legal advice). No survival exemption is claimed here.
6. **Universal Edibility Test.** Last resort only.
