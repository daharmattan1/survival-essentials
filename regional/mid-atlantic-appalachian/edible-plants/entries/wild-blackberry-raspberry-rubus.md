## ⚠ POSITIVE-ID SAFETY GATE

> ⚠ **BRAMBLE FRUIT: low poison risk in the sources, but the risks are the place you pick and what you pick with**
>
> **Positive-ID checklist (ALL must hold):**
> - Prickly canes, arching with age, that fruit in their second year [2][3][5].
> - Leaves with three or five leaflets (wineberry: 3 to 5, usually 3) on prickly stems; more than five leaflets points to multiflora rose [2][3][5][11].
> - Flowers with five white petals [2][3].
> - Fruit is a cluster of many tiny juicy bumps (a "compound drupe") that goes white or green, then red, then black (blackberry, black raspberry) or stays bright red (wineberry) [2][3][5].
> - Pick one ripe berry off the stem and look at the stem end. A hollow cup means raspberry type. A pale, spongy core that comes away with the fruit means blackberry [1].
> - Typically sunny, disturbed ground [1][3].
>
> **Do NOT use if ANY of these are present (disqualifying traits):**
> - The plant does not match every trait above. Multiflora rose (*Rosa multiflora*) has **5 to 11 leaflets** on each leaf and **fringed leaf-base stipules** [11]. Stem colour is not a reliable separator: NC State's rose page says both "consistently green" and "green to red" canes [11], and its black raspberry page says canes are initially green [3].
> - The patch is on a roadside or public land where herbicide may have been sprayed [7].
> - The fruit is moldy, leaking, or you cannot tell what it is (general practice).
> - You have had a reaction to this fruit before (general practice).
>
> **Confusable with:** Multiflora rose → 5 to 11 leaflets, fringed stipules, small red hips in clusters instead of a cluster of tiny bumps [11] → NC State tags rose hips as edible fruit and names no toxicity [11]. *Rubus canadensis* (smooth blackberry) → nearly prickle-free stems [12] → also an edible blackberry [12]. No poisonous look-alike is named for the ripe fruit of these plants. That is missing data, not proof that none exists.
>
> **Verdict:** Positive ID on every checklist item, or do not consume. Do not use berry color as a test. A source reminds readers that blackberries are red when unripe and that raspberries come in red and black [1].

## Look-alikes (no poisonous look-alike named)

**Between brambles (all edible per the sources).** Black raspberry and blackberry are both black when ripe, so color is no guide. Look at the core. Raspberries (and wineberry) leave the core on the plant and the picked berry is hollow. Blackberries keep the core and have a spongy white center [1][4]. Canes differ too: blackberry canes are purplish-red with stout, straight, broad-based thorns [1]. Black raspberry canes have a whitish bloom with scattered, smaller hooked thorns [1][3]. Red raspberry canes are bristly with few or no thorns [1]. Allegheny blackberry is told from other native blackberries by gland-tipped hairs on its branches [2].

**Wineberry (*Rubus phoenicolasius*).** Introduced from East Asia and invasive. Stems and the sepals around the fruit are densely covered with wine-red hairs and prickles, and leaf stalks are often wine-red. Leaf undersides are silvery-white and woolly. The fruit is glossy, bright red, hollow when picked, and stays hidden under the hairy sepals until almost ripe [4][5]. Wineberry can be confused with other blackberries and raspberries, but white woolly leaf undersides plus dense glandular hairs on the canes separate it [5].

**Multiflora rose (*Rosa multiflora*).** NC State lists the three bramble species as confusable with it, and says it can be mistaken for brambles because of similar growth and prickles [11]. The tells: compound leaves with **5 to 11 leaflets**, **fringed stipules** (small leaf-like flaps) where the leaf stalk meets the stem, small white flowers in many-flowered clusters and small red hips about a quarter inch across in clusters in late summer [11]. NC State marks the fruit as edible and lists no toxicity [11]. Rose hips are not a bramble berry: no cluster of tiny bumps.

**Smooth blackberry (*Rubus canadensis*).** Listed by NC State as confusable with Allegheny blackberry [2]. It has almost completely smooth, prickle-free stems and edible fruit up to an inch long [12]. Do not take a thornless cane as proof of anything.

Do not eat from a plant you cannot match.

## Identification

**Growth.** Canes are biennial: first-year canes are long and do not flower, and fruit forms on second-year canes, which then die back [2][5]. The plant is perennial because it sends up new canes each year [5]. Blackberries tend to be taller than raspberries [1].

**Leaves and thorns.** Blackberry leaves are usually three-parted, leaflets up to 4 inches with coarse double-toothed edges [2]. Black raspberry canes start green and hairless with a powdery bloom, then turn brown and woody [3]. Stout thorns on blackberry, small hooked thorns on black raspberry, bristles on red raspberry (see above) [1].

**Flowers.** Five white petals, April to June for black raspberry [3] and May to June for Allegheny blackberry [2]. Wineberry flowers in late spring or early summer [4].

**Fruit.** Allegheny blackberry fruit is about three-quarters of an inch long and shaped like a thimble [2]. Black raspberry fruit is under an inch and turns black-purple [3]. Wineberry looks like a small glossy red raspberry [4].

## Habitat & Range (this biome)

Brambles are usually found in disturbed areas such as roadsides, logged woods, clearings, abandoned fields and fence rows, and they need full sun [1]. Black raspberry grows along roadsides, in woodlands and in disturbed areas, and prefers partial sun and moist, organic soil [3]. Allegheny blackberry is native to forests, woodlands and grassy balds in the mountains [2]. Wineberry is common along fence rows, paths and forest edges, and in fields, orchards and roadsides [4].

**Season.** NC State gives black raspberry fruit in June to July and blackberry fruit in July [2][3]. Penn State Extension says black and red raspberries usually ripen in July and blackberries a little later, with some overlap [1]. Wineberry fruits in midsummer [4]. Higher elevations ripen later (general practice).

---

## Uses & Nutrition

Allegheny blackberry fruit is seedy and sweet when fully ripe and is eaten fresh, preserved or frozen, and used in pies, syrups and jams [2]. Black raspberry fruit is sweet and slightly tart and can be eaten raw or cooked [3]. Wineberry produces edible berries in midsummer [4]. No source opened gives calories. Fresh bramble fruit is a summer food that spoils fast (general practice).

## Cautions

**Thorns.** Blackberry thorns are stout, straight and broad-based, black raspberry thorns small and hooked, wineberry covered in red prickles and hairs [1][4]. Wear long sleeves and gloves (general practice). The sources opened give no wound care.

**Ticks.** Ticks live in grassy, brushy or wooded areas [6]. A bramble patch is brushy ground (general practice). CDC advice: avoid wooded and brushy areas with high grass and leaf litter, walk in the center of trails, do a full body check after being outdoors, shower within two hours of coming in, and remove any tick you find [6]. See the [Ticks & Lyme entry](../../hazards/entries/ticks-and-lyme.md) for what to watch for afterward.

**Sprayed roadsides and pesticides.** Brambles grow along roadsides [1][3], and roadsides are a place where herbicide may be applied. Colorado State Extension tells foragers to "avoid contaminants by staying away from trails and roadsides where pesticides and herbicides are sprayed" and to check that an area has not been sprayed [7]. That source is from Colorado, not this region. In this region, a Penn State training module describes PennDOT's roadside herbicide program, which includes selective weed and brush control, with primary roads typically treated every year [13]. It does not name brambles or mention foraging, so no in-region foraging statement was found. Pick away from roadsides and from land you cannot confirm was left unsprayed (this guide's judgment). Wash fruit before eating (general practice).

**Wineberry is invasive.** University of Maryland Extension says do not plant wineberry even though the fruit is edible, because it forms thickets that crowd out native plants. Seeds are spread by birds and other animals, and arching canes root where they touch soil [4]. University of Illinois Extension reports it is on a formal invasive-species list in 16 states [5]. Do not carry canes or seed to new places.

**Poison ivy.** Poison ivy also has three leaflets and can grow in the same edges and fence rows (general practice). See the [toxic plants entry](../../hazards/entries/toxic-plants-hemlock-poison-ivy.md).

**Reactions and toxicity.** NC State lists no toxicity warnings for the fruit or other parts of these two native species [2][3]. If someone feels ill after eating wild fruit, call Poison Help at 1-800-222-1222. Call 911 if the person collapses, has a seizure, has trouble breathing or cannot be woken. Do not induce vomiting [10]. Keep a sample.

**Public land.** Rules differ by land manager. Shenandoah National Park says small amounts of fruit may be collected for personal use (generally up to one quart per person per day) and names blackberries as an example, with the full list set by the superintendent's compendium [8]. Virginia state parks allow edible fruits, berries, fungi or nuts for personal use only [9]. Check the exact land manager's rule before you pick (not legal advice). No survival exemption is claimed here.

**Do not dig up wild brambles** to move them to a garden, because of disease risk [1].

**Universal Edibility Test.** Last resort only.
