## ⚠ POSITIVE-ID SAFETY GATE

> ⚠ **PAWPAW: only the ripe flesh is listed as edible; skin, seeds, leaves and stems are not**
>
> **Positive-ID checklist (ALL must hold):**
> - A small tree or large shrub, usually 15 to 30 feet tall, often in colonies [1][4].
> - Leaves are simple (one blade, not divided), alternate, smooth-edged, and large: about 6 to 12 inches long, hanging in two rows, with a pale underside [1][6].
> - Crushed leaves have a strong smell. Sources describe it as tomato or green pepper [6]. One describes crushed leaves and cut wood as "somewhat like turpentine" [5]. Do not use smell alone.
> - Twigs have naked (scale-free), red-brown, hairy buds [6].
> - Fruit is a large green berry, roughly 2.5 to 6 inches long, becoming yellowish and then black as it overripens [1][3][6].
> - Habitat is moist, rich forest or bottomland, not dry open ground [1][4].
> - Season is late summer into fall (see Habitat & Range).
>
> **Do NOT use if ANY of these are present (disqualifying traits):**
> - The plant does not match every leaf, twig and fruit trait above.
> - The fruit is hard. A source says not to harvest until the skin gives slightly to a finger squeeze [3].
> - You are only holding skin, seeds, leaves or stems. Sources say do not eat them (see Cautions) [1][3].
> - You have had a reaction to pawpaw before, or your skin reacts when you handle it [1][2].
>
> **Confusable with:** The sources opened name no toxic look-alike. One regional flora says there are no related species and no potentially confusing species in its area [5]. NC State lists dwarf pawpaw (*A. parviflora*) as often confused with it, and a federal review says the two hybridize; neither source calls dwarf pawpaw toxic [1][2].
>
> **Verdict:** Positive ID on every checklist item, or do not consume. This entry does not say any wild plant is edible for you. It reports what the sources say.

## Deadly / Toxic Look-Alikes

No source opened names a poisonous plant that is commonly mistaken for pawpaw [1][2][5]. That is missing data, not proof that none exists. The checklist above is what you rely on. The risk with pawpaw is less about mistaking the plant and more about eating the wrong parts or reacting to the fruit.

If any trait fails, stop. Do not eat fruit from a plant whose leaves, twigs and fruit you could not match.

## Identification

**Form.** A native, deciduous large shrub or small tree, 20 to 40 feet tall in one federal review [2], typically 15 to 30 feet in another [1]. It spreads by root suckers into groves that can be a single plant [4]. Maryland Extension notes it is the only member of its family (Annonaceae) in the northeastern U.S. [4].

**Leaves.** Alternate, simple, smooth-edged, up to about a foot long. They hang in two rows and have a pale underside [1][3][6]. The leaves are "odorous when bruised" [2]. Fall color is yellow [1].

**Twigs and bark.** Twigs carry naked, red-brown, hairy buds [6]. Bark is smooth, light brown or gray with wart-like lenticels, becoming fissured with age [1].

**Flowers.** Six dark purplish-brown (maroon) petals, about 1 to 1.5 inches across, with a weak, unpleasant odor, pollinated by flies and beetles [1][3]. Sources disagree on timing: before the leaves in early spring [1][3], or with the leaves from February to May depending on latitude [2]. Maryland Extension gives March to May [4]. A tree needs a genetically different tree nearby to set fruit [3][4].

**Fruit.** A large oblong to cylindrical berry, alone or in clusters like banana "hands" [3]. Length is given as 2.5 to 6 inches [1], 1 to 6 inches [3] or about 4 inches [6]. Skin goes from green to light green or yellowish at peak ripeness, then black when overripe [3]. The flesh is custard-like, with large seeds inside [1][4].

## Habitat & Range (this biome)

Pawpaw is widely distributed across the eastern United States, east to the Appalachians [2]. It is native throughout Maryland [4]. It is typically found in open bottomland woods and also does well in uplands with rich soil [4]. It grows in moist, nutrient-rich forests [1].

**Season.** Maryland Extension and NC State give fruit from August to October [1][4]. A federal review gives July to September [2]. Expect the earlier end in the warmer lowlands and the later end higher in the Appalachians (general practice). Maryland Extension notes that deer generally leave pawpaw alone, because of chemicals in the bark and foliage [4], so groves can be dense in browsed woods.

## ⚠ MANDATORY PREPARATION

> These steps come from the extension sources. None of them is a cooking step, and no source opened says cooking removes the harmful compounds from skin or seeds.
>
> 1. **Wear gloves when harvesting.** NC State states that contact dermatitis (a skin rash from touching) "has been known to occur" [1].
> 2. **Pick fruit only when the skin gives slightly under a finger squeeze** [3].
> 3. **Eat only the ripe flesh.** Do not eat the skin. Do not eat the seeds. Clemson says the seeds and the green peel contain alkaloids that cause vomiting and "should not be consumed" [3]. NC State lists stomach and intestinal pain from the skin or seeds [1].
> 4. **Eat a small amount the first time.** NC State: raw fruit can be eaten "in small quantities" but can cause stomach or skin irritation in some people [1].
> 5. **Do not eat leaves or stems** [1].

Brooklyn Botanic Garden's flora says the fruit can be eaten raw or cooked for puddings. Its page also carries a general disclaimer against using any listed plant as food or medicine without first consulting a physician [5].

## Uses & Nutrition

The ripe fruit is the only food part named. Maryland Extension describes it as a mix of banana, mango and citrus with a hint of vanilla, and says it is the largest native fruit in North America [4]. It bruises easily and has a short shelf life [4]. No calorie figure was found in the sources opened.

Wildlife use the fruit: foxes, opossums, raccoons and squirrels eat it [4]. In a food emergency it is a late-summer fruit, not a staple (this guide's judgment).

## Cautions

**Reported reactions (what sources say).**
- **Stomach and intestines:** stomach and intestinal pain from the skin or seeds [1]; vomiting from seed alkaloids [2] and from seed and green-peel alkaloids [3]. The seeds contain asiminine, an alkaloid "reported to have emetic properties" [2]. Brooklyn Botanic Garden calls the seed alkaloid asiminine "an emetic and narcotic" [5].
- **Skin and allergy:** skin irritation or contact dermatitis from handling fruit [1]; handling may cause allergic reactions in sensitive people [2][5].
- **Raw fruit in some people:** stomach or skin irritation [1].

**Parts not to eat.** Leaves and stems [1]. Skin and seeds [1][3]. The bark holds an alkaloid (analobine) that a federal review says was once used as medicine [2]. That is not a food use. Leaf, bark and twig tissue contain acetogenins, defensive compounds [3][1].

**Neurotoxin in the pulp: lab findings.** The fruit pulp contains annonacin, a compound from the acetogenin family. A 2012 lab study found an average of 0.0701 mg per gram of pulp and found that purified annonacin and crude pulp extract killed half of cultured rat brain cells in 48 hours at the concentrations tested [7]. The authors call for more study of the risk from chronic exposure [7]. FDA-affiliated researchers measured annonacin and squamocin in freeze-dried pulp [8].

**Neurotoxin in the pulp: one human case report.** In 2020, doctors at Johns Hopkins described an 80-year-old man in the Northeast U.S. with what the authors called possible progressive supranuclear palsy (a rare brain disease affecting balance, eye movement and speech) who had habitually eaten pawpaw from the family nursery for about 5 years before his symptoms began and until his death. His wife estimated he ate up to 13.6 kg of raw fruit a year in the five years before he died [12]. The authors suggest annonacin-containing products, pawpaw included, as "a possible environmental risk factor" for atypical parkinsonism. They note his illness did not match the pattern seen in earlier studies of populations that regularly ate annonaceous fruit, and say further study is needed [12]. This is a single case, and a single case does not show that pawpaw caused his illness (this guide's reading).

NC State rates poison severity as low [1]. The practical reading is to eat small amounts of ripe flesh now and then, not large amounts day after day for years (this guide's judgment). People with a parkinsonism-type illness should ask their doctor before eating pawpaw (general practice).

**If you feel ill.** Call Poison Help at 1-800-222-1222. Call 911 if the person collapses, has a seizure, has trouble breathing or cannot be woken. Do not induce vomiting [9]. Keep a sample of what was eaten.

**Public land.** Foraging rules differ by land manager. Shenandoah National Park allows small amounts of some fruit for personal use (generally up to one quart per person per day) and sends readers to the superintendent's compendium for the list [10]. Pawpaw is not named on that page, so check the compendium. Virginia state parks allow edible fruits, berries, fungi or nuts for personal use only [11]. Check the rule of the exact land manager before you pick (not legal advice). No survival exemption is claimed here.

**Universal Edibility Test.** Last resort only, and it is not a green light.
