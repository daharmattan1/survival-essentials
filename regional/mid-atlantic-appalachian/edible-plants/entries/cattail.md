## ⚠ POSITIVE-ID SAFETY GATE

> ⚠ **CATTAIL — Tall marsh plant with cigar-shaped seed pods; clean-water habitat only**
>
> **Positive-ID checklist (ALL must hold):**
> - Grows in shallow standing water or marsh (freshwater, preferably clean)
> - Tall unbranched stem (3–8 feet), arising from rhizome in mud
> - Long, narrow, blade-like leaves (0.5–1 inch wide) surrounding stem base
> - Dense, cylindrical, dark-brown "cigar" seed head at top of stem (female spike, ~1 inch diameter, 3–9 inches long)
> - Below female spike: lighter male pollen spike (yellow, disappears early summer)
> - When crushed, rhizomes smell starchy/earthy, not musty or foul
>
> **Do NOT use if ANY of these are present (disqualifying traits):**
> - Water source is stagnant, foul-smelling, or visibly polluted (absorbs contaminants)
> - Growing in saltwater or brackish marsh (salt tolerance low; indicates stressed habitat)
> - Rhizome smells musty, rotten, or sulfurous (anaerobic decay or chemical contamination)
> - Mistaken for Iris (see Look-Alikes below)
>
> **Confusable with:** **Iris** (toxic) before flowering — see section below.
>
> **Verdict:** Positive ID on every checklist item. **Water quality is critical**: harvest only from clean, moving or fresh standing water. Cattail is a hyperaccumulator of heavy metals and absorbs toxins from polluted habitats.

## Deadly / Toxic Look-Alikes

**Yellow Flag Iris** (*Iris pseudacorus*) and **Blue Flag Iris** (*Iris versicolor*) are toxic plants that share cattail marshes.

### Iris vs. Cattail (Early-Season Confusion)

The confusion risk is **highest in spring (Apr–May)** when iris shoots are emerging and cattail shoots are leafing out, before flower spikes appear.

| Feature | **Cattail** | **Iris** | Discriminator |
|---------|-----------|------|----|
| **Leaf cross-section shape** | D-shaped: flat on one side, rounded on the other (like a ribbon with a slight convex back) | Equitant / flattened, with a prominent central ridge or keel — strongest in *Iris pseudacorus* | Squeeze the leaf edge-on; cattail = smooth D-profile; iris = distinct keel or ridge down center |
| **Leaf base color** | Green throughout, no purple tinge | Often has a **purple-tinged leaf base** where it sheathes the stem | Check where leaves emerge from the mud; purple base = iris |
| **Leaf center rib** | No raised rib; leaf is flat/smooth | Prominent raised center rib (strongest in *Iris pseudacorus*) | Feel the leaf texture: iris has a stiff ridge; cattail is uniformly smooth |
| **Leaf smell** | Starchy/earthy/neutral | Musty/iris perfume scent | Iris leaves have a distinctive musty iris perfume; cattail does not |
| **Flower/spike** | Brown cigar-shaped seed head (late spring onward); absent in early spring shoots | Blue, yellow, or purple iris flower (May–Jun); no brown cigar spike | If flowers or the cigar spike are visible, ID is certain |
| **Rhizome / tubers** | Thick, fibrous, stringy rhizome in mud; white inside | Thick, fleshy, smooth rhizome (thumb-sized); often pale tan | Dig and compare: iris rhizome is smooth and fat; cattail has a stringy, fibrous rhizome |

**Why this matters:** Iris rhizomes are **toxic** (contain pentacyclic terpenoids — zeorin, missourin, missouriensin — concentrated in the rhizome; ingestion causes nausea, vomiting, abdominal pain, and diarrhea; sap also causes skin irritation). A mistake is unlikely if you wait until flower spike / true flower appears (positive ID), but early-season harvests of cattail shoots can be confused with iris shoots.

**Solution:** When harvesting spring shoots, either:
1. Wait until the male pollen spike appears atop the stem (clearly cattail), or
2. Feel the central rib on the leaf — iris has it; cattail does not.

## Identification

**Vegetative form (year-round in marsh):**
- **Stems:** Unbranched, stiff, cylindrical, 3–8 feet tall, arising singly or in clusters from rhizomes in mud/shallow water.
- **Leaves:** Long, narrow (0.5–1 inch wide), blue-green, sheathing the stem at the base. Cross-section is D-shaped (flat on the inner face, slightly rounded on the outer face); no central ridge (unlike iris, which has a distinct central keel). Leaf base is consistently green, with no purple tinge (iris often shows purple at the leaf base).
- **Rhizome:** White-cream inside, fibrous and stringy, often growing horizontally through mud 1–2 feet deep. No tubers.

**Flower/fruiting form (late spring–fall):**
- **Male spike (pollen):** Light tan or yellow, at the very top, ~2 inches long. Appears May–Jun, sheds pollen (bright yellow, visible on hands after touching), then withers and disappears.
- **Female spike (seed pod):** Dense, cylindrical, dark-brown, 1–1.5 inches diameter, 3–9 inches long. Matures Jun–Nov. When mature, spike becomes cottony (wind-dispersed seeds in papery fluff).

**Growth stages by season:**
- **Spring (Mar–May):** Shoots emerge as thick stems (pencil-sized), coated in leaf sheaths, entirely white inside. Male pollen spike appears briefly, then fades.
- **Summer (Jun–Aug):** Female seed spike matures; leaves fully expand; plant at maximum height.
- **Fall–winter (Sep–Nov):** Seed spike fluffs and disperses; plant senesces but rhizomes persist and are harvestable.

## Habitat & Range (this biome)

Cattail is ubiquitous in suitable habitat across Mid-Atlantic/Appalachian (7a):

- Freshwater marshes, swamps, pond/lake edges
- Stream backwaters and slow-moving water
- Ditch banks (if water is permanent)
- Wet meadows with seasonal ponding

**Range:** Two species in this region: *Typha latifolia* (broadleaf cattail, wider leaves, prefers deeper water) and *Typha angustifolia* (narrowleaf cattail, narrower leaves, tolerates shallow water). Both are edible and similar.

**Water quality requirement:** Harvest from clean, fresh-water habitats only. Avoid:
- Urban storm drains / roadside ditches (heavy metals from runoff)
- Industrial / discharge areas
- Visibly stagnant, foul-smelling water
- Saltwater marshes

Cattail absorbs mercury, lead, arsenic, and other contaminants from polluted water and sediment. A polluted habitat will concentrate toxins in the rhizome.

## ⚠ MANDATORY PREPARATION

Preparation differs by plant part and season:

**Rhizomes (year-round harvest; best fall–winter):**

Rhizomes are starchy and calorie-dense (wild carbohydrate source). Harvesting is labor-intensive but rewarding:

1. **Harvest:** Dig up rhizomes from mud (use a stick or digging tool; they're tough). Look for the white, fibrous horizontal runner in the mud.
2. **Clean:** Wash thoroughly in running water to remove mud and debris. Peel away the fibrous outer layer to expose the white starch.
3. **Extract starch (pounding method):**
   - Cut peeled rhizomes into 2-inch segments.
   - Pound in a mortar or between two rocks into pulp.
   - Submerge pulp in cold water; stir well. The starch settles; fibrous material floats.
   - Decant water and fibers; collect settled white starch.
   - Repeat 2–3 times until water is mostly clear.
   - Dry the starch paste on a cloth in the sun or over low heat (~100 °F) until brittle.
   - Grind into flour for baking or thickening.
4. **Alternative (boiling):**
   - Boil peeled rhizome segments until soft (~20–30 min).
   - Drain and mash, or slice and dry for later use.
   - Boiled rhizomes have a mild, starchy flavor and can be eaten as-is with salt.

**Young shoots (spring, Mar–Jun):**

Emerging shoots (before leaf unroll) are tender and mild. Harvest in spring, when shoots are 6–12 inches tall and still mostly white.

1. **Harvest:** Pull or cut at the base where the shoot emerges from the water.
2. **Prepare:** Remove outer fibrous leaf layers; the inner white core is the edible part.
3. **Blanch:** Boil in salted water for 3–5 min to soften. Drain and cool.
4. **Eat:** Serve hot with butter/salt, or chill for salad.

**Immature flower spikes (May–Jun):**

When flower spikes are still green/pale and tender (before they brown and fluff), they can be boiled like corn-on-the-cob:

1. **Harvest:** Break off the immature spike (still compact, pale tan or green).
2. **Boil:** Drop into salted boiling water for 5–10 min.
3. **Eat:** Scrape the kernels off the central core with your teeth (like corn). Mild, slightly starchy flavor.

**Pollen (Jun–Jul):**

The male spike sheds bright yellow pollen. Collect by shaking the spike over a cloth:

1. **Harvest:** Shake mature male spike over a container or cloth. Pollen falls freely.
2. **Use:** Add raw to flour for baking (1 part pollen to 4 parts flour for bread/pancakes). Pollen is ~23% protein and mildly sweet.
3. **Storage:** Dry pollen in the shade; store in a cool, dry place for months.

**Seeds (Sep–Nov):**

As the female spike matures and fluffs, seeds can be collected:

1. **Harvest:** Collect the fluffy seed heads; place in a bag and shake to extract seeds from the papery fluff.
2. **Process:** Winnow to remove chaff. Seeds are tiny (like poppy seeds).
3. **Use:** Ground into a flour-like powder (labor-intensive; very small yield), or eaten raw in small amounts for texture/nutrition.

## Uses & Nutrition

**Rhizome starch:** The primary caloric yield. Dried starch flour can be stored for months and used as a survival carbohydrate source.

**Shoots:** Spring green, low-calorie, tender texture.

**Flower spikes:** Novelty "corn-dog" texture; mild, starchy flavor. More of a food experience than a caloric source.

**Pollen:** High protein (~23%), mildly sweet; valuable in survival situations.

**Nutritional profile (per 100g raw rhizome):**
- ~41 calories
- 1.5g protein
- 8g carbohydrate (starch)
- Minerals: potassium, magnesium

Cattail is a **calorie-productive wild plant** in the Mid-Atlantic. The rhizome is one of the few wild starches that accumulates harvestable yields (unlike many wild greens, which are nutrient-dense but low-calorie).

## Cautions

1. **Water pollution:** Cattail is a bioaccumulator. **Never harvest from polluted water.** Urban ditches, roadsides, industrial discharge areas, and stagnant "dead" water are off-limits. Clean, fresh flowing or unpolluted standing water only.

2. **Seasonal legal restrictions:** In some states/counties, harvesting from protected wetlands requires a permit. Check local regulations.

3. **Muddy habitat hazard:** Harvesting rhizomes means digging in mud and shallow water. Wear waterproof boots and gloves; watch for sharp objects, broken glass, or contaminated sediment.

4. **Fiber irritation:** The fibrous outer layer of rhizomes and the leaf sheaths can irritate skin. Wash hands after handling.

5. **Mistaken identity (iris risk):** If you are uncertain whether a shoot is cattail or iris, wait until flower spikes appear or the central rib test (iris = rib; cattail = flat) confirms identity.

6. **Universal Edibility Test:** Cattail does not require it due to clear ID, but the test is last-resort only (and useless for fungi).
