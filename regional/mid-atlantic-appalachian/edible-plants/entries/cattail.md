## ⚠ POSITIVE-ID SAFETY GATE

> ⚠ **CATTAIL: a marsh plant that iris can imitate before it flowers; the water it grew in matters as much as the ID**
>
> **Positive-ID checklist (ALL must hold):**
> - Grows in or beside water: marsh, pond or lake edge, ditch [1][2][3].
> - Leaves are long, strap-like, with parallel veins, and **do not fan out flat from the base**. Cattail leaf bases are round [6]. Cattail leaves also sit in two ranks and are called sword-like by the Tennessee-Kentucky Plant Atlas [11], so a cattail shoot can look flattened from the side: judge the base, round (cattail) versus flat and fan-like (iris), and treat a fan as a stop even if it may reject a real cattail.
> - Look for last year's brown, sausage-shaped heads still standing next to the green shoots (general practice), or, later in the year, the head itself: a brown cylindrical spike with a **male (pollen) section above the female section** [1][2].
> - Shoots come up in spring, 4 to 16 inches tall, for the young-shoot harvest [1].
> - The water is not polluted (see Cautions).
>
> **Do NOT use if ANY of these are present (disqualifying traits):**
> - The leaves rise in a **flat fan** from the base [4][6]. That is an iris.
> - Any flower with three upright and three drooping petals (blue, purple or yellow) is on the plant [4][5].
> - You are standing in water that gets runoff, sewage, farm or industrial discharge, or that looks or smells foul (general practice; see Cautions).
> - The plant is not an obvious cattail by all of the traits above.
>
> **Confusable with:** Blue flag iris (*Iris versicolor*) and yellow flag iris (*Iris pseudacorus*) → leaves in a fan, not round at the base; iris flowers in spring; no brown sausage head → NC State rates iris poison severity **low**: nausea, vomiting, drooling, diarrhea, lethargy; the rootstock holds the most poison; skin irritation from sap [4][5].
>
> **Verdict:** Positive ID on every checklist item, or do not consume. Check the water and the land rule too.

## Deadly / Toxic Look-Alikes

**Iris is the one that matters.** Blue flag is native; yellow flag is an invasive escapee, listed as invasive in Virginia and other states [4][5]. Both grow in the same wet ground as cattail. Neither is rated deadly: NC State rates both "low" severity [4][5]. The risk is eating the rootstock or shoot by mistake.

| Trait | Cattail | Iris (blue flag, yellow flag) |
|---|---|---|
| Base of the leaves | **Round** leaf bases, not fanned [6] | **Fan-like**: yellow flag leaves "emerge in a fan-like arrangement" [6]; blue flag leaves "cross at the base to form a fan-like clump" [4] |
| Leaf shape | Strap-like; broadleaf is flat [2]; narrowleaf is convex outside and flat to slightly concave inside [3] | Sword-like, flattened [6]; yellow flag has a raised midrib [6] |
| Flower | Brown cylindrical spike with the male part above the female [1][2] | Showy three-up, three-down flower, blue to purple (blue flag) or yellow (yellow flag), April to June [4][5] |
| Rootstock | Rhizome, edible [1][2] | Poisonous, with the highest concentration of poison in the rhizome [4][5]; yellow flag rhizome sap is black [6] |

**Do not use a midrib test alone.** The sources describe a raised midrib on yellow flag [6]. None of the sources opened says blue flag has one, so "no rib" does not clear a plant. Use the fan shape of the leaf base first, and the flower or last year's heads second.

**What iris does.** Nausea, salivation, vomiting, abdominal pain, lethargy, diarrhea; skin irritation from sap, rootstock or seeds, and NC State marks blue flag as causing contact dermatitis [4][5]. Wear gloves when digging. See Cautions for first aid.

No source opened gives a smell test for telling iris from cattail. Do not use one.

## Identification

**Plant.** A tall marsh plant with long slender green stalks topped with a brown, fluffy, sausage-shaped flowering head [1]. Broadleaf cattail grows about 5 to 10 feet; NRCS gives 15 to 30 decimeters [1] and NC State 5 to 10 feet [2]. Narrowleaf runs 3 to 7 feet [3].

**Leaves.** Broadleaf: flat, gray-green, sword-like leaves from the base, up to about 1.5 inches wide [2]. Narrowleaf: tall, narrow, stiff leaves under an inch wide, parallel veins, base sheathed [3].

**Flower head.** In the spike, **male (pollen) flowers sit above the female flowers** [1][2]. NC State adds that the male part disintegrates after pollination, leaving the brown female head [2]. In narrowleaf there is a gap of half an inch or more between the two parts [3].

**Two species, same use.** NRCS says broadleaf generally grows in shallower water than narrowleaf [1]. NC State says narrowleaf grows in brackish and fresh water [3] and that broadleaf tolerates moderate salinity [2]. Both are edible when gathered at the right stage [3].

## Habitat & Range (this biome)

Fresh waters of ponds, lakes and marshes, including tidal freshwater and slightly brackish marshes [2][3]. Cattails are always in or near water [1]. Brackish tidal marsh is normal habitat in this region; avoid it because of what runs into it, not because of the salt (general practice).

**Season.** Young shoots appear in spring when 4 to 16 inches long [1]. NC State gives flowers from May to July and brown fruit from June to November [2].

## ⚠ MANDATORY PREPARATION

> The sources say parts of cattail can be eaten raw. **This guide's default is to cook wild marsh plants and wash them well** (general practice), because the plants stand in water you cannot test. No source opened gives times or temperatures for cattail.

- **Young shoots (spring).** NRCS: gathered at 4 to 16 inches; raw they taste like cucumber, steamed like cabbage; they can also be pickled [1]. NC State: raw, cooked or pickled [2]. Peel off the tough outer leaves and cook.
- **Stem base.** NRCS: the base of the stem where it meets the rhizome can be boiled or roasted like potatoes [1].
- **Young flower stalks.** NRCS: take them out of their sheaths and boil or steam them like corn [1]. Cook until tender (general practice).
- **Pollen.** NRCS: a flour substitute that colors baked goods yellow [1]. Shake it from the male part into a clean container.
- **Rhizomes.** NRCS and NC State: raw, baked, roasted or broiled [1][2]. NRCS puts the starch at about 30 to 46 percent, and says the flour would be about 80 percent carbohydrate and 6 to 8 percent protein [1]. Wash the rhizomes and peel them. Cook them.
- **Seeds.** The sources opened do not list cattail seed as human food. Do not eat it.

---

## Uses & Nutrition

NRCS lists edible young shoots, stem base, flower stalks, pollen and rhizomes, and says all parts are edible when gathered at the right stage [1]. The starch figures above are the only calorie-related numbers in the sources opened [1]. Cattail also serves for matting, tinder and insulation (fluff) and cordage [1].

## Cautions

1. **Polluted water.** NRCS notes cattails are used for wastewater tertiary treatment [1]. A study of broadleaf cattail in a Polish lake found lead, zinc, copper, nickel, cadmium and iron in roots and rhizomes (roots held the most; rhizomes were next for several metals) [7]. That is a lab finding, not a food limit. **Do not harvest from urban ditches, storm drains, runoff, discharge areas or foul water** (general practice).
2. **Iris poisoning.** If someone may have eaten iris, call Poison Help at 1-800-222-1222. Call 911 if the person collapses, has a seizure, has trouble breathing or cannot be woken. Do not induce vomiting [8]. Keep a piece of the plant for identification.
3. **Skin.** Iris sap can irritate the skin [4][5]. Wear gloves.
4. **Mud and sharp debris.** Digging in marsh mud means cuts and contaminated sediment. Wear boots and gloves (general practice).
5. **Public land.** Plants in Shenandoah National Park "may not be disturbed or collected," including roots; the exceptions cover small amounts of fruit, common nuts and edible mushrooms [9]. Virginia state parks bar gathering "any tree, flower, fern, shrub... or plant"; only edible fruits, berries, fungi or nuts may be collected for personal use [10]. Shoots, flower stalks and rhizomes are not on those lists. Check the exact land manager and any wetland rule before you pick (not legal advice). No survival exemption is claimed here.
6. **Universal Edibility Test.** Last resort only, and not a green light.
