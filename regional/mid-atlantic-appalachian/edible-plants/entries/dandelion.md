## ⚠ POSITIVE-ID SAFETY GATE

> ⚠ **DANDELION — Unmistakable milky sap + basal rosette + single hollow stem = positive ID**
>
> **Positive-ID checklist (ALL must hold):**
> - Basal rosette of deeply lobed leaves with jagged, backward-pointing margins
> - **Milky white latex sap from stems and broken leaves** (diagnostic)
> - Single hollow, leafless flower stem with one yellow head (not branched)
> - Flowers composed entirely of yellow strap-shaped florets (no disk florets)
> - Thick taproot; white/tan color when dug
>
> **Do NOT use if ANY of these are present (disqualifying traits):**
> - No milky sap when leaf is broken
> - Multiple flower heads on single stem (indicates different genus)
> - Orange or red coloration in stems or center of flower
> - Leaves not in a basal rosette pattern
>
> **Verdict:** Positive ID on every checklist item, or do not consume. Dandelion has no deadly look-alike in this region; the milky sap alone makes it unmistakable.

## Identification

Dandelion is one of the safest wild plants to identify in the Mid-Atlantic. The combination of features is diagnostic:

**Leaves:** Form a dense basal rosette, grow flat against the ground initially, deeply lobed with backward-pointing teeth (margin looks like a row of small arrows pointing toward the center). Leaves are entirely basal in the first year; flowering plant shows only the hollow stem.

**Stem:** Single, smooth, hollow, unbranched, leafless, 4–16 inches tall. **Milky latex flows from any break** — break the stem in half and white sap exudes immediately.

**Flower:** One yellow head per stem (20+ varieties of yellow flowers are misidentified as "dandelion"; true dandelion is the only common one with a solitary yellow head on a hollow stem). Head is 1–1.5 inches wide, composed entirely of strap-shaped florets (no tubular disk florets in the center).

**Taproot:** When dug, a thick white-to-tan root extends 2–3 feet deep (the whole plant will not uproot without a tool). Roots are bitter and woody.

**Flower maturation:** After flowering, the flower head matures into a spherical seed head ("blowball") of feathery seeds.

## Habitat & Range (this biome)

Dandelion is ubiquitous in Mid-Atlantic/Appalachian (7a). It appears in:
- Lawns, meadows, open fields
- Roadsides, driveways
- Disturbed ground (construction sites, power-line rights-of-way)
- Dry upland fields to occasional moist edges

**Availability by season:**
- **Spring (Mar–May):** First leaves emerge in late Feb; prime harvest window for tender young leaves (less bitter). Flower heads appear Apr–May.
- **Early summer (Jun–Jul):** Leaves tougher and more bitter; roots begin filling with energy for winter.
- **Fall (Aug–Oct):** Second rosette of leaves often appears; roots maximally bitter but nutritious.
- **Winter (Nov–Feb):** Dormant underground; roots harvestable by digging.

Dandelion thrives in disturbed, sunny areas. It is rare in deep shade or continuously wet ground.

## ⚠ MANDATORY PREPARATION

Dandelion is edible raw and cooked, but bitterness intensifies with plant age and season:

**Young leaves (spring, pre-flower):** Minimal bitterness. Eat raw in salads or blanch briefly (1–2 min). Blanching softens the texture and reduces bitterness.

**Older leaves (summer onward) or roots:** Bitterness becomes pronounced. Options:
- **Boil-and-discard method:** Bring leaves to boil in salted water, drain after 3–5 minutes, discard water. Repeat if needed. Rinse, then prepare as greens or salad.
- **Sauté in fat:** Older leaves sautéed in butter/oil with salt and garlic become palatable; the fat masks bitterness.
- **Roots:** Dig, wash thoroughly, roast at 350 °F for ~45 min until dried and brittle. Grind and brew as "dandelion coffee" (caffeine-free). Alternatively, simmer fresh roots in water for a decoction.

**Unopened flower buds & heads:** Can be sautéed whole or pickled. No bitterness issue.

Raw flowers are edible and mildly sweet; use for garnish or fritter.

## Uses & Nutrition

Dandelion is calorie-lean but nutrient-dense:

**Nutritional content (per 100g raw leaves):**
- ~45 calories
- 2.7g protein
- Fiber
- High in vitamins A, K, C
- Minerals: iron, calcium, potassium
- Bitter compounds (taraxacin) stimulate digestion

**Culinary uses:**
- Young spring leaves in salads, sandwiches, soups
- Blanched or sautéed as a cooked green (similar to kale texture)
- Flower heads as fritters (dip in batter, pan-fry)
- Roots roasted and ground for coffee substitute
- Flower petals as garnish

**Medicinal/nutritional tradition (not medical advice):**
- Traditionally used as a mild diuretic and digestive aid (bitter compounds)
- High vitamin K content supports bone and blood health
- Inulin in roots (soluble fiber) promotes gut health when cooked

**Cautions:**
- Oxalates present (like spinach); not problematic for most, but excessive consumption may affect calcium absorption in susceptible individuals
- Latex sap can cause dermatitis in sensitive people; wash hands after harvesting
- Avoid harvesting from lawns treated with pesticides or herbicides

## Cautions

1. **Latex allergies:** A small percentage of people are allergic to the milky sap. If you develop skin irritation or itching after handling, discontinue use and wash thoroughly.

2. **Pesticide exposure:** Dandelion bioaccumulates easily. Harvest only from untreated areas (home gardens, wild fields, not roadsides or golf courses).

3. **Over-harvesting:** Entire leaves in an area can be stripped in spring. Leave at least 50% of plants unharvested so they can restore themselves.

4. **Universal Edibility Test:** Dandelion does not require it due to unmistakable ID, but the test is a last-resort only (and useless for fungi).

5. **Diuretic effect:** Traditional use as a diuretic is mild. If on pharmaceutical diuretics, avoid large quantities without medical consultation.
