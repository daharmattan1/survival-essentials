## ⚠ POSITIVE-ID SAFETY GATE

> ⚠ **DANDELION: milky sap does not identify it. Look-alikes include a spurge that blisters the mouth, a groundsel that can harm the liver and arnica.**
>
> **Positive-ID checklist (ALL must hold):**
> - All leaves grow from the base in a rosette. Leaves are deeply toothed or lobed, with the teeth pointing backward toward the center, and **hairless** [1].
> - The flower stem is **hollow, unbranched and leafless**, and carries **one** head [1].
> - The head is bright yellow, with more than 20 toothed ray petals (no separate center disk) [1].
> - There is a deep taproot [1]. After flowering the head turns into a round, fluffy puffball [1].
> - A milky sap seeps from broken stems. This fits dandelion but proves nothing by itself [1][2][3][6].
>
> **Do NOT use if ANY of these are present (disqualifying traits):**
> - **Leaves on the flower stem, or a branched flower stem.** That fits catsear [2], sow thistle [4] and chicory [3], not dandelion.
> - **Hairy leaves** [2][5]. Dandelion leaves are hairless [1].
> - Opposite leaves, a low mat on sidewalk cracks or gravel, or a maroon spot on the leaf: that is spotted spurge [6].
> - Blue flowers [3] or a tall plant (chicory is 3 to 4 feet) [3].
> - Spiny leaf teeth [4].
> - Opposite leaves up a hairy stem with a single flower head (arnica) [11], or small petal-less heads in clusters on a branched, leafy stem with deeply lobed alternate leaves (common groundsel) [10].
> - Any doubt, or the ground may have been sprayed.
>
> **Confusable with:** Catsear, chicory, sow thistle, hawkweeds, common groundsel, arnica and spotted spurge → see below → most are edible or mildly irritating, but groundsel and arnica are poisonous and spurge sap blisters the mouth and can damage an eye [6][10][11].
>
> **Verdict:** Positive ID on every checklist item, or do not consume. No *deadly* look-alike is named by the sources opened, but two poisonous ones are (common groundsel, arnica; arnica is rated high severity, with coma reported in children), and missing data is not a promise. Field identification alone is not enough here: have someone who knows plants confirm it first (this entry's rule).

## Deadly / Toxic Look-Alikes

NC State lists these as often confused with dandelion: sow thistle (*Sonchus*), chicory (*Cichorium intybus*), catsear (*Hypochaeris radicata*), hawkweed (*Pilosella*), dwarf dandelion (*Krigia*), Oriental false hawksbeard (*Youngia japonica*), fireweed (*Erechtites*), common groundsel (*Senecio vulgaris*) and arnica (*Arnica montana*) [1]. Pages were opened for all of these except dwarf dandelion, Oriental false hawksbeard and fireweed, so for those the rule is: any plant with a branched stem, leaves up the stem, or no true rosette is not dandelion.

- **Catsear (*Hypochaeris radicata*).** Hairy basal leaves, flower stalks typically branched with a few alternate leaves, pale yellow heads with 20 to 30 rays, blooms mid-summer to early fall, milky latex [2]. NC State says its leaves can be eaten; it is a problem for horses (stringhalt), not listed as a human hazard [2]. The discriminators are hairy leaves and a branched stalk.
- **Chicory (*Cichorium intybus*).** Its first-year rosette leaves "resemble dandelion leaves at the base." Later it is a 3 to 4 foot plant with alternate leaves up the stem and blue flowers; milky sap in the root [3]. NC State lists leaves, roots and flowers as edible [3].
- **Sow thistle (*Sonchus asper*, prickly sowthistle).** Spiny-toothed leaves clasping the stem, stems branched above, clusters of 1 to 5 yellow heads on short stalks [4].
- **Mouse-ear hawkweed (*Pilosella officinarum*).** Felty, silver-grey, woolly leaves in a low rosette with runners; heads are single or two or three; no leaves on the stem [5].
- ☠ **Common groundsel (*Senecio vulgaris*).** NC State rates it a medium-severity poison for people: flowers, leaves, roots and stems are toxic, with "chronically delayed toxicity" that can include lethargy, vomiting and signs of liver failure [10]. Leaves are alternate, deeply lobed and irregularly toothed; the small dandelion-like heads have no petals and grow in clusters on hollow, fleshy stems [10]. A branched or leafy stem fails the checklist.
- ☠ **Arnica (*Arnica montana*).** NC State rates it high severity; the toxin is helenalin and its esters in flowers and roots, and ingestion can cause heart palpitations, vomiting, drowsiness, shortness of breath, and coma in children [11]. The stem is hairy and carries a single flower, the stem leaves are opposite, and the rays surround a yellow disc [11]. Opposite leaves up the stem fail the checklist.
- ☠ **Spotted spurge (*Euphorbia maculata*).** Not a rosette plant, but its sap is "similar to dandelion's" [6]. A low mat on sidewalk cracks, gravel and gardens; small opposite leaves, often with a maroon-to-purple spot [6]. The sap can cause mild to severe skin rash; in an eye it can damage the cornea; if eaten, blistering of the lips and mouth can follow [6]. **The sap check cannot clear a plant.** Opposite leaves, a mat form or a leaf spot means stop.

## Identification

Dandelion is a perennial weed from Eurasia [1]. Leaves: 3 to 6 inches long, oblanceolate, with backward-pointing teeth, hairless, in a basal rosette [1]. Flowers: bright yellow ray heads, 1 to 3 inches across, closing in the evening [1]. Stems: hollow, leafless, unbranched, with milky sap [1]. Fruit: a round puffball of wind-blown seeds [1]. It forms a deep taproot [1]. NC State's descriptions do not give a stem height or a root depth in inches, so none is stated here.

## Habitat & Range (this biome)

NC State places dandelion in full sun: lawns and naturalized areas [1]. The pages opened give no Mid-Atlantic month window beyond a spring bloom time, so this entry gives no firmer dates. Spotted spurge is found in warm weather and dies at frost [6], so the spurge mix-up is a summer problem; chicory flowers from mid-summer to frost [3]; catsear blooms mid-summer to early fall [2].

## Uses & Nutrition

Preparation is not a safety step for dandelion, so there is no ⚠ MANDATORY PREPARATION block. NC State says young leaves are best raw and older leaves are better cooked; the flowers go in salads, stir-fries, jams, wines and teas; the roots can be roasted as a coffee substitute [1]. No nutrition numbers or cooking times were verified for this entry, so none are given.

**Sap.** The milky sap can irritate skin. NC State rates dandelion a low-severity poison for this reason (taraxinic acid; skin irritation after sap exposure) [1]. Wash your hands after picking.

**Sprayed ground.** Do not pick from lawns or roadsides that may have been treated with herbicide or pesticide (general practice).

## Cautions

1. **If you or someone else ate a plant you cannot name:** call Poison Help at **1-800-222-1222**; call **911** for collapse, seizure, trouble breathing or if the person cannot be awakened. Do not induce vomiting [7]. Sap in the eye: rinse with room-temperature water for at least 15 to 20 minutes, then call [7]. Sap on skin: rinse for at least 15 minutes [7]. Keep a sample of the plant. This is commonly taught first aid, not medical advice.
2. **Do not use the Universal Edibility Test or a taste test to confirm dandelion.** Spurge sap blisters the lips and mouth [6].
3. **Law.** Plants in Shenandoah National Park are protected and may not be collected, apart from small amounts of fruit, nuts and edible mushrooms [8]. Virginia state parks bar taking plants, with edible fruits, berries, fungi and nuts as the exceptions [9]. Other land managers differ; check the rule. Not legal advice.
