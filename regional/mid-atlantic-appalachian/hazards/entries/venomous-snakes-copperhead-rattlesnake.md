## Identify

Treat every snake you cannot name from a distance as venomous. Do not walk up to a snake to check its head, eyes or pit. Maryland's wildlife agency lists a wide head, an elliptical pupil and a heat-sensing pit as pit viper traits, and warns that harmless snakes flatten their heads when threatened to mimic pit vipers [3]. Maryland also says pit vipers should not be approached or handled [3]. Head shape is not a reliable way to sort a snake. The marks below are for naming a snake from where you already are.

**Copperhead** (*Agkistrodon contortrix*). Virginia's wildlife agency describes a heavy-bodied snake, 24 to 36 inches long, with a coppery-red head and chestnut to dark brown crossbands shaped like hourglasses [5]. It lives across Virginia except the barrier islands, in wetlands, forests, fields and edges, and likes rock, brush piles, stone walls and old buildings [5]. CDC lists copperheads in the eastern states in forests, rocky areas, swamps and near water [2].

**Timber rattlesnake** (*Crotalus horridus*). Color varies a great deal, but most have dark brown to black blotches and crossbands, with a rattle made of loose segments at the tail tip. Young snakes are born with only a single segment, the button [6]. Average length is 30 to 60 inches [6]. In Virginia the Virginia Herpetological Society describes populations in the western mountains and separate ones in the southeast, in upland hardwood forest with ledges or talus in the west [6]. Maryland lists the copperhead and timber rattlesnake as its two medically significant venomous snakes [3].

**Also in this region:**
- **Cottonmouth** (*Agkistrodon piscivorus*). Virginia's wildlife agency says none have been found north or west of Colonial Heights, and most known populations are south of the James River, so this is a far southeastern Virginia snake of swamps, marshes, ponds and ditches [7]. The harmless northern watersnake is often mistaken for it [7]. UVA Health also lists three venomous snakes for Virginia: cottonmouth, copperhead and timber rattlesnake [4].
- **Eastern massasauga** (*Sistrurus catenatus*). Pennsylvania's Fish and Boat Commission says it is known at only four sites, in Butler, Venango and Mercer counties in western Pennsylvania, and lists it as an endangered species there [8]. It is rare, and the Pennsylvania plan is the only source opened for it.

**Season.** CDC says snakes are most active at dawn, dusk and in warm weather [2].

## Avoid

- **Watch where you put your hands and feet** when you turn logs, move firewood or step over rock piles. CDC advises avoiding tall grass and leaf piles, and not climbing on rocks or wood piles where snakes may hide [2].
- **Leave snakes alone.** Never pick up or try to trap one. Maryland says never handle a venomous snake, alive or dead [3]. UVA Health says trying to kill a snake can get you bitten again [4].
- **Give the snake room** to leave. Do not corner it.
- **Wear boots and long pants** in tall grass, brush and leaf litter (general practice).
- **Do not count on hearing a warning.** No source opened for this entry says a rattlesnake will rattle before it bites, so a quiet snake can still bite. Young rattlesnakes have only a button [6].

## If Exposed / Bitten / Stung

CDC's first-aid list for a snakebite [1]:

1. **Get emergency medical care as soon as possible.** Antivenom, if needed, is started at a hospital, and care stops damage that cannot be undone [1]. **Do not wait for symptoms to appear** [1].
2. **Call 911, or have someone drive you. Do not drive yourself.** A bite can make you dizzy or make you pass out [1]. Poison Help, 1-800-222-1222, is also an option and Maryland tells people to call it right after a bite [3].
3. **Keep calm. Lie or sit down with the bite in a neutral, comfortable position** [1].
4. **Take off rings and watches before swelling starts** [1].
5. **Wash the bite with soap and water. Cover it with a clean, dry dressing** [1].
6. **Mark the edge of the swelling or tenderness on the skin and write the time next to it** [1].
7. **Photograph the snake only if you can do it safely from a distance** [1]. Do not hunt for it, pick it up or try to catch it, not even a dead one or its head [1].

**What not to do** [1][3][4]:
- No tourniquet. A tourniquet is right for severe bleeding and wrong for a snakebite. This guide's bleeding-control entry applies to bleeding only.
- Do not cut the wound or try to suck out venom.
- Do not apply ice or put the bite in water.
- No electric shock and no folk remedies.
- No alcohol as a painkiller. No aspirin, ibuprofen or naproxen [1].

**How serious it is.** CDC says antivenom is recommended when a rattlesnake bite shows progressive effects, such as worsening local tissue injury or whole-body symptoms [1]. For copperhead bites, CDC says antivenom as soon as possible helps the limb recover faster and lowers the chance of a disabled limb [1]. A copperhead bite is not minor. UVA Health says doctors use the same antivenom for all North American snakes except the coral snake, which does not live in Virginia [4]. Doses are for the hospital team and are not given here.

## ⚠ WHEN IT'S AN EMERGENCY

**Every snakebite from a venomous or unidentified snake is an emergency.** Call 911 or have someone take you to an emergency department right away [1][4].

- Do not wait to see whether symptoms develop [1].
- Do not treat it at home. UVA Health says there is nothing you can do at home to limit the damage from venom [4].
- Do not drive yourself [1].
- CDC lists the signs that can follow: swelling, bruising, nausea, trouble breathing, numbness and a weak pulse [1]. Any of these makes the call more urgent. Their absence does not make the bite harmless.
