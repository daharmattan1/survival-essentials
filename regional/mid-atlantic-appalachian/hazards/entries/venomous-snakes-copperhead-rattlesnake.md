## Identify

**Copperhead** (*Agkistrodon contortrix*): hourglass-shaped bands on coppery body, triangular head (pit viper feature), heat-sensing pit between nostril and eye. 20–36 inches, stocky build.

**Timber rattlesnake** (*Crotalus horridus*): dark diamonds or chevrons on lighter body, prominent rattle at tail tip (the unmistakable diagnostic), triangular head with pit. 36–60 inches, more robust than copperhead.

Both are **pit vipers** — the heat-sensing pit between eye and nostril is the signature anatomical marker separating venomous snakes from non-venomous colubrids in this biome.

**Where/when in this biome:** Rocky slopes, wooded hillsides, leaf litter, woodpiles, stone walls, and stream edges. Active warm months (May–September peak), dawn and dusk hunting times. Copperhead is more common in lowlands/wooded areas. Timber rattlesnake frequents rocky outcrops and ridgelines.

## Avoid

- **Watch hands and feet** when turning logs, moving firewood, or stepping over rocky debris.
- **Stay alert at dawn/dusk** in warm months — active feeding times.
- **Never corner a snake or attempt to move it by hand.** Give it an escape route; snakes are not aggressive and will leave if given the chance.
- **Wear boots and long pants** when moving through tall grass, brush, or leaf litter during warm months.
- **Avoid reaching blindly** into rock crevices, firewood stacks, or under boards.
- **Listen for a rattle** — timber rattlesnakes will often rattle as a warning. Stop, step back slowly, and let it retreat.

## If Exposed / Bitten / Stung

**Immediate response (field):**
1. **Stay calm and still.** Panic increases heart rate and venom spread. Sit or lie down.
2. **Immobilize the bitten limb** — keep it level with (or slightly below) the heart. If bitten on the foot/lower leg, don't walk; if bitten on the hand/arm, use a sling.
3. **Remove jewelry, bracelets, rings** if on the limb — swelling will happen and these will constrict.
4. **Do NOT apply a tourniquet.** (Myth: tourniquets are dangerous and do not help with pit viper envenomation; they increase local tissue damage.)
5. **Do NOT cut the bite or attempt to suck venom out.** (Myth: these make tissue damage worse.)
6. **Do NOT apply ice directly to the skin.** (Myth: ice causes frostbite and tissue damage; it does not slow venom spread.)
7. **Seek urgent care immediately.** This is a medical emergency even if the bite feels minor at first. Antivenom (CroFab or Anavip) is the standard treatment and must be given in a hospital.

**Copperhead bite:** Usually less severe than rattlesnake; may cause local swelling, discoloration, pain over hours. Still requires antivenom evaluation.

**Timber rattlesnake bite:** Higher venom load; can cause systemic symptoms (coagulopathy, hypotension, shock). Requires immediate antivenom administration.

## ⚠ WHEN IT'S AN EMERGENCY

**Seek urgent care (call 911 or get to an ER) immediately if:**
- Any snakebite has occurred, even if you think it was a dry bite (no venom injected). Assume envenomation and get to an ER for observation and antivenom if needed.
- Swelling progresses beyond the bite site (moving up the limb).
- Systemic symptoms appear: dizziness, nausea, coagulopathy (bleeding, bruising), numbness, or difficulty breathing.
- The bite is on the face, neck, or hand — higher-risk locations.

**Do not wait to see if symptoms develop; do not treat this at home.** Pit viper envenomation can be lethal without antivenom, and even "minor" bites can progress. Get to a hospital for evaluation within 2–4 hours of the bite.
