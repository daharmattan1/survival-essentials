## ⚠ POSITIVE-ID SAFETY GATE

> ⚠ **AMATOXIN MUSHROOMS: hours of nothing, then liver and kidney failure**
>
> **Treat a mushroom as an amatoxin suspect if ANY of these hold:**
> - White gills, plus a ring on the stem, plus a sac or cup at the base (the base is often buried, so dig the whole mushroom up) [7][10]
> - A white mushroom, round "egg" or "puffball" shape. A young *Amanita* button can resemble a small puffball [9]. The cut test is in [wood ear and puffball](../../mushrooms/entries/wood-ear-or-puffball.md)
> - A small mushroom in lawns or mulch. Some *Lepiota* and *Conocybe filaris* carry amatoxins [6]
> - A small brown mushroom on rotting wood with a ring or ring zone on the stem and a rusty-brown spore print [13][14]
>
> **Do NOT eat a wild mushroom if ANY of these are present:**
> - It could be a white-gilled mushroom with a ring or a volva
> - It could be a small brown wood mushroom you cannot rule out with a spore print
> - It is decayed, or was bagged with other mushrooms
> - Anyone has said "it looks like" something edible. Look-alikes are how people are poisoned [1]
>
> **Confusable with:** paddy-straw mushrooms and puffballs (both named by Idaho's health agency for death cap [12]; StatPearls also names paddy-straw mushrooms [5]), white button mushrooms and meadow mushrooms (for white *Amanita*); honey mushroom and velvet foot (for *Galerina*) → the discriminators below are necessary but never sufficient → delayed liver and kidney failure that can be fatal (sources differ on how often; see the mortality note below).
>
> **Verdict:** Positive ID by an expert, or do not consume. This hazard must never be eaten on field identification alone. If anyone may have eaten one, call 1-800-222-1222 now. Do not wait for symptoms.

This entry covers what sources say about the amatoxin mushrooms that grow, or have been reported, in the Mid-Atlantic and Appalachians. It is not medical advice. It is commonly-taught guidance for when professional care is far away.

## Identify

### The pattern to know

Amatoxins are in a small group of mushrooms. *Amanita* species account for 90 percent of mushroom-related deaths, per the Missouri Department of Conservation [9]. A USDA Forest Service field guide gives the *Amanita* signature as a ring on the stalk, a cup (volva) at the base that is often within the soil, white gills free from the stalk, and a white spore print. It says to dig mushrooms up rather than pick them, so the cup is not missed [7]. Cornell mycologists add the same four traits and say to use them together, not one at a time [10].

### Destroying angels (*Amanita bisporigera* and relatives)

- **Look:** all white, a skirtlike ring on the upper stalk, and a large saclike cup around the base, sometimes hidden underground. White, close gills. White spore print. Grows on the ground in mixed woods and in grass near trees, June through November [8].
- **Range:** *A. bisporigera* is described as widely distributed and common in eastern North America, living with oaks and possibly other hardwoods in summer and fall [17].
- **Names:** field guides list *A. virosa*, *A. verna* and *A. bisporigera* together as "destroying angel." The USDA guide says they can be told apart only by their spores [7]. MushroomExpert says *A. virosa* and *A. verna* are European species [17]. For eating decisions none of this matters. Treat every white *Amanita* as deadly.
- **Button stage:** a young *Amanita* starts as an egg-shaped button that can resemble a small puffball [9]. Cornell notes a button can also pass for an edible, brown-spored white button mushroom, and that the white spore print is the key difference [10]. The puffball cut test is in the [wood ear and puffball](../../mushrooms/entries/wood-ear-or-puffball.md) entry.
- **Meadow mushroom comparison:** the meadow mushroom has pink gills that turn brown, a brown spore print and no cup at the base [8].

### Death cap (*Amanita phalloides*)

- **Look:** CDC says the mature cap is usually metallic green but varies from light yellow to greenish-brown [4]. Like the other white-gilled *Amanita*: white gills, a skirtlike ring, a cup at the base and a white spore print [7][10][15][18]. A rare all-white form exists and resembles the eastern destroying angel [15]. The ring and cup details for death cap itself come from lower-tier sources only [15][18]. Do not rely on cap color.
- **Mistaken for:** Idaho's health agency says death cap can look like edible mushrooms such as paddy-straw mushrooms or puffballs [12]. StatPearls says death caps are often mistaken for the paddy straw mushroom [5]. NAMA notes that victims are disproportionately immigrants, though death caps and related species occur on every inhabited continent [6]. Do not treat a cup at the base as proof of anything. Treat it as a reason not to eat.
- **Where sources report it in the East:** it is a European species introduced to North America. A peer-reviewed study found two ranges, one on the West Coast and one on the East Coast "Maryland to Maine" [11]. Another source gives the eastern range as Pennsylvania and Virginia to Maine, and says it was introduced in the New Jersey area [18]. The sources opened for this entry give no West Virginia or southern Appalachian records. That is a gap in the sources, not proof of absence.
- **Dose:** an Idaho health agency says eating as little as half a cap can kill an adult [12].

### Deadly galerina (*Galerina marginata*)

- **Look:** a small brown mushroom. Cap dark brown to tawny ochre, sticky, fading lighter as it dries. Gills start yellowish and turn rusty. A ring on the upper stalk. Grows singly or in clusters on decaying logs, both hardwood and conifer [13].
- **Spore print:** rusty brown [13][14][16]. Volk describes a small, fragile ring [14], and MykoWeb says it is sometimes missing in age, but the ring zone may be coated with rusty spores [16]. A rusty print alone does not clear a ringed brown wood mushroom, because other brown-spored mushrooms grow on wood. That is why only an expert identification counts.
- **Season:** Missouri's description says year-round, but especially September to November. A photo caption on the same page says most common in spring and fall [13]. One university source finds it in every month without snow, on very well-rotted, often moss-covered logs [14].
- **Toxin:** the same α-amanitin found in destroying angels [14].
- **Look-alikes:** honey mushroom (*Armillaria*) and velvet foot (*Flammulina*) have white spore prints, unlike the rusty print of *Galerina* [13][14]. Velvet foot has no ring [13]. Species of *Psilocybe*, sometimes mistaken for it, have brown prints, so a brown print alone does not clear it [14].
- **Where else:** wood-growing mushrooms people seek, such as [oyster](../../mushrooms/entries/oyster-pleurotus.md) and [chicken-of-the-woods](../../mushrooms/entries/chicken-of-the-woods-laetiporus.md), fruit on the same kind of logs. Doctrine bars mixed collections for this reason ([mushroom doctrine](../../mushrooms/_index.md)).

### Other amatoxin mushrooms sources name

The North American Mycological Association (NAMA) says amatoxins also occur in some *Lepiota* (most notably *L. subincarnata*, also called *L. josserandii*) and in *Conocybe filaris* [6]. A medical reference names the same genera [5]. The principal North American *Amanita* researcher, Dr. Rodham Tulloss, told NAMA of four eastern North American incidents since August 2008 involving two undescribed *Amanita* species [6]. The honest summary: there are more amatoxin species than any one field list covers.

## Avoid

- Do not eat wild mushrooms that are white-gilled with a ring or a cup, or that are small, brown and growing on rotting wood, unless an expert has confirmed the exact species. This follows the [mushroom doctrine](../../mushrooms/_index.md).
- Poison Help says it is never wise to eat a wild mushroom unless an identification expert says so, because look-alikes make non-expert identification unreliable [1].
- Dig the whole mushroom, base included, when you must look at one. Look but do not taste [7][8].
- Cooking does not remove the danger [12].
- Keep dogs and livestock away. Pets and livestock are not immune [8]. For a pet that ate one, the Idaho agency lists the ASPCA Animal Poison Control Center at 888-426-4435 or a veterinarian [12].

## If Exposed / Bitten / Stung

Act on suspicion. The timing below is why.

1. **Call Poison Help, 1-800-222-1222, right away** if you think someone ate a wild mushroom [1]. Do not wait for symptoms. Poison centers work with mycologists to identify the mushroom [1].
2. **Do not make the person vomit** unless poison control or a clinician tells you to (general poison-exposure home care [19]).
3. **Save the evidence.** An intact mushroom, or a large vomited piece, can be kept in a paper bag in a refrigerator. Photograph the cap, the underside and the stem from several angles. Note what the person meant to pick and where it grew [2].
4. **Get medical care even if the person feels fine.** Early treatment is critical [1][2][12].
5. **Everyone who shared the meal** should call too (general practice). In one household cluster, symptoms began about 9 hours after the meal [3].
6. **There is no specific antidote.** Hospital care is supportive, and a poison center or toxicologist guides it [5][6]. Do not rely on supplements or home remedies. Treatment details and doses are for the hospital team and are not given here.

### Why waiting kills

Sources agree on the shape of the course and differ on exact hours:

- **Silent period.** No symptoms for at least 6 hours [2]. Windows given: 6 to 24 hours [3][4], 6 to 12 hours [5], 5 to 12 hours [10].
- **Stomach phase.** Vomiting, severe diarrhea, cramps, dehydration. It looks like food poisoning, and people may not connect it to the mushroom because of the delay [3][4].
- **The false recovery.** Symptoms ease, and the person seems to be getting better, while the liver and kidneys are deteriorating [4]. Sources put this at 24 to 36 hours [3], 24 to 48 hours after the first stage [4], or "two to three days" [5]. In CDC's California report, one patient was sent home from the emergency department during the stomach phase with a diagnosis of gastroenteritis and returned the next day with worse liver function [3].
- **Organ failure.** Liver and kidney failure typically 3 to 5 days after eating [3][4]. Death can occur in three to seven days [5], or four to nine days after eating in CDC's 1997 report [4]. Amatoxins halt protein synthesis in liver cells [3].

**How often it kills depends on the source.** Reported figures differ because they come from different places and patient groups:
- NAMA's review of over 2,000 cases from western Europe, the United States and Canada found an average mortality of 11.58 percent. NAMA also says the rate exceeds 50 percent in many non-western countries, and that with good hospital supportive care alone the majority (80 to 90 percent) of poisonings resolve [6].
- CDC (2016) describes a case fatality rate of 10 to 20 percent [3]. CDC (1997) gives 20 to 30 percent among treated patients [4].
- StatPearls says deaths occur in under 5 percent of cases in developed countries with early access to intensive care [5].

The pattern is the same in every source. Early hospital care helps, and delay raises the risk. Young children (under age 10) and seniors appear more likely to die [6]. Do not plan around the best figure.

## ⚠ WHEN IT'S AN EMERGENCY

- **Any suspected wild mushroom ingestion where the mushroom could be a white Amanita or a Galerina is an emergency from the first call, even with no symptoms.** Call Poison Help, 1-800-222-1222, and go to a hospital.
- **Call 911** (or get to an emergency room) if the person is unresponsive, has trouble breathing, or is having seizures [19].
- **Vomiting or diarrhea beginning late, or lasting beyond 6 hours after a mushroom meal,** should raise concern about a serious ingestion [2].
- **Jaundice, confusion, bleeding or drowsiness** days later are signs of liver failure [4][5]. Go to an emergency room.
- **Easing symptoms are not a reason to cancel the call or the visit.**
