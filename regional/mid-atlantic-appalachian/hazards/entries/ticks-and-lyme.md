## Identify

**Blacklegged tick (deer tick)** (*Ixodes scapularis*): The primary vector for *Borrelia burgdorferi* (the bacterium causing Lyme disease). Size varies by stage:
- **Larva:** barely visible, pinhead-sized.
- **Nymph:** sesame-seed-sized (this stage most commonly transmits Lyme because it is so small and often goes unnoticed).
- **Adult female:** small apple-seed to sesame-seed-sized, reddish-brown body, black legs (hence "blacklegged").
- **Adult male:** smaller, darker, sometimes missed because of size.

**Where/when in this biome:** Tall grass, brush, leaf litter, low vegetation, and wooded edges. Peak activity **spring–summer** (April–July for nymphs; year-round for adults in milder winters). Found in moist, shaded habitats. This biome (USDA 7a) is endemic for *Ixodes* ticks.

## Avoid

- **Tuck pants into socks or use gaiters** when walking through tall grass or brush during tick season.
- **Wear light-colored clothing** to spot ticks before they attach.
- **Use an EPA-approved tick repellent** (0.5% permethrin on clothing; 20% DEET or picaridin on exposed skin — read label for application).
- **Stick to cleared trails** and avoid brushing against vegetation.
- **Perform daily tick checks** — inspect your body, hair, armpits, groin, and behind knees. Ticks need several hours to attach; early removal prevents disease transmission.
- **Check clothing and pets** after time outdoors; wash clothing in hot water.

## If Exposed / Bitten / Stung

**Tick removal (the critical first step):**

1. **Use fine-tipped tweezers** — grasp the tick as close to the skin as possible, at the mouthpart (not the body).
2. **Pull straight outward** with steady pressure — do not twist or jerk. The goal is to remove the entire tick without crushing the body or leaving the mouthpart in the skin.
3. **Do NOT burn the tick, apply nail polish, oil, or use a match** — these do not cause the tick to release and can cause it to regurgitate infectious material into the bite.
4. **Do NOT squish the tick** — crushing a possibly-infected tick can spread bacteria to your hands or the wound.
5. **After removal, disinfect the bite** with soap and water or rubbing alcohol. Wash your hands.
6. **Save the tick in a sealed plastic bag or vial** — if you develop symptoms, it can be tested to confirm if it was an *Ixodes* tick carrying *Borrelia*.

**What to watch for (Lyme disease signs):**
- **Erythema migrans (EM rash):** A red, expanding rash often in a "bull's-eye" pattern, typically appearing 3–30 days after a tick bite (often at the bite site, but can appear elsewhere).
- **Fever, chills, malaise, joint pain, muscle aches** in the days to weeks after a bite.
- **Early Lyme disease** (treated with antibiotics like doxycycline) is curable; untreated Lyme can progress to arthritis, neurological symptoms, and carditis.

## ⚠ WHEN IT'S AN EMERGENCY

**Seek medical care if:**
- An expanding red rash (especially bull's-eye pattern) appears after a tick bite.
- Fever, chills, and joint/muscle pain develop in the days following a known tick exposure or bite.
- Neurological symptoms appear: severe headache, stiff neck, or facial droop (possible Lyme neuroborreliosis).
- Chest pain or irregular heartbeat develops (rare; Lyme carditis).

**Early treatment is key:** Lyme disease caught early (within the first few weeks) is treated with a course of oral antibiotics (doxycycline, amoxicillin) and is typically curative. Delay increases the risk of systemic complications.
