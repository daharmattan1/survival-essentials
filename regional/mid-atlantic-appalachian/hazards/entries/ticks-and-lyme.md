## Identify

Three ticks account for most bites in this region. Virginia's health department says lone star ticks are the most common tick to bite people in Virginia [6]. The other two are the blacklegged (deer) tick and the American dog tick [7]. The Gulf Coast tick is found more commonly along Virginia's coastline, can deliver a painful bite, and carries *Rickettsia parkeri*, a spotted fever germ [7].

| Tick | How to tell it | What it can pass on, per Virginia's health department [6] |
|------|----------------|-----------------------------------------------------------|
| **Blacklegged tick** (deer tick) | Adult males are dark brown or black and resemble a small watermelon seed. Females are red-brown behind a black shield. Nymphs are less than 1/16 inch long [7]. | Lyme disease, anaplasmosis, babesiosis, Powassan virus; also ehrlichiosis |
| **Lone star tick** | The adult female has a white dot on the center of her back [7]. | Alpha-gal syndrome (a serious meat allergy), ehrlichiosis, Heartland virus; Bourbon virus is suspected |
| **American dog tick** | Short, rounded mouthparts. Common across Virginia in sunny, open areas [7]. | Rocky Mountain spotted fever (the brown dog tick is also listed) |

**Which stage bites most.** The blacklegged nymph most often spreads disease to people and is most active in late spring and early summer [7]. Adult female and nymph lone star ticks spread disease to people more often than males [7].

**Where and when.** Blacklegged ticks are most common in shady forest where deer are common [7]. CDC says to stay in the center of trails, away from high grass and leaf litter [5]. Virginia's health department says ticks can be active on any warm day, in any month [8].

## Avoid

- **Walk in the center of trails** and avoid high grass and leaf litter [5].
- **Use an EPA-registered repellent** on skin and follow the label. Treat clothing and gear with 0.5% permethrin [5]. Permethrin is for clothing and gear, not skin.
- **Shower within two hours of coming indoors.** CDC says this reduces Lyme risk and may help with other tick-borne diseases [5].
- **Tumble dry clothes on high heat for 10 minutes** to kill ticks. Wash them in hot water first if they need it [5].
- **Do a full-body check** when you come in, using a mirror for hard-to-see places. Check pets, coats and daypacks too [5].
- **Remove an attached tick as soon as you find it.** Some viruses move fast. Virginia's health department says Powassan virus can be passed in as little as 15 minutes [6]. Prompt removal lowers the risk. It does not remove it.

## If Exposed / Bitten / Stung

**Remove the tick now, at home** [1]:

1. Use clean, fine-tipped tweezers and grasp the tick as close to the skin as possible. CDC says regular tweezers or fingers also work [1].
2. Pull straight away from the skin with steady, even pressure. Do not twist or jerk the tick [1].
3. If mouthparts break off, the skin usually pushes them out as it heals. If they are hard to grab with tweezers, leave them alone [1].
4. Do not use petroleum jelly, heat, nail polish or similar. CDC says these can agitate the tick and push infected fluid into your skin. Do not delay removal to wait for a clinician [1].
5. Clean the bite and your hands with soap and water, rubbing alcohol or hand sanitizer [1].
6. Check the rest of your body for other ticks [1].

**What to do with the tick.** Seal it in a container, wrap it tightly in tape, flush it or drop it in alcohol. Do not crush it with your fingers [1]. **Testing the tick is not recommended.** CDC says testing a removed tick is generally not recommended, and the results should not guide treatment. A positive result does not confirm infection, and a negative result can give false reassurance [1]. If you want to show a clinician what bit you, take a photo before you dispose of it. Write down the date and where you probably picked it up [1].

**Watch for symptoms for several weeks.** CDC says to see a doctor if a rash or fever develops within several weeks of removing a tick, and to tell the doctor about the bite, when it happened and where you likely picked it up [1].

**Lyme disease signs** [3]:
- The expanding rash (erythema migrans) usually shows up 3 to 30 days after a bite, often around day 7, in roughly 70 to 80 percent of infected people. It can grow very large. **It does not always look like a "classic bull's-eye," so do not wait for a target** [3].
- Fever, chills, headache, fatigue, muscle and joint aches and swollen lymph nodes can occur with no rash at all [3].
- Later, days to months after a bite: severe headache with stiff neck, nerve pain, numbness or tingling, facial droop, heart palpitations or an irregular heartbeat, and joint swelling, especially in the knees [3].

**Other tick-borne illness in this region.** Many of them share symptoms, mainly fever, chills, aches and rash [2]. Virginia's health department lists these [6]:

| Illness | Tick | Signs it lists |
|---------|------|----------------|
| Anaplasmosis | Blacklegged | Fever, headache, body aches, nausea, vomiting; usually begins within 1 to 2 weeks |
| Babesiosis | Blacklegged | Fever, chills, sweats, headache, body aches, nausea |
| Ehrlichiosis | Lone star, blacklegged | Fever and other symptoms about 1 to 2 weeks after the bite |
| Rocky Mountain spotted fever | American dog tick | Sudden moderate to high fever, severe headache, fatigue, deep muscle pain, chills and rash |
| Powassan virus | Blacklegged | Fever, headache, vomiting, weakness, confusion, loss of coordination, speech trouble or seizures; 1 week to 1 month after the bite |
| Alpha-gal syndrome | Lone star | Allergy to certain meats: hives, swelling, stomach upset and diarrhea. Virginia's health department calls it a serious, potentially life-threatening allergy |

**Tick paralysis** is thought to come from a toxin in the saliva of an attached tick. CDC says weakness or paralysis can move gradually up the body, may resemble other nerve conditions such as Guillain-Barre syndrome or botulism, and people typically regain movement within 24 hours of removing the tick [2]. If you feel weakness creeping up while a tick is attached, remove the tick and get medical care now (this guide's rule, because it can look like other nerve conditions [2]).

**Treatment.** Lyme disease treated early with antibiotics usually ends in a rapid and complete recovery. CDC names doxycycline, amoxicillin or cefuroxime axetil as the usual drugs, with the choice and length set by a clinician [4]. CDC also says a clinician may offer a preventive antibiotic after a bite in an area where Lyme is common. Ask a clinician promptly after a bite whether that applies to you [4]. Do not self-treat. Doses are not given here.

## ⚠ WHEN IT'S AN EMERGENCY

**Get medical care promptly. Say you had a tick bite.** Do not wait for a bull's-eye rash [3].

- A fever, severe headache or rash within several weeks of a tick bite [1][6].
- Confusion, loss of coordination, speech trouble or seizures (Powassan virus signs listed by Virginia's health department) [6].
- A sudden high fever with severe headache and rash (signs of Rocky Mountain spotted fever) [6].
- Severe headache with a stiff neck, facial droop, or weakness or numbness [3].
- Heart palpitations or an irregular heartbeat [3].
- Weakness or paralysis creeping upward while a tick is attached: remove the tick and get medical care now [2].
- Hives, swelling, vomiting or trouble breathing after eating meat (possible alpha-gal reaction) [6]. Call 911 for trouble breathing. See [anaphylaxis and allergic reaction](../../../../substrate/06_first_aid/entries/anaphylaxis-and-allergic-reaction.md).
