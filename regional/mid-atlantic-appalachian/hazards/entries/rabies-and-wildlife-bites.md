This entry is commonly-taught guidance for when professional care is far away. It is not medical advice. Rabies treatment is decided by a clinician and your health department.

## Identify

### What rabies is

Rabies is a viral disease of the nervous system, spread mostly by the saliva of an infected animal, usually through a bite [6]. Saliva or brain tissue from a rabid animal that gets into a scratch, a fresh open wound, the eyes, the nose or the mouth can also count as an exposure [3][6][8]. Any mammal can get it [4][6].

The most important fact in this entry comes from Pennsylvania's health department: rabies is always fatal without post-exposure care, and by the time symptoms appear, virtually all cases are fatal. If post-exposure care is given appropriately after the bite of a rabid animal, the risk of infection is eliminated [6]. CDC says that after a possible exposure you must get medical help right away [1]. The care works only if it is started in time.

### Which animals, in this region

CDC reports that wild animals account for more than 90 percent of reported animal rabies cases, with bats (35 percent), raccoons (29 percent), skunks (17 percent) and foxes (8 percent) most often exposing Americans to rabies [2]. What CDC says about each:

- **Raccoons:** a rabies reservoir in the eastern United States from Canada to Florida, as far west as the Appalachian Mountain range [2]. That is this region.
- **Bats:** rabid bats are found in all U.S. states except Hawaii [2]. CDC's bats page says any contact with bats should be reported to the health department [3].
- **Skunks:** CDC describes the skunk reservoir as most states in the Midwest and West [2].
- **Foxes:** CDC lists fox reservoirs in the Southwest (gray foxes) and Alaska (arctic foxes) [2].

State health agencies in this region list them anyway, and they are the better guide for local risk:

- **Virginia:** rabies is commonly found in wildlife such as raccoons, skunks and foxes [4].
- **Maryland:** most often found in raccoons, skunks, foxes, cats, bats and groundhogs [5].
- **Pennsylvania:** the animals most likely to transmit rabies include raccoons, bats, skunks, foxes and cats. About half of confirmed animal cases are raccoons, followed by skunks, cats, bats and foxes. Cats are the most commonly reported domestic animal with rabies [6].
- **West Virginia:** raccoons are the primary reservoir. In the state's surveillance, raccoons were 60 percent of documented rabid animals and skunks 18 percent [8]. The state's list of vector species also includes coyotes and bobcats [8].

USDA Wildlife Services drops oral rabies vaccine baits for wildlife, mostly raccoons. It reports that this program has created a barrier against the westward spread of raccoon rabies beyond the Appalachian Mountains [10]. The barrier lowers risk. It does not remove it.

### What a rabid animal may do

Unusual tameness or aggression, daytime activity in animals that are normally nocturnal, drooling or excessive salivation, and stumbling or trembling [9]. Virginia warns that a rabid animal sometimes acts tame [4]. Pennsylvania's Game Commission lists excitability, apparent lack of fear or aggression, and adds that infected animals sometimes appear normal [7]. You cannot rule rabies out by watching an animal.

## Avoid

- **Keep your distance from wildlife.** CDC says that is the best way to avoid rabies in the United States. Never approach an animal that appears injured, sick or dead [1].
- **Do not touch dead animals found in the wild** [9]. Do not feed wildlife. Do not keep wild animals as pets [6].
- **Avoid wild animals that behave abnormally** [7]. Call animal control for assistance [1][4].
- **Do not try to capture, kill or carry a wild animal** (general practice; CDC says to call animal control instead of approaching it [1]). Report it. The health department decides whether an animal should be observed or tested [9].
- **Keep pets vaccinated.** Dogs, cats and ferrets should be vaccinated by a veterinarian and kept current [1][4][5][6]. Keep pets away from wild animals as much as possible [1]. After a wildlife encounter, Virginia says a pet may need a booster, so call your veterinarian [4].
- **Do not handle bats with bare hands** (see below) [3].

## If Exposed / Bitten / Stung

### A bite or scratch from a wild mammal

1. **Wash the wound with soap and water for 15 minutes** to flush out virus [1]. Maryland adds a disinfectant to flush the wound if you have one [5]. Pennsylvania says plenty of soap and warm water [6].
2. **Get medical help right away.** CDC: you must get medical help right away [1], and for a bat bite, wash the wound and get medical help right away [3]. This guide's own rule is to make the call the same day at the latest. A clinician decides whether post-exposure care is needed [8][9]. Do not wait for symptoms [6].
3. **Contact your health department immediately** after exposure to an animal that might have rabies [1]. Maryland says to report the exposure to local animal control, health department or police [5]. Virginia says to report a bite to your local health department [4]. West Virginia requires animal bites and potential exposures to be reported to the local health department within 24 hours [8]. That is a reporting rule, not a care deadline. Get care right away.
4. **Tell the clinician what animal it was,** where, and what happened.
   - **A bite or scratch from a stray or unfamiliar cat, dog or ferret** gets the same steps: wash for 15 minutes, then get medical help right away. At the very latest, call a clinician or the health department the same day (this guide's rule) [1][6][8]. Cats are the most commonly reported domestic animal with rabies in Pennsylvania [6]. CDC's advisory committee says a healthy dog, cat or ferret that bites a person is confined and observed for 10 days by the authorities, and that this decision belongs to the health department [11].
5. **If saliva or brain material reaches your eyes, nose, mouth or an open wound,** treat it as an exposure and get medical help right away [1][6]. Rinse with water first (general practice).
6. **Post-exposure care exists and works if started in time.** It is a series of vaccine doses plus immune globulin given under medical direction. Your clinician sets the plan [6][8]. This guide gives no schedule. Do not try to manage it yourself.
7. **Do not give up on a late call.** Pennsylvania says it is never too late to seek medical attention for a potential exposure [6]. Incubation is usually weeks to months, but sources differ and it can be days or years [6][8].

### Bats in the bedroom

Bat teeth are small, bites can be tiny, and you may not know you were bitten [3][5][8]. West Virginia's protocol lists a person sleeping in a room with a bat, and a bat found in the room with an unattended child, a person with a mental disability or an intoxicated person, as possible exposures [8]. Maryland says to consider treatment when exposure cannot be ruled out, such as a sleeping person who wakes to find a bat in the room [5]. CDC's advisory committee lists the same situations, and says they are not exposures if rabies is ruled out by testing the bat. If the bat is available for testing and is negative, post-exposure care is not necessary [11]. That is why the bat should be kept for testing if it can be contained safely [3][5].

- **Do not release the bat** until you talk with a public health expert [3].
- **If you can capture it without bare hands:** CDC says put on leather work gloves, contain the bat in a box or jar covered with cardboard with air holes, and contact your health department so the bat can be tested [3].
- **If you cannot capture it,** call animal control or the health department for advice [3].
- **Call a clinician or the health department right away, and at the very latest the same day (this guide's rule),** even if you see no mark. Bats found in a home should be collected if possible and tested [5].

### Other bite care (not rabies)

Any animal bite can become infected. For cleaning, infection signs and when to get care, see [wound infection management](../../../../substrate/06_first_aid/entries/wound-infection-management.md). Rabies is the reason to call a clinician about a wild-animal bite even when the wound looks small.

## ⚠ WHEN IT'S AN EMERGENCY

- **Treat any bite, scratch or saliva exposure from a bat, raccoon, skunk, fox or other wild mammal as urgent.** Wash for 15 minutes, then get medical help right away [1]. At the very latest, call a clinician or your health department the same day (this guide's rule).
- **A bat in the room with someone who was asleep, an unattended child, or an intoxicated or impaired person** is a possible exposure even with no visible bite [5][8][11]. Call right away; at the very latest the same day (this guide's rule). A negative test on the bat ends the question [11].
- **Do not wait for symptoms.** Once symptoms appear, rabies is almost always fatal [6][8]. Early symptoms resemble flu, with discomfort at the bite site [8].
- **Call 911** for severe bleeding, a face or head bite, an animal that is still attacking, or a person with confusion or breathing trouble (general practice).
- **Call animal control or the health department** about an animal that is acting strangely, is out in daytime when it should not be, or bit a person or pet [1][4][9].
