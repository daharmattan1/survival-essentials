## ⚠ POSITIVE-ID SAFETY GATE

> ⚠ **WATER HEMLOCK: a piece of root can kill an adult within hours**
>
> **This list can help you recognize a suspect. It can never clear a plant.**
>
> **Treat a plant as water hemlock if ALL of these hold:**
> - A white umbrella-shaped flower cluster (umbel) on a tall, hairless, hollow stem, often purple-streaked or spotted [5][6][8]
> - Leaves divided two or three times into toothed leaflets [5], with the side veins running to the notches between the teeth [1][4]. The Forest Service describes veins that fork, one branch to the leaflet tip and one to the notch [3]. Sources word this differently, so do not use it to clear a plant
> - Growing in wet ground: ditches, stream banks, pond edges, marshes [3][4]
>
> **Do NOT dig, taste or eat any wild member of the carrot family if ANY of these are present:**
> - It has an umbrella-shaped white flower cluster and you are not certain which plant it is
> - It grows at a wet edge and has any resemblance to a parsnip or wild carrot [2]
> - A root, or a root piece, is the part you are considering. The root has the most toxin [2]
>
> **Confusable with:** wild parsnip and other edible-looking parsnips [2], water parsnip [6], and wild carrot (Queen Anne's lace) [4]; the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service groups it with cow parsnip, wild parsnip and Queen Anne's lace as look-alikes to avoid [8] → differences are listed below and are necessary but never sufficient → violent seizures; death in hours is documented [2].
>
> **Verdict:** Positive ID by an expert, or do not consume. This plant must never be eaten on field identification alone. Do not taste it to check.

This is a hazard entry, not a food entry. No part of this plant is food. The wild umbels as a group are covered in the [deadly-look-alike matrix](../../edible-plants/_index.md). Poison hemlock and poison ivy are in [toxic plants: hemlock and poison ivy](toxic-plants-hemlock-poison-ivy.md).

## Identify

### Why it is the one to fear

The USDA's Agricultural Research Service calls water hemlock the most violently toxic plant that grows in North America [1]. CDC's *MMWR* calls it the most toxic indigenous plant in North America and says it causes most of the deaths attributed to misidentifying a poisonous plant, because it is lethal in small quantities, resembles edible plants and is found throughout North America [2]. CDC says the root has the most cicutoxin and that eating a 2 to 3 cm piece of root can be fatal in an adult [2]. The toxin, cicutoxin, acts on the central nervous system as a violent convulsant [1].

### The plant

- **Species and range:** spotted water hemlock (*Cicuta maculata*) occurs across most of North America. A western species, *C. douglasii*, replaces it in the northwestern U.S. and western Canada [3]. Several sources opened here describe the western species, which is closely related. The West Virginia Department of Agriculture says water hemlock (*C. maculata*) is common along moist ditches and the margins of swamps in every county of that state [7]. Sources opened for Virginia, Maryland and Pennsylvania specifically were not found.
- **Habitat:** an obligate wetland plant, almost exclusively found in wetland areas [5]. Wet meadows, thickets, swamps, roadside ditches and floodplains [4]. Ditches, stream banks, pond margins and marshes [3].
- **Height and stem:** 3 to 6 feet, smooth and hollow [8]. Hairless, typically pale green with purple stripes [5]. Color varies from solid green or purple to green with purple spots or stripes [8]. Purple streaks do not settle it, because poison hemlock also has purple spots [7].
- **Leaves:** pinnately divided two or three times, with toothed leaflets [5]. **Vein pattern:** USDA ARS says the side veins lead to notches, not to tips at the outer margin [1]. NC State says the veins end in the notch between the teeth, and offers this as the feature that separates water hemlock from other members of the carrot family [4]. The Forest Service says the veins fork at their tips, with one branch ending at the leaflet tip [3]. The sources differ in wording. This is a hard feature to judge in the field.
- **Flowers:** small white flowers in an umbrella shape up to 6 inches across, June to August [5].
- **Root and stem base:** a cluster of chambered taproots, with a slightly swollen stem base that has many narrow cross-partitions [5]. The lower stem and upper root show numerous internal partitions or air spaces when sliced lengthwise [3]. The thick rootstalk has a number of small chambers, and cutting it releases a poisonous liquid [1]. West Virginia's agriculture department describes spindle-shaped roots, internally chambered, with a yellowish oil-like liquid [7].
- **Smell and taste:** CDC says it is similar in appearance to parsnips, smells like fresh turnips and tastes sweet [2]. USDA ARS says cicutoxin has a strong carrot-like odor [1]. The two sources describe the smell differently. Do not use smell or taste to identify it. A sweet taste is not a sign of anything.

### What it is mistaken for

- **Parsnips.** CDC says it is similar in appearance to parsnips [2]. USDA ARS says it has been mistaken for wild parsnips, other herbs and medicinal plants [1]. A boy in Cabell County, West Virginia, died after eating a root, presumably mistaken for a parsnip, in a case reported in 1934 [7].
- **Wild ginseng.** Two brothers digging for wild ginseng in a Maine swamp ate a water hemlock root. One died about 3 hours after eating it [2].
- **Water parsnip.** Water parsnip has green, often finely hairy stems. Its leaves are slightly serrated and usually not subdivided. Its flower heads are flatter and smaller, 2 to 5 inches, against about 6 inches and more dome-shaped for water hemlock [6].
- **Wild carrot (Queen Anne's lace).** NC State lists wild carrot as often confused with water hemlock [4]. Wild carrot is about 1 to 4 feet tall [9]. The Fish and Wildlife Service describes a small reddish floret sometimes in the middle and fuzzy stems [8]. Its stems are covered with coarse hairs, and it often has one dark purple floret in the center of the umbel [9]. Water hemlock stems are hairless [5].
- **Cow parsnip and wild parsnip.** The U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service groups them with water hemlock and Queen Anne's lace as six look-alikes to avoid, and says they can be hard to tell apart. It does not say which is mistaken for which [8]. Cow parsnip is taller, 4 to 10 feet, with fuzzy, grooved stems. Yellow flowers help tell wild parsnip apart [8].
- **Poison hemlock.** USDA ARS says water hemlock may be confused with poison hemlock because of the similar names [1]. They are different plants with different toxins.
- **Angelica.** No source opened for this entry names angelica as a look-alike of water hemlock, so it is not listed. Treat any unidentified wetland umbel as water hemlock.

## Avoid

- Do not dig, taste or eat any wild white-flowered umbel. A wet habitat does not clear a plant, and a dry habitat does not clear one either.
- Do not use the chamber test as your field test. CDC reports that cicutoxin can also be absorbed through the skin [2]. If someone must handle the plant, wear gloves (general practice) and wash afterward.
- Do not let children make whistles or pipes from hollow stems. CDC links stem whistles to deaths in children [2].
- Do not rely on season. The plant is poisonous at all stages of development and most toxic in the spring [2]. Young leaves and seed heads can also carry dangerous amounts [5].
- Keep dogs and livestock out of wet stands. Livestock signs can appear 15 minutes to 6 hours after eating it, and animals can die within hours [1].

## If Exposed / Bitten / Stung

**This is commonly-taught first-aid guidance, not medical advice. It does not replace a poison center or an emergency room. Steps 1 to 3 are general poison-exposure home care [10].**

1. **Call Poison Help, 1-800-222-1222,** and call 911 if the person has a seizure, is unresponsive or cannot breathe [10]. Do this for any suspected ingestion, even with no symptoms yet.
2. **Do not make the person vomit** unless poison control or a health care provider tells you to [10].
3. **Have ready:** the person's age, weight and condition, the time the plant was swallowed, how much, and what part [10]. Take the plant or photographs of it, with the stem base and leaves, to the emergency room if you can do so without further handling (general practice).
4. **There is no antidote and treatment is supportive,** per CDC [2]. That is why speed to a hospital matters.
5. **Skin contact:** wash the area with soap and water (general practice). The skin route is documented [2].

### How fast it moves

- CDC reports mild toxicity as nausea, abdominal pain and epigastric distress within 15 to 90 minutes [2]. In the Maine case, one man vomited and began convulsing within 30 minutes of eating a root and died about 3 hours after eating it, and his brother, who took one bite, had seizures and delirium about 2 hours later [2].
- Severe poisoning brings profuse salivation, sweating and respiratory distress, then severe seizures. Death usually results from status epilepticus [2]. The Forest Service lists vomiting and convulsions within 60 minutes and says ingestion often leads to death [3].
- The case-fatality rate for poisonings reported from 1900 through 1975 was 30 percent [2]. Complications include muscle breakdown with kidney failure, severe acid build-up in the blood, slow heart rate and low blood pressure [2]. Early vomiting may be partly protective, because many people bring up the root [2]. Do not count on that.

## ⚠ WHEN IT'S AN EMERGENCY

- **Any suspected eating of water hemlock, even a bite or a taste, is an emergency now.** Call Poison Help, 1-800-222-1222, and get to an emergency room. Mild symptoms can begin within 15 to 90 minutes, and a death has been reported about 3 hours after a few bites [2].
- **Call 911** for seizures, unresponsiveness, trouble breathing or collapse [10].
- **Do not wait for symptoms to settle.** Severe poisoning can move from first symptoms to seizures quickly [2].
- **Hairless, hollow, purple-streaked stem plus wet ground plus a root that was eaten:** treat it as water hemlock until an expert says otherwise.
