## Legal & Ethical Note

Eastern cottontail is a regulated small-game species in the Mid-Atlantic. Normal harvest occurs during controlled seasons (typically October or November through February) under state license, with method limits. Virginia, for example, allows rabbit trapping from October 15 to January 31 with box traps only, and bans deadfall traps [4]. Pennsylvania's 2026-27 cottontail season runs Oct 17 to Feb 28, 2027, with a junior and mentored season Oct 3 to 18 [15]; West Virginia's runs Nov 7 to Feb 28 [16]; Maryland's 2024 notice said rabbit and squirrel seasons were open until February 28 [13]. Seasons change every year, so check the state agency. Licences: Pennsylvania requires a current hunting license to hunt wildlife that is not a furbearer, with a junior-hunter exception for squirrels and rabbits [14]; Maryland says it is unlawful to hunt without a valid hunting license unless you fall under its listed exemptions [12]; West Virginia lists its few no-license cases in its regulations summary [16]. **None of the pages opened for this entry (Maryland, Pennsylvania, West Virginia, Virginia) lists a survival or emergency exemption from season, license or method rules**; do not assume one. The Virginia trapping page lists only a landowner exception for damage or a threat to health or safety, not a survival one [4]. **Know your local small-game regulations; this is not legal advice.** Humane dispatch and full utilization are ethical minimums.

## Identification & Behavior

**Appearance:** Small lagomorph, roughly 15 to 19 inches long and 2 to 3.5 lbs. Virginia DWR gives females, the larger sex, as 400–477 mm (about 16 to 19 inches) and 842–1,533 g (about 2 to 3.4 lbs) [10]; Maryland DNR says cottontails seldom weigh more than three pounds or exceed 15 inches [11]. Brown to gray fur on the back, white belly and white underside of the tail [10]. Short ears, prominent front teeth (incisors), hind legs long and muscular.

**Sign:** Small round dark pellets, trails through brush, clipped stems (general practice), and resting spots. Cottontails do not dig burrows or live in warrens. They rest in shallow, scratched-out depressions in grass, and in very bad weather may use abandoned groundhog dens [11]. Virginia DWR says nests are slanting holes lined with leaves, grass and fur, and that brushpiles give cover [10].

**Behavior:** Mostly nocturnal, most active at dawn and dusk [10]. Breeding starts at different times in different populations and years, with three to four litters per year [10]. Speed escape strategy; will zigzag through brush and into brush piles or cover. Does not hibernate; remains active year-round (general practice).

## Harvest

**Trap method (passive):** Check your state's rules first; they decide which traps you may use. In Virginia, rabbits may be trapped only with box traps, deadfall traps are prohibited, and land snares need the landowner's written permission and loops 12 inches or less in diameter [4]. See [`../../../substrate/05_food/`](../../../../substrate/05_food/) for trapping technique and the legal note.

**Strike method (active):** Firearm (.22 rifle) or club/cudgel (requires close approach in brush; risky). Lethal strike: back of the skull or spinal cord, severing the central nervous system.

**Dispatch from snare:** If caught alive, immediate cervical dislocation (sharp twist of head/neck) or heavy blunt blow to skull base ensures rapid death.

## Field Dressing & Skinning

**Immediate post-harvest (within minutes):**

1. **Separate the skin from meat.** Hold the rabbit by the hind legs (head down). Make a circular cut around the anus and genitals. Make an incision up the inside of each hind leg. Grasp the hide and pull downward toward the head—the skin separates cleanly in seconds. Sever the head (which comes with the hide) and front legs with the skin.

2. **Open the body cavity.** Make a small cut on the underside from sternum to anus; do not cut deeply into the viscera. Remove the paunch (stomach), liver, kidneys, and intestines by hand. **Inspect the liver as a tularemia screen:** if it has numerous white lesions about the size of a pinhead, discard the rabbit and do not eat it [2]. Discard the paunch, intestines and all other organs after the check (this guide's rule; this guide does not recommend eating wild-game organ meats).

3. **Cool at once.** Penn State says to dress and cool small game as soon as possible [9]. Keep the meat at 40°F or below and use it within two to three days [8]. Penn State says a carcass may be hung and chilled outdoors when the air is below 40°F, and above 40°F to use ice or refrigeration [9]. Do not hang a rabbit outdoors unless the air is certain to stay below 40°F (this guide's rule). Penn State gives no cooling temperature for rabbit alone.

**Tularemia handling precaution:** Wear latex or nitrile gloves when field-dressing and skinning [1][2]; take extra care if you have cuts or open wounds on your hands [2]. Do not touch a rabbit that was found dead or that acts sick; CDC says to avoid contact with sick or dead animals [1]. Wash hands thoroughly with soap and water after handling (general practice). If you get flu-like symptoms after handling rabbits, see a clinician and say you handled rabbits [2].

## Butchering & Preserving

**Butchering:**

1. **Remove the four legs** (hind and front) by cutting around the hip and shoulder joints, separating the ball-and-socket connections.
2. **Remove the saddle (loin)** by cutting along both sides of the spine and separating the tender backstrap meat.
3. **Remove the rib meat** by stripping muscle away from the ribcage.
4. **Discard the spine and all organs.** A healthy-looking liver is not a clearance; the liver check is only a test for discarding the rabbit [2].

**Preserving (rabbit meat is lean and spoils quickly):**

1. **Refrigeration:** Keep it at 40°F or below and use it within two to three days [8].
2. **Freezing:** Vacuum-seal or wrap in multiple freezer-paper layers. Penn State says properly wrapped game meat keeps 9 to 12 months in the freezer [8].
3. **Drying (jerky):** Slice lean meat thin and heat the strips to 165°F first (the rabbit rule; Penn State's jerky page says 160°F before drying), then dry at 140°F until a cooled strip cracks but does not break when bent [5]. Drying alone does not reliably kill disease organisms [6]. Do not sun-dry raw meat. Penn State says to eat jerky within one to three weeks, or freeze it [5].
4. **Stewing:** Rabbit is ideally suited to long-cooked stews and broths (boil for 45–60 minutes); tough connective tissue tenderizes with heat.

## ⚠ DISEASE & PARASITE

> ⚠ **TULAREMIA (RABBIT FEVER) — Bacterial infection (Francisella tularensis); can be life-threatening, and rabbits are especially susceptible [7].**
>
> **Screening checklist (ALL must hold; a healthy-looking rabbit can still carry disease, so gloves and full cooking apply every time):**
> - Animal is active and alert (no lethargy or staggering)
> - Normal weight for species (not visibly thin or emaciated)
> - No visible abscesses, lesions, or discharge
> - Liver is normal dark-red color, with no numerous white pinhead-sized lesions [2]
>
> **Do NOT harvest, handle or eat the animal if ANY of these are present (disqualifying traits):**
> - It was found dead, or acts sick: lethargic, disoriented, or unusually approachable [1]
> - Visible abscesses or nodules on skin or organs
> - Numerous white lesions about the size of a pinhead on the liver [2]
> - Pale, enlarged, or discolored liver
>
> **How tularemia spreads:** CDC lists tick and deer fly bites, skin contact with infected animals, and inhaling contaminated aerosols or dust [7]. Bacteria live in the blood, organs and tissues of infected rabbits. Missouri's conservation department says to heat rabbit meat to a minimum of 165°F to kill any potential disease [2].
>
> **Season does not remove the risk:** Infection is less likely in cold months, when ticks and biting flies are fewer, but waiting for cold weather will not eliminate the risk [2]. Rabbit season runs in the cold months, so use the same gloves, liver check and cooking temperature in every season.
>
> **MANDATORY PRECAUTION:**
> - **Wear gloves when field-dressing and skinning** [1][2]. Take extra care if you have cuts or open wounds on your hands [2].
> - **Do not handle a rabbit that acts sick or was found dead** [1]. If a rabbit lets you approach or is slow to flee, do not harvest it (this guide's precaution).
> - **Avoid aerosol exposure:** do not blow on the carcass or use high-pressure water to clean it (this guide's precaution; CDC lists inhaling contaminated aerosols as a route [7]).
> - **Cook thoroughly** (see MANDATORY PREPARATION section).
>
> **Verdict:** CDC says to cook game meat thoroughly [1]; this guide's rule is 165°F internal [2][3]. Do not count on freezing, drying or any storage step to remove the risk (this guide's rule); cooking is the control for the meat and gloves are the control for your hands. Healthy-appearing rabbits still carry some risk, so handle with care and cook fully.

## ⚠ MANDATORY PREPARATION

**Internal cook temperature:**
- **Whole rabbit or chunks:** 165°F internal temperature, measured at the thickest part of the thigh or loin, no pink. The USDA chart lists 160°F for rabbit [3]; the Missouri Department of Conservation says a minimum of 165°F [2]. The sources differ; this guide uses 165°F so both are met (this guide's rule).

**Cooking methods:**
- **Boiling/stewing:** Place whole or quartered rabbit in water, bring to boil, simmer 45–60 minutes until meat is tender and reaches 165°F internally. Broth from boiling counts as part of the cooked dish; bring it to a full boil before serving (general practice).
- **Roasting:** Clean whole rabbit, roast at 375°F for 45–60 minutes; check temperature at thigh (165°F).
- **Frying:** Quarter the rabbit, coat with flour/salt if available, fry in fat over medium heat until golden and cooked through (25–35 minutes).

**Rabbit as a sole food:**
- Rabbit meat is very lean, so it should not be your only food for long (general practice; no source opened for this entry gives a threshold). Eat it with any fat, starch or other food you have.
- **Do not eat the liver or other organs** (see Butchering; this guide's rule).

**Never eat:**
- Brain, spinal cord, or organs from any rabbit.
- Undercooked rabbit (must reach 165°F internal).
- Meat that smells off or sour.
