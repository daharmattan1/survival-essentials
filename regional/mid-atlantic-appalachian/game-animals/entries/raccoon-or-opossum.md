## Legal & Ethical Note

Both raccoon and opossum are regulated furbearers in Mid-Atlantic states; harvest is often restricted to specific seasons and methods (shotgun, rifle, or trap). Opossum is less commonly harvested (less valuable fur, less palatable); raccoon is the more typical harvest. This entry describes emergency-exception processing only. **Know your local furbearer regulations and method restrictions; this is not legal advice.** Both species yield modest meat (4–8 lbs of usable meat per animal) but are energy-expensive to field dress and butcher relative to calorie yield.

---

## Identification & Behavior

**Raccoon (Procyon lotor):**
- Medium carnivore, 15–20 inches long, 10–20 lbs.
- Distinctive "mask" (dark fur across eyes and face), gray-brown coat, black bushy tail with dark rings.
- Stocky build, short legs, distinctive hand-like front paws.
- Nocturnal; solitary except during mating season.
- Intelligent, dexterous, opportunistic omnivore; inhabits forests, wetlands, and human settlements.

**Virginia Opossum (Didelphis virginiana):**
- Medium marsupial, 20–24 inches long (including tail), 8–14 lbs.
- Pale gray-white coat, white face, pink nose, prehensile tail (not patterned like raccoon's).
- Smaller ears, pointed snout, corpulent body.
- Nocturnal; solitary. Defensive behavior: "playing possum" (feigning death when threatened).
- Omnivorous; scavenges carrion, insects, plants.

**Sign (both species):** Nocturnal feces (scat), claw marks on trees (raccoon), dens in hollow trees or burrows, foraging sign (overturned rocks, torn logs).

---

## Harvest

**Trap method (primary):** Large body-grip trap, Conibear trap (lethal), or heavy snare set at den entrance or along well-used trail. Both species are strong and can escape weak snares; robust construction is essential. Trap must be sufficiently large to kill the animal quickly (no prolonged suffering). Check traps daily (legal requirement in most states).

**Firearm method:** .22 rifle (head shot) or shotgun (close range, head shot). Raccoons can be aggressive if cornered or protecting young; extreme caution is needed.

**Dispatch from live trap:** Cervical dislocation or heavy blow to the skull base ensures rapid death.

---

## Field Dressing & Skinning

**Immediate post-harvest (within 30 minutes):**

1. **Separate the skin.** Raccoon and opossum hides are valuable and typically removed first. Make circular cuts around the hind legs and anus; make incisions up the inside of each hind leg. Pull the hide down from the legs toward the head—the hide separates in a continuous sheet. For commercial fur purposes, heads are removed with the hide; for meat harvest, separate the head.

2. **Open the body cavity.** Make a shallow incision from the sternum to the anus; do not cut deeply into the viscera. Remove the paunch (stomach), liver, gizzard, intestines, and heart. **Discard the paunch, intestines, and any organs with lesions.** Inspect the liver (should be dark brown-red; pale spots or discoloration suggest disease or parasites).

3. **Cool the carcass.** Remove to shade immediately; hang in cool environment if possible. Raccoon and opossum meat is fatty and spoils faster in warm weather than lean game.

**Trichinella handling note:** Gloves are recommended (though trichinella transmission via intact skin is rare). Wash hands thoroughly with soap and water after field dressing.

---

## Butchering

**Carcass breakdown (both species similar):**

1. **Remove the four legs** (separate at hip and shoulder joints by cutting around the ball-and-socket connections). Raccoon legs are the most valuable cut (higher meat yield, more tender).

2. **Remove the saddle (loin)** along both sides of the spine.

3. **Remove the rib meat** by cutting muscle away from the ribcage.

4. **Remove neck meat** by separating muscle from cervical vertebrae.

5. **Discard the spine, ribs, and other bones** after meat is removed. Organ meat (liver, heart, if healthy-looking) can be retained.

**Yield:** Raccoon yields ~4–6 lbs of usable meat; opossum yields ~3–4 lbs. Both species are fatty (higher calories per unit mass compared to venison/rabbit).

---

## Preserving

**Raccoon/opossum meat is fatty and spoils faster than lean game.**

1. **Refrigeration:** Wrapped in cloth or paper, lasts 2–3 days at 32–40°F. Fatty meat becomes rancid if not kept cold.

2. **Freezing:** Vacuum-seal or wrap in multiple freezer-paper layers; lasts 6–9 months at 0°F or below. Fat oxidizes over time, so freezer duration is shorter than for lean game.

3. **Drying (jerky, with caution):** Fatty meat is prone to rancidity during drying. Slice lean trim only (remove visible fat), salt, dry over low heat (160–170°F). Jerky from fatty meat should be consumed within 2–4 weeks.

4. **Stewing/braising:** Raccoon and opossum are ideally suited to long-cooked stews and braises (braise 2–3 hours at low temperature). Fat renders out during cooking; skim fat from broth before serving (fat carries parasites/bacteria at lower temperatures).

5. **Canning (pressure canning only):** Hot-pack rendered-and-seasoned meat into sterilized jars, process at 11 PSI (dial gauge) or 10 PSI (weighted gauge) for 75–90 minutes (pints) or 90 minutes (quarts). Requires pressure canner.

## ⚠ DISEASE & PARASITE

> ⚠ **TRICHINELLA (Trichinella spiralis) — Parasitic nematode; causes trichinellosis in humans; must be killed by heat.**
>
> **Positive-ID checklist (animal is low-risk if ALL hold):**
> - Animal appears healthy (normal weight, alert, no discharge or lesions)
> - Liver appears normal dark-brown-red, no pale spots or nodules
> - No visible abscesses or parasites
> - Muscles are firm (not soft or gelatinous, which can indicate heavy parasite load)
>
> **Do NOT harvest if ANY of these are present (disqualifying traits):**
> - Animal appears lethargic, disoriented, or unusually approachable
> - Visible lesions, abscesses, or discharge (especially from rectum or urinary tract)
> - Pale, enlarged, or discolored liver
> - Extremely emaciated or obese (both indicate poor health)
> - Soft, gelatinous muscle texture (indicates possible parasitic cyst load)
>
> **Trichinella transmission:** Raccoons are frequent carriers of *Trichinella spiralis*. The parasite lives in muscle tissue as microscopic cysts. Transmission to humans occurs via consumption of undercooked infected meat. The parasite encysts in human muscle, causing fever, myalgia (muscle pain), and severe systemic illness (potentially fatal if untreated).
>
> **MANDATORY PRECAUTION:**
> - **Cook to a HIGH internal temperature:** ≥165°F (74°C) minimum — the CDC wild-game Trichinella threshold. Recommended safety margin is 170–180°F at the thickest part of the leg or loin. A meat thermometer is essential; do not rely on visual appearance.
> - **No rare or medium-rare cooking:** All raccoon/opossum meat must be fully cooked throughout; do not leave pink centers.
> - **Avoid undercooked organ meat:** Liver, heart, and kidneys are potential cyst sites; cook to the same high temperature.
> - **Trim visible fat (palatability):** Trimming fat is fine and recommended for taste, but note that Trichinella cysts encyst in **skeletal muscle**, not fat — fat trimming does not eliminate the parasite risk. Full heat treatment of all muscle tissue is the only control.
> - **Do not freeze trichinella out:** Freezing does not reliably kill wild-game Trichinella (unlike some pork strains); only heat does.
>
> **Verdict:** Trichinella is not uncommonly found in raccoons. Strict adherence to high-temperature cooking (≥165°F / 74°C minimum, targeting 170–180°F, verified by thermometer) is the only reliable control. Opossum trichinella load is typically lower than raccoon, but the same precaution applies.

## ⚠ MANDATORY PREPARATION

**Internal cook temperature (CRITICAL for trichinella control):**
- **Raccoon/opossum meat (all cuts):** ≥165°F (74°C) minimum internal temperature — the CDC threshold for wild-game Trichinella. Measured at the thickest part of the leg or loin. Cook to 170–180°F for maximum safety margin. Use a meat thermometer; do NOT rely on visual appearance.

**Cooking methods:**
- **Stewing/braising (preferred method for raccoon/opossum):** Cut meat into 1–2 inch cubes, brown in fat, add water/broth and root vegetables, simmer at low temperature for 2–3 hours until meat is tender. Internal temperature reaches ≥165–180°F gradually during braising. Skim fat from broth before serving (improves flavor; note that Trichinella cysts are in the muscle, not the fat, but skimming is still good practice).
- **Roasting (parts or whole):** Roast at 325°F until internal temperature reaches ≥165°F, targeting 170°F (measure at thickest part of leg/loin, away from bone).
- **Frying (pieces):** Fry in fat over medium heat until cooked through to ≥165–180°F internal (measure with thermometer; visual doneness is unreliable).
- **Boiling:** Boil cut meat in water for 60–90 minutes until fully cooked; do not undercook.

**Never eat:**
- Undercooked or rare raccoon/opossum (must reach ≥165°F / 74°C internal minimum; target 170–180°F, no exceptions).
- Brain, spinal cord, or organs from ANY wild carnivore (parasite/disease risk).
- Meat from a visibly sick animal.
- Meat that smells sour or off.

**Least-preferred species:** Raccoon and opossum rank as least-preferred game in most survival contexts due to (1) higher disease/parasite burden, (2) higher preparation/cooking time, (3) lower meat yield relative to size, (4) palatability concerns (some find taste objectionable). Rabbit and squirrel are preferred for passive trap yield.
