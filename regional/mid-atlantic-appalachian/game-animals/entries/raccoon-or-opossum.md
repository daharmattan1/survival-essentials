## Legal & Ethical Note

Both raccoon and opossum are regulated furbearers in Mid-Atlantic states; harvest is often restricted to specific seasons and methods (shotgun, rifle, or trap). In Virginia, for example, trapping raccoon and opossum on private land requires the landowner's permission, traps must be visited once each day, and deadfall traps are prohibited [10]. **No source opened for this entry supports a survival or emergency exemption from these rules** (this guide's judgment); do not assume one. Opossum is less commonly harvested (less valuable fur, less palatable); raccoon is the more typical harvest. **Know your local furbearer regulations and method restrictions; this is not legal advice.** Both species are effort-heavy to dress and butcher for the meat they give (general practice).

## Identification & Behavior

**Raccoon (Procyon lotor):**
- Medium carnivore, 15–20 inches long, 10–20 lbs.
- Distinctive "mask" (dark fur across eyes and face), gray-brown coat, black bushy tail with dark rings.
- Stocky build, short legs, distinctive hand-like front paws.
- Nocturnal; solitary except during mating season.
- Intelligent, dexterous, opportunistic omnivore; inhabits forests, wetlands, and human settlements.

**Virginia Opossum (Didelphis virginiana):**
- Medium marsupial, 20–24 inches long (including tail), 8–14 lbs.
- Pale gray-white coat, white face, pink nose, prehensile tail (not patterned like raccoon's).
- Smaller ears, pointed snout, corpulent body.
- Nocturnal; solitary. Defensive behavior: "playing possum" (feigning death when threatened).
- Omnivorous; scavenges carrion, insects, plants.

**Sign (both species):** Nocturnal feces (scat), claw marks on trees (raccoon), dens in hollow trees or burrows, foraging sign (overturned rocks, torn logs).

---

## Harvest

**Trap method (primary):** Large body-grip trap, Conibear trap (lethal), or heavy snare set at den entrance or along well-used trail. Both species are strong and can escape weak snares; robust construction is essential. Trap must be sufficiently large to kill the animal quickly (no prolonged suffering). Check traps at least daily (Virginia requires a visit once each day [10]; know your state).

**Firearm method:** .22 rifle (head shot) or shotgun (close range, head shot). Raccoons can be aggressive if cornered or protecting young; extreme caution is needed.

**Dispatch from live trap:** Cervical dislocation or heavy blow to the skull base ensures rapid death.

## Field Dressing & Skinning

**Immediate post-harvest (within 30 minutes):**

1. **Separate the skin.** Raccoon and opossum hides are valuable and typically removed first. Make circular cuts around the hind legs and anus; make incisions up the inside of each hind leg. Pull the hide down from the legs toward the head—the hide separates in a continuous sheet. For commercial fur purposes, heads are removed with the hide; for meat harvest, separate the head.

2. **Open the body cavity.** Make a shallow incision from the sternum to the anus; do not cut deeply into the viscera. Remove the paunch (stomach), liver, intestines, and heart. **Discard the paunch, intestines, and all organs** (this guide's rule, see MANDATORY PRECAUTION). Inspect the liver first (should be dark brown-red; pale spots or discoloration suggest disease or parasites) and discard the animal if it looks wrong.

3. **Cool the carcass.** Remove to shade immediately; hang in cool environment if possible. Keep the carcass cold; warm weather speeds spoilage (general practice).

**Handling note:** Wear disposable gloves when handling wild game [7]. *Trichinella* infection comes from eating infected meat, not from touching it [1]. Wash hands thoroughly with soap and water after field dressing (general practice).

**Rabies:** Raccoons are among the animals most often found with rabies in the US [11]. Never approach or handle a raccoon or opossum that appears sick, injured or is not acting normally; CDC says to keep your distance from wildlife [12]. Never handle one bare-handed (this guide's rule). After any bite or scratch, wash the wound with soap and water for 15 minutes and seek medical care urgently [12]; tell the clinician what animal it was [12]. If you may have been exposed, contact your health department [12].

## Butchering

**Carcass breakdown (both species similar):**

1. **Remove the four legs** (separate at hip and shoulder joints by cutting around the ball-and-socket connections). Raccoon legs are the most valuable cut (higher meat yield, more tender).

2. **Remove the saddle (loin)** along both sides of the spine.

3. **Remove the rib meat** by cutting muscle away from the ribcage.

4. **Remove neck meat** by separating muscle from cervical vertebrae.

5. **Discard the spine, ribs, and other bones** after meat is removed. Discard all organs, including the liver and heart (this guide's rule; see MANDATORY PRECAUTION).

**Yield:** This guide has no opened source for usable-meat weight or calories; treat both species as modest in yield for the effort of dressing them (general practice).

---

## Preserving

**Raccoon/opossum meat is fatty; this guide has no source on how fast it goes off compared with lean game, so keep it cold and use it quickly.**

1. **Refrigeration:** Unfrozen game meat goes in the refrigerator and is used within two to three days [6].
2. **Freezing:** Wrap in multiple layers of freezer paper or plastic wrap. Penn State says properly wrapped game meat keeps 9 to 12 months in the freezer [6]; the fat in this meat may shorten the good-quality time (general practice). **Freezing is storage, not a way to kill *Trichinella*;** some wild-game strains survive freezing [2][3].
3. **Drying (jerky):** Not recommended for this meat. If you do it, trim visible fat, heat the strips to 165°F first (CDC's 2024 figure for wild game [13]), then dry at 140°F [8]. CDC says drying, smoking and curing alone do not consistently kill *Trichinella* worms, and homemade jerky from wild game has caused outbreaks [1][2].
4. **Stewing/braising:** Raccoon and opossum suit long-cooked stews and braises (general practice). Skim fat from the broth before serving.
5. **Canning (pressure canning only):** Pressure canning is the only recommended method for low-acid foods such as meat [9]. Use a tested pressure-canning recipe from an extension service for your altitude; this guide gives no pressures or times. If you are unsure how a jar was canned, throw it out [9].

## ⚠ DISEASE & PARASITE

> ⚠ **TRICHINELLA — Parasitic worm; causes trichinellosis in people who eat undercooked infected meat; killed by cooking, not reliably by freezing, drying, smoking or curing.**
>
> **Screening checklist (appearance does NOT rule out Trichinella; infected meat can look normal):**
> - Animal appears healthy (normal weight, alert, no discharge or lesions)
> - Liver appears normal dark-brown-red, no pale spots or nodules
> - No visible abscesses
>
> **Do NOT harvest or eat the animal if ANY of these are present (disqualifying traits):**
> - Animal appears lethargic, disoriented, or unusually approachable
> - Visible lesions, abscesses, or discharge
> - Pale, enlarged, or discolored liver
> - Extremely emaciated
>
> **Trichinella and raccoons:** The CDC says infection comes from eating raw or undercooked pork or wild game, in particular bear, wild boar, wildcat, fox, wolf, seal or walrus; it also says meat-eating animals are the usual source [1]. **The CDC does not name raccoon.** A study in south-central Wisconsin found *Trichinella* in 19 percent of 59 raccoons tested (and 26 percent of coyotes) [4]. That is one study in one place, but it shows raccoons can carry it, so this guide treats raccoon meat as a *Trichinella* risk (this guide's precaution). This guide found no opened source on opossum and *Trichinella*; FM 21-76 says only that scavenging mammals such as the opossum may carry diseases [5]. Treat opossum the same way (this guide's precaution).
>
> **MANDATORY PRECAUTION:**
> - **Cook to at least 165°F (74°C) internal.** CDC's 2024 report says cooking wild game to 165°F is necessary to kill *Trichinella* [13]; its 2004 report gave 160°F [3]. Use a thermometer in the thickest part of the leg or loin; do not rely on color [3][2].
> - **No rare or medium-rare cooking:** cook all raccoon and opossum meat fully; no pink centers.
> - **Do not eat the brain, spinal cord or organs of wild carnivores** (this guide's rule; general practice).
> - **Trimming fat is for taste only.** It does not change the need to cook to temperature (general practice).
> - **Do not rely on freezing, drying, smoking, salting or curing.** CDC: freezing wild game may not kill all worms because some are freeze-resistant, and curing, drying, smoking or microwaving alone does not consistently kill them [2].
> - **Do not taste meat until it is fully cooked** [2].
>
> **Verdict:** Cook every piece of raccoon or opossum to temperature, verified by thermometer. Trichinellosis is preventable by cooking [2].

## ⚠ MANDATORY PREPARATION

**Internal cook temperature (critical for Trichinella control):**
- **Raccoon/opossum meat (all cuts):** at least **165°F (74°C)**, per CDC's 2024 report on wild game [13] (its 2004 report gave 160°F [3]). Measure at the thickest part of the leg or loin with a food thermometer, away from bone. Do not rely on color [2][3].

**Cooking methods (general practice):**
- **Stewing/braising (preferred):** Cut meat into 1–2 inch cubes, brown in fat, add water or broth and root vegetables, and simmer at low heat until tender. Confirm with a thermometer that the meat has reached temperature. Skim fat from the broth before serving.
- **Roasting:** Roast until the thickest part of the leg or loin reaches temperature; check with a thermometer.
- **Frying:** Fry pieces until cooked through, and verify with a thermometer, not by eye.
- **Boiling:** Boil cut meat until it reaches temperature; do not undercook.

**Never eat:**
- Undercooked or rare raccoon/opossum (below 165°F [13]).
- Brain, spinal cord, or organs from any wild carnivore (this guide's rule; general practice).
- Meat from a visibly sick animal.
- Meat that smells sour or off (do not taste it to check).
- Any smoked, dried or canned wild-game meat you cannot account for; botulism toxin cannot be seen, smelled or tasted [9].

**Least-preferred species:** Raccoon and opossum rank low as survival food (this guide's judgment): *Trichinella* risk (above), long cooking time, modest meat yield relative to effort, and taste. Rabbit and squirrel are better trap yields; see the rabbit and squirrel entries and `fish-and-game-processing-basics` for tularemia handling.
