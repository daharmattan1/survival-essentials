## Legal & Ethical Note

Gray squirrel is a regulated small-game species in the Mid-Atlantic states whose pages were opened for this entry. Normal harvest occurs during controlled seasons (typically September through February) under state license, with method limits. The 2026-27 Pennsylvania squirrel season runs Sept 12 to Feb 28, 2027 (closed Dec 25) [15], and West Virginia's runs Sept 12 to Feb 28 [16]; Maryland's 2024 notice put the season at September to February, with a bag limit of six a day [11]. Seasons change every year, so check the state agency. Licences: Pennsylvania requires a current hunting license for wildlife that is not a furbearer, with a junior-hunter exception for squirrels and rabbits [14]; Maryland says it is unlawful to hunt without a valid hunting license unless you fall under its listed exemptions [17]; West Virginia lists its few no-license cases in its regulations summary [16]. Trapping: Virginia's trapping page, which does not list squirrels, bans deadfall traps and requires written landowner permission for land snares [4]; this guide did not confirm whether squirrels may be trapped in any state, so check yours. **None of the Maryland, Pennsylvania, West Virginia or Virginia pages opened for this entry lists a survival or emergency exemption from season, license or method rules**; do not assume one. **Know your local small-game regulations; this is not legal advice.**

**Protected look-alike on the Delmarva Peninsula:** the Delmarva fox squirrel is a different, protected squirrel. Maryland's squirrel season does not include it, and the subspecies remains protected [11]. Delaware says hunting and harvesting Delmarva fox squirrels is prohibited and calls them state-endangered [12]. It is much larger than a gray squirrel (about 1½ times), with steely blue to whitish-gray fur, a white belly and short, rounded ears [12][13]. Do not shoot any squirrel on the Delmarva Peninsula that you cannot tell apart from it.

Squirrels offer modest protein per animal, so you need several for a meal (general practice; no source opened for this entry gives a yield). The hazard here is rated moderate, lower than the rabbit entry's high, because CDC names rabbits as the animals especially susceptible to tularemia [9]; the gloves and full-cooking rules are the same.

## Identification & Behavior

**Appearance:** Small rodent. Virginia DWR gives 16–18 ounces and a total length of 12–21 inches including the tail [10]. Fur is grayish to yellowish brown above and white below, including the chin and belly; the tail is bushy with a white border; ears are of moderate size and are not tufted [10]. Virginia DWR tells it from the larger fox squirrel by its smaller size and white-edged tail [10].

**Sign:** Chewed nut shells at base of trees, scratched bark on nut-bearing trees, droppings (small, dark pellets), tracks (four toes on front paws, five on hind) (general practice; no source opened for this entry describes squirrel sign).

**Behavior:** Diurnal and active year-round. Solitary or in loose groups. Arboreal (lives in trees); descends to forage on ground. Excellent hearing and vision; wary. Nests in tree cavities or builds outside leaf nests (dreys). Caches nuts for winter.

## Harvest

**Trap method (passive):** Where your state allows it, a snare at a tree base, on logs, or along well-used foraging trails. Deadfalls are prohibited in Virginia (DWR); check your state's rules. See [`../../../substrate/05_food/`](../../../../substrate/05_food/) for snare construction. Snare size and height depend on your state's rules; Virginia limits land snare loops to 12 inches in diameter [4]. This guide gives no squirrel-specific loop size.

**Firearm method:** .22 rifle or small-caliber firearm (lethal shot to the head or spine).

**Dispatch:** If caught alive in snare, cervical dislocation (sharp twist of head/neck) or heavy blow to the skull ensures rapid death.

## Field Dressing & Skinning

**Immediate post-harvest:**

1. **Put on gloves first.** CDC says to use gloves when handling animals, especially rabbits, muskrats, prairie dogs and other rodents [1]. Squirrels are rodents. Take extra care with cuts or open wounds on your hands, and wash your hands with soap and water afterward (general practice).

2. **Separate the skin.** Hold the squirrel by the hind legs (head down). Make circular cuts around the anus and hind legs. Make an incision up the inside of each hind leg. Grasp the hide and pull sharply downward—the skin separates in one continuous motion. Remove the head and front legs with the skin.

3. **Open the cavity.** Make a small incision from sternum to anus (shallow, to avoid cutting into viscera). Remove the paunch (stomach), liver, kidneys, and intestines. Inspect the organs as a disease screen only. Penn State says to discard the carcass if any internal organ smells offensive or shows greenish discharge, black blood, or blood clots in the muscle [7]. Pale spots or nodules on the liver are a reason to discard the animal (this guide's rule; no opened source gives squirrel-specific liver findings). Discard all organs after the check; a healthy-looking liver is not a clearance (this guide's rule; this guide does not recommend eating wild-game organ meats).

4. **Cool immediately.** Even in cold weather, do not delay processing. Squirrel meat is small and spoils quickly.

## Butchering & Preserving

**Butchering (minimal, as the animal is small):**

1. **Remove the four legs** (already partially separated with the hide).
2. **Remove the saddle (loin)** by cutting along both sides of the spine.
3. **Discard the spine, ribs, and all organs** after the disease screen above.

**Preserving:**

1. **Refrigeration:** Keep it at 40°F or below and use it within two to three days [18].
2. **Freezing:** Vacuum-seal or wrap in freezer paper. Penn State says properly wrapped game meat keeps 9 to 12 months in the freezer [18].
3. **Drying (jerky):** Slice meat thin and heat the strips to 165°F first (CDC's 2024 wild-game figure [8]; Penn State's jerky page says 160°F before drying), then dry at 140°F until a cooled strip cracks but does not break when bent [3]. Drying alone does not reliably kill disease organisms [6]. Do not sun-dry raw meat. Penn State says to eat jerky within one to three weeks, or freeze it [3].
4. **Stewing/boiling:** Squirrel is ideal for long-cooked broths and stews (simmer 45–60 minutes); tough connective tissue tenderizes with heat.

**Yield:** Most of the usable meat is in the legs and saddle. This guide has no opened source for a weight of usable meat; plan on several squirrels per meal (general practice).

## ⚠ DISEASE & PARASITE

> ⚠ **PARASITES & BACTERIA — Squirrels can harbor intestinal parasites, fleas, and general bacterial flora.**
>
> **Screening checklist (ALL must hold; appearance does NOT rule out disease, so cook every squirrel to 165°F regardless):**
> - Animal is active and alert (normal behavior)
> - No visible lesions, abscesses, or discharge
> - Fur is clean and full (not patchy or matted)
> - Liver appears normal dark-red color, no pale spots or enlargement
> - No fleas visible on body (inspect neck and groin)
>
> **Do NOT harvest if ANY of these are present (disqualifying traits):**
> - Animal appears lethargic or unusually approachable
> - Visible lesions, nodules, or abscesses on skin
> - Pale, enlarged, or discolored liver
> - Obvious flea infestation or parasitic damage
>
> **Parasites:** Wild rodents can carry intestinal worms and fleas (general knowledge; no source opened for this entry quantifies them). A heavy load may mean an unhealthy animal; discard that animal.
>
> **Tularemia:** CDC lists rodents among the animals to handle with gloves, and says to cook game meat thoroughly [1]. Wear gloves for all dressing and skinning. If you get flu-like symptoms after handling wild rodents or rabbits, see a clinician and say what you handled (general practice, carried over from the rabbit guidance).
>
> **Verdict:** Wear gloves, and cook to 165°F internal temperature (this guide's rule; see MANDATORY PREPARATION). Avoid obviously sick or heavily parasitized animals. A healthy-looking animal is not a clearance, so handle and cook every squirrel the same way.

## ⚠ MANDATORY PREPARATION

**Internal cook temperature:**
- **Whole squirrel or chunks:** 165°F internal temperature, measured at the thickest part of the thigh or loin. CDC's 2024 report gives 165°F for cooking wild game [8] (it does not name squirrel; this guide applies it to all wild game); its 2004 report gave 160°F [5]. This also matches rabbit and birds.

**Cooking methods:**
- **Boiling/stewing:** Place whole or quartered squirrel in water, bring to boil, simmer 45–60 minutes until tender and cooked through. Broth is nutritious.
- **Roasting:** Clean whole squirrel and roast until the thigh reaches 165°F (45 minutes or more; time varies, so use a thermometer).
- **Frying:** Quarter the squirrel, coat with flour/salt if available, fry in fat over medium heat until cooked through (20–30 minutes).

**Never eat:**
- Undercooked squirrel (must reach 165°F internal).
- Brain, spinal cord, or any organs (this guide's rule; this guide does not recommend eating wild-game organ meats).
- Meat from a visibly sick or heavily parasitized animal.
- An animal whose liver shows pale spots, nodules, or discoloration.

**Storage:** Refrigerate cooked meat within 2 hours, or within 1 hour if the air is above 90°F [2]. Keep the refrigerator at 40°F or below [2].
