## Legal & Ethical Note

Gray squirrel is a regulated small-game species in all Mid-Atlantic states. Normal harvest occurs during controlled seasons (typically mid-fall through winter) under state license. This entry describes emergency-exception processing only. **Know your local small-game regulations; this is not legal advice.** Squirrels offer modest protein (~50–100g per animal); multiple squirrels are needed for meaningful calories.

---

## Identification & Behavior

**Appearance:** Small rodent, 8–11 inches long (with tail), 0.5–1.5 lbs. Fur is gray above, white or pale below. Tail is large, fluffy, and brushy (used for balance and communication). Ears are small and tufted; eyes are prominent and dark.

**Sign:** Chewed nut shells at base of trees (characteristic splits on acorn caps), scratched bark on nut-bearing trees, droppings (small, dark pellets), middens (piles of scale debris at feeding areas), tracks (four toes on front paws, five on hind; prints ~1.5 inches long).

**Behavior:** Diurnal and active year-round. Solitary or in loose groups. Arboreal (lives in trees); descends to forage on ground. Excellent hearing and vision; wary. Nests in tree cavities or builds outside leaf nests (dreys). Caches nuts for winter.

---

## Harvest

**Trap method (passive):** Snare or deadfall at tree base, on logs, or along well-used foraging trails. See [`../../../substrate/05_food/`](../../../../substrate/05_food/) for snare construction. Loop should be 2–3 inches in diameter, set at head height when squirrel is running along a branch or at ground level along a trail.

**Firearm method:** .22 rifle or small-caliber firearm (lethal shot to the head or spine).

**Dispatch:** If caught alive in snare, cervical dislocation (sharp twist of head/neck) or heavy blow to the skull ensures rapid death.

---

## Field Dressing & Skinning

**Immediate post-harvest:**

1. **Separate the skin.** Hold the squirrel by the hind legs (head down). Make circular cuts around the anus and hind legs. Make an incision up the inside of each hind leg. Grasp the hide and pull sharply downward—the skin separates in one continuous motion. Remove the head and front legs with the skin.

2. **Open the cavity.** Make a small incision from sternum to anus (shallow, to avoid cutting into viscera). Remove the paunch (stomach), liver, kidneys, and intestines. Inspect the liver (should be dark, uniform color; pale spots or nodules suggest parasites or disease).

3. **Cool immediately.** Even in cold weather, do not delay processing. Squirrel meat is small and spoils quickly.

---

## Butchering & Preserving

**Butchering (minimal, as the animal is small):**

1. **Remove the four legs** (already partially separated with the hide).
2. **Remove the saddle (loin)** by cutting along both sides of the spine.
3. **Discard the spine, ribs, and organs** (except liver, if healthy-looking).

**Preserving:**

1. **Refrigeration:** Wrapped in cloth, lasts 2–3 days at 32–40°F.
2. **Freezing:** Vacuum-seal or wrap in freezer paper; lasts 6–9 months at 0°F or below.
3. **Drying (jerky):** Slice meat thin, salt, dry over low heat (160–170°F) or sun-dry (3–5 days). Dried squirrel lasts months if kept dry.
4. **Stewing/boiling:** Squirrel is ideal for long-cooked broths and stews (simmer 45–60 minutes); tough connective tissue tenderizes with heat.

**Yield:** A single squirrel yields approximately 50–100g of usable meat (mostly legs and saddle). Multiple squirrels needed for meaningful meal.

## ⚠ DISEASE & PARASITE

> ⚠ **PARASITES & BACTERIA — Squirrels can harbor intestinal parasites, fleas, and general bacterial flora.**
>
> **Positive-ID checklist (animal is low-risk if ALL hold):**
> - Animal is active and alert (normal behavior)
> - No visible lesions, abscesses, or discharge
> - Fur is clean and full (not patchy or matted)
> - Liver appears normal dark-red color, no pale spots or enlargement
> - No fleas visible on body (inspect neck and groin)
>
> **Do NOT harvest if ANY of these are present (disqualifying traits):**
> - Animal appears lethargic or unusually approachable
> - Visible lesions, nodules, or abscesses on skin
> - Pale, enlarged, or discolored liver
> - Obvious flea infestation or parasitic damage
>
> **Common parasites in wild rodents:** Intestinal nematodes (roundworms), tapeworms, fleas (external). These are NOT transmissible to humans via properly cooked meat but indicate general parasite load. Heavy infestation may indicate an unhealthy animal.
>
> **Verdict:** Thorough cooking to 160°F internal temperature kills all parasites and bacteria. Avoid obviously sick or heavily parasitized animals. Most healthy squirrels pose low risk if cooked properly.

## ⚠ MANDATORY PREPARATION

**Internal cook temperature (USDA for wild small game):**
- **Whole squirrel or chunks:** 160°F internal temperature (measure at thickest part of thigh or loin).

**Cooking methods:**
- **Boiling/stewing:** Place whole or quartered squirrel in water, bring to boil, simmer 45–60 minutes until tender and cooked through. Broth is nutritious.
- **Roasting:** Clean whole squirrel, roast at 375°F for 30–45 minutes; check internal temperature.
- **Frying:** Quarter the squirrel, coat with flour/salt if available, fry in fat over medium heat until cooked through (20–30 minutes).

**Never eat:**
- Undercooked squirrel (must reach 160°F internal).
- Brain or spinal cord from any wild rodent (parasite/prion risk).
- Meat from a visibly sick or heavily parasitized animal.
- Liver showing pale spots, nodules, or discoloration.
- Organs from a flea-infested animal (though heat kills fleas, the visual sign indicates overall poor health).

**Storage:** Small meat volume; use within 1 day of cooking if unrefrigerated.
