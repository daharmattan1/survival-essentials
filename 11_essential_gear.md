# Essential Gear: What to Keep, Where, and How to Keep It Working

You need this guide before the emergency, or in the first hour of one. It covers what to carry every day, what to keep at home, in a bag by the door, in your car and on a hike, and how to keep it all working.

Three things hurt people first:

1. **Gear they cannot find or do not know how to use.** A tourniquet in the box does nothing if nobody has practiced.
2. **Gear that fails when needed.** Dead batteries, expired medicine, a radio nobody has turned on in two years.
3. **Gear that causes the emergency.** Fuel-burning heaters, camp stoves and grills used indoors, and generators run too close to a house cause carbon monoxide (CO) poisoning. Candles cause fires. The rules are in [Gear that can hurt you](#gear-that-can-hurt-you).

Skills and a plan matter more than the gear. Ready.gov tells every household to make a plan: how you get alerts, where you shelter, how you evacuate, how you reach each other [10]. The National Park Service says to know how to use a map, compass or GPS before you go out [27]. No source ranks skills against gear, so "skills first" is this guide's judgment. It is the reason the first-aid and tourniquet advice below says "get trained."

First aid advice here is general information, not medical advice. For the steps, use [06_first_aid.md](06_first_aid.md).

## What to do first, in order

1. **Make the plan.** Meet with the household and cover alerts, shelter, evacuation route and how you will reach each other. Write down each person's needs: age, medical needs, disabilities, pets or service animals [10].
2. **Water.** Store at least 1 gallon per person per day [1][2]. Plan for at least 3 days, and 2 weeks if you can [29].
3. **Light, radio, power.** A flashlight for every household member, extra batteries, a battery or hand-crank radio, and a phone with chargers and a backup battery [1][5]. Add a CO alarm for each level here (this guide's judgment; see [Gear that can hurt you](#gear-that-can-hurt-you)).
4. **First aid and medicines.** A first aid kit, the medicines each person needs, and training for anything in the kit you could get wrong [1][13].
5. **Food, warmth, hygiene, documents.** Non-perishable food, a blanket or sleeping bag per person, hygiene items, copies of key documents [1].
6. **Extras.** Everything else.

Buy in the same order if money is tight (this guide's judgment). [Scenario A](#scenario-a-a-family-builds-a-home-kit-in-one-weekend) follows it.

## Four kits, one idea

Ready.gov's pet guidance says to have two kits: a larger one for sheltering in place and a lightweight one for evacuating [7]. Keep the kit in one or two easy-to-carry containers, with items in airtight plastic bags [1]. Red Cross says to keep a first aid kit in your home and your car [13].

This guide uses four kits, a structure that is this guide's judgment. A hike has [the Ten Essentials](#the-ten-essentials-for-the-outdoors).

| Kit | Where | Covers | Main source |
|---|---|---|---|
| [Carry daily](#carry-daily) | Pocket or bag | The first hour | Ready.gov [1][6]; the rest is general practice |
| [Grab-and-go](#grab-and-go-bag) | By the door | About 3 days away from home | Ready.gov [1][7]; Red Cross [12] |
| [Car kit](#car-kit) | In the car | A breakdown or being stuck in snow | Ready.gov [3][4]; Red Cross [11] |

The one idea: each kit supports the guides it points to, and skills come first. Gear buys time to use what the other guides teach.

## Carry daily

What to have on you before anything goes wrong. Choosing these for a pocket or bag is general practice.

- A phone, and a charger or power bank [1][6].
- A whistle [1] and a small flashlight [1][5].
- Photo ID and a card with emergency contacts (general practice).
- A day's medicine, if you take any (general practice).

**What goes wrong:** the phone is nearly dead, the flashlight has dead batteries, or the medicine is at home.

## Home supply: the base kit

Ready.gov's basic disaster supplies kit [1]. CDC's short list agrees on the core: first aid kit and fire extinguisher, battery-powered radio, water and non-perishable food, flashlights and extra batteries, sleeping bags or blankets [14].

**Water and food**
- Water: one gallon per person per day for several days, for drinking and sanitation [1]. CDC says at least 3 days, and "try to store a 2-week supply if possible" [29]. Store more for pregnancy, illness, pets and heat [29]. Ready.gov says water needs can double in very hot temperatures [2].
- Food: at least a several-day supply of non-perishable food, and a manual can opener [1]. Because CDC water guidance reaches 2 weeks, this guide suggests aiming for 2 weeks of food at home (this guide's judgment). What to throw out after a power loss is in [09_urban_survival.md](09_urban_survival.md#food-what-to-keep-and-what-to-throw-out).

**Information, light and power**
- A battery-powered or hand-crank radio and a NOAA Weather Radio with tone alert [1]. NOAA Weather Radio broadcasts National Weather Service information around the clock, also carries warnings for non-weather hazards, and needs a receiver built to pick up its signal on seven frequencies (162.400 to 162.550 MHz) [22]. An ordinary radio may not receive it. Radio use is in [08_communication.md](08_communication.md).
- A flashlight for every household member, and extra batteries [1][5].
- A phone with chargers and a backup battery [1]. Keep a back-up power source on hand, and put a portable phone charger in your car [6].

**Shelter in place and utilities**
- Plastic sheeting, scissors and duct tape [1].
- A wrench or pliers to turn off utilities [1]. Learn where the shutoffs are and how they work before you need them (general practice).
- Garbage bags, plastic ties and moist towelettes [1].
- A dust mask [1], a whistle [1], local maps [1] (see [07_navigation.md](07_navigation.md)).

**Warmth, hygiene and safety**
- A sleeping bag or warm blanket for each person, a change of clothing for your climate, and sturdy shoes [1].
- Soap, hand sanitizer and disinfecting wipes, feminine supplies and personal hygiene items [1].
- Matches in a waterproof container [1]. Fire starting is in [03_fire.md](03_fire.md).
- A fire extinguisher [1], and CO alarms (see below).

**Paper and money**
- Copies of important family documents, saved electronically or in a waterproof container [1].
- Cash or traveler's checks [1].
- Paper and pencil [1].

**Medicines and care items**
- Prescription and non-prescription medicines (pain relievers, anti-diarrhea medicine, antacids, laxatives), and prescription eyeglasses or contact lens solution [1]. Over-the-counter (OTC) medicines: follow the label dose only.
- Infant, pet and other special needs: [Special needs](#special-needs).

**What goes wrong:** dead batteries, expired medicine, water never replaced, a radio nobody has turned on in two years. Fix: [Keeping it working](#keeping-it-working).

## First aid kit

Red Cross's recommended kit list, which the Red Cross text says is for a family of four [32]:

- 2 absorbent compress dressings (5 x 9 inches)
- 25 adhesive bandages in assorted sizes
- 1 adhesive cloth tape (10 yards x 1 inch)
- 5 antibiotic ointment packets and 5 antiseptic wipe packets
- 2 hydrocortisone ointment packets
- 2 pairs of non-latex gloves
- Roller bandages (one 3-inch and one 4-inch), 5 sterile gauze pads (3 x 3 inches) and 5 (4 x 4 inches)
- 2 triangular bandages
- Tweezers, an oral thermometer (non-mercury, non-glass), an emergency blanket, a breathing barrier with one-way valve, an instant cold compress
- First aid instructions, plus personal medicines and emergency phone numbers [13]

Red Cross's list also includes 2 packets of aspirin (81 mg each) [13]. Do not give aspirin to someone because it is in the kit, and never give it to a child unless the child's clinician says to [31]. Ask a clinician, pharmacist or 911 dispatcher, and follow the label.

**Add a tourniquet, and get trained.** The Red Cross list does not include one [13]. [06_first_aid.md](06_first_aid.md#severe-bleeding) says to use a tourniquet for life-threatening arm or leg bleeding.

1. Keep a purpose-made tourniquet in the kit. A belt or cord is a last resort, not a kit item.
2. Place it at least 2 to 3 inches from the wound, between the wound and the heart, never on a joint [25].
3. Note the time you put it on [25].
4. Get trained before you need it. Stop the Bleed (American College of Surgeons) teaches three techniques: direct pressure, packing the wound, and a tourniquet [26]. A tourniquet is a tool you must have practiced with, so find a course (this guide's judgment).

**This guide's additions** (general practice, not from a cited list): scissors or trauma shears, non-stick pads, an elastic wrap, a splint, oral rehydration salts, a pen to write the time, and epinephrine if prescribed. See [06_first_aid.md](06_first_aid.md#a-basic-kit).

Check the kit regularly. Replace anything used or out of date [13].

**What goes wrong:** a tourniquet nobody has practiced with, and used or expired items that were never replaced.

## Grab-and-go bag

This is the lightweight kit for leaving home. Ready.gov's basic list [1] is the base. Red Cross describes 3-day (72-hour) kits as built for the crucial first period after an emergency [12].

Pack, in this order:

1. **Water.** A 3-day supply at 1 gallon per person per day is 3 gallons, which weighs about 24 pounds (about 8 pounds per gallon, general practice). That is too heavy to carry far. If you will be in a vehicle, carry the full amount. If you may go on foot, carry what you can, and add a way to treat water (this guide's judgment). [02_water.md](02_water.md) covers boiling, bleach, tablets and filters.
2. **Food** for several days, no cooking needed, with a manual can opener if any is canned [1].
3. **The rest of the [Home supply](#home-supply-the-base-kit) list, in its lightest form** [1][13]: radio, flashlight or headlamp, batteries, phone with a charger and backup battery; first aid kit and medicines ([Special needs](#special-needs) covers how much); warmth (see [04_shelter.md](04_shelter.md)); the whistle, dust mask, plastic ties, wrench or pliers and matches; copies of documents, cash and local maps; hygiene items. Leave bulky items at home.
4. **A paper list of phone numbers** (general practice) [10].

**Weight.** Pack it, then carry it for a mile (this guide's judgment). If you cannot, cut weight. Keep things that do two jobs (general practice).

**What goes wrong:** the bag is too heavy to carry, or its batteries and medicines were never checked.

## Car kit

Ready.gov's car list [3]: jumper cables; flares or a reflective triangle; an ice scraper; a car cell phone charger; a blanket; a map; cat litter or sand for tire traction. Keep the gas tank full in case of evacuation or power outages [3].

Red Cross's 10 essentials for a car [11]: first aid kit; emergency radio; flashlight with spare batteries; jumper cables; water and non-perishable food; blankets and extra layers; tire repair kit with inflator; road flares or reflective triangles; shovel with sand; portable power bank. Winter additions: hand warmers, gloves, a reflective emergency sleeping bag or blanket [11].

For winter, Ready.gov also names a flashlight, warm clothes, bottled water and non-perishable snacks, and says to keep a full tank [4].

Before the season:

1. Have a mechanic check antifreeze, battery, brakes, and fuel and air filters [3].
2. Put on good winter tires with enough tread, or any chains or studs your area requires [3].
3. Pack the kit and put a charged phone charger in the car [6].

If you get stuck, stay in the car [4]. Do not run the engine for heat except by the rules in [04_shelter.md](04_shelter.md#vehicle-as-shelter) (exhaust pipe clear of snow, window cracked). Never sleep with the engine running.

**What goes wrong:** a phone charger that was never charged, and running the engine for heat with the exhaust pipe blocked. That is a carbon monoxide risk.

## The Ten Essentials for the outdoors

For a day hike or longer, carry these, not a home kit.

The National Park Service lists ten categories [27]:

1. Navigation: map, compass and GPS, with a paper map as backup
2. Sun protection
3. Extra clothing
4. Illumination: a flashlight and headlamp, plus extra batteries
5. First-aid supplies, checked for expiry
6. Fire: matches or lighter and fire starters
7. Repair kit and tools: duct tape, knife or multi-tool, scissors
8. Nutrition: an extra day of food, preferably no-cook
9. Hydration: water and treatment supplies
10. Emergency shelter: tent, tarp, bivy or emergency blanket

The Mountaineers' Ten Essential Systems list is close but differs in places [28]. It lists a headlamp separately and says to carry shelter at all times, plus extra food, water (or the means to purify it) and clothes [28]. Neither list replaces skill. NPS also says to tell someone where you are going and when to expect you back [27]. For navigation, see [07_navigation.md](07_navigation.md).

**What goes wrong:** you carry a map, compass or GPS and cannot use it [27], or the headlamp batteries or first-aid supplies are dead or expired.

## Special needs

**Medicines.** Ready.gov says to talk to a doctor or pharmacist about creating an emergency supply of medicines [8]. Sources differ on how many days. Ready.gov says several days [8], CDC's disability page says at least 1 week [15], and CDC's checklist for infants, pregnancy and children says at least 2 weeks [16]. The safer choice is the longer supply, if your insurer, prescriber and pharmacist allow it (this guide's judgment).

This guide never gives a dose or a way to stretch a prescription. Do not skip, split or ration a dose without a clinician. Questions go to a pharmacist, a prescriber or 911.

- Keep a list of prescriptions with diagnosis, dose, frequency, medical supply needs and allergies [8].
- FDA says the expiration date is part of deciding whether a product will work as intended, and if a medicine has expired, it may not be safe to use, so do not use it [23]. Store medicines in a cool, dry place, not a damp bathroom cabinet, and away from heat [23].
- For medicines that need cold, keep a cooler and chemical ice packs [8][15]. FDA says insulin vials and cartridges can stay unrefrigerated at 59 to 86 F for up to 28 days, must never be frozen, and should stay out of direct heat and sun [24]. More in [09_urban_survival.md](09_urban_survival.md#medicines-and-power-dependent-devices).

**Devices that need power.** Buy an extra battery for a power wheelchair or other battery-powered assistive device and keep it charged [8]. Include extra hearing-aid batteries, spare chargers, and backup oxygen if you use it [8][15]. Ask your doctor how to keep powered medical equipment running in an outage, and ask your power provider about a priority-restoration list [8]. Link: [09_urban_survival.md](09_urban_survival.md#medicines-and-power-dependent-devices). If you cannot power a device, go where there is power.

**Older adults.** Include items for your needs, batteries and chargers for health devices, and copies of Medicaid, Medicare and other insurance cards [9]. Plan how you will take assistive devices with you if you evacuate, and tell someone in your support network where the kit is [9].

**Infants and pregnancy.** CDC's list for infants includes diapers, wipes and diaper rash cream; bottles, nipples and powdered or ready-to-feed formula in case breast milk supply is affected; a breast milk cooler and a battery-operated or manual pump if you use one; a thermometer; extra clothes and blankets; and vaccination records [16]. For pregnancy, add prenatal vitamins and medicines, extra water, and a plan with your provider if the office or hospital is closed [16]. CDC gives no formula or diaper quantities [16].

**Pets.** CDC: a 2-week supply of food and water per animal, a 2-week supply of medicines with instructions, a 1-month supply of flea, tick and heartworm preventive, copies of vaccination and registration records, a leash, collar with ID and harness, a carrier, and litter for cats [17]. Ask your vet about a pet first aid kit [7][17].

## Gear that can hurt you

The full carbon monoxide rule is in [03_fire.md](03_fire.md#fire-indoors-in-tents-and-in-vehicles-the-carbon-monoxide-rule). Short version for buying decisions:

- **Never** run a generator in a home, garage, basement, crawlspace, shed or other enclosed space. Opening doors or windows is not enough [19][20]. Run it outside, at least 20 feet from windows, doors and vents, exhaust pointed away [5][19][18]. CPSC says to choose a model with a CO shut-off feature [20].
- **Camp stoves, grills and charcoal stay outdoors.** Never heat a home with a gas stovetop or oven [5][18].
- **CO alarms:** battery-operated or battery-backup alarms on every level and outside sleeping areas. Interconnected alarms are best [19]. Test monthly [20]. Replace per the maker's date or every 5 years [18]. Check detector batteries when you change your clocks [18].
- **Candles:** use flashlights, not candles, in an outage. If you must use a candle, keep it at least 12 inches from anything that burns, and put it out when you leave the room or go to sleep [21]. About 20 home candle fires are reported each day [21]. Battery-operated flameless candles are an option [21].
- **Space heaters (electric):** keep all sides at least 3 feet from anything that burns, on a flat stable surface, never unattended, plugged straight into a wall outlet [20]. They do not work in an outage.
- **Fuel for backup gear** goes outside the living space, away from heat (general practice).

## Keeping it working

Check radio, flashlights and food and water dates every three months (this guide's judgment); replace self-stored water every six months (Ready.gov) [2]; do a full kit review once a year (this guide's judgment). The six-month list below is the kit check that CDC and the Red Cross recommend [12][15][16]. Ready.gov says to reassess your needs every year, and to keep canned food in a cool, dry place and boxed food in tightly closed containers [1].

Every 6 months:

1. **Water.** Replace home-stored water that was not commercially bottled [2][29]. Follow the date on store-bought water [29].
2. **Batteries.** Put fresh ones in the flashlight and radio. Use the clock-change dates as a reminder (this guide's judgment).
3. **Medicines.** Check the dates. Replace anything expired [23]. Rotate in new stock before the old one goes.
4. **First aid kit.** Restock what you used and what expired [13].
5. **Food.** Eat the oldest, buy new. Do not eat from cans that are swollen, dented or corroded; throw them out [30]. See [09_urban_survival.md](09_urban_survival.md).
6. **Test everything.** Turn on the radio and the NOAA Weather Radio, run the flashlight, charge the power bank, and check the car kit.
7. **Update the paper.** Documents, phone numbers, insurance cards, and the plan [1][10].
8. **Look at the household.** New baby, new medicine, new pet? Update the kit [1].

After any use, restock right away [13]. Practice what is in the kit: run the radio, put on a tourniquet in class, light the stove outside (general practice).

## Scenario A: a family builds a home kit in one weekend

Two adults, a toddler and a dog. Money is limited. The power went out for a day last winter and nobody could find a flashlight.

**Saturday morning**
1. Sit down for 30 minutes. Write each person's needs: the toddler's formula and diapers, one adult's daily medicine, the dog's food [10][16][17].
2. Put what you already own in one place: flashlights, blankets, a can opener, tape, a first aid kit. Check batteries and dates [1].
3. Count water. Three people for 3 days at 1 gallon each per day is 9 gallons. For 2 weeks it is 42 [2][29]. Add water for the dog and for the toddler's formula on top of that [29][30]. Fill clean food-grade containers or buy bottled water (see [02_water.md](02_water.md#storing-water)). Write the date on each.

**Saturday afternoon**
4. Buy in the order of [What to do first](#what-to-do-first-in-order): water, flashlights and batteries, a hand-crank or battery radio that receives NOAA Weather Radio, a CO alarm for each level and outside bedrooms, first aid supplies, then shelf-stable food the family will actually eat [1][19][22]. With a toddler on formula, buy the formula with the water.
5. Ask the pharmacist about an emergency supply of the adult's medicine [8].

**Sunday**
6. Pack the kit in one or two containers by the door, with items in airtight bags [1]. Save copies of documents electronically and in a waterproof bag [1].
7. Put a calendar reminder 6 months out [15].
8. Run the radio and flashlights. Tell the family where the kit is [9].
9. Sign up for a first aid and tourniquet class (see [06_first_aid.md](06_first_aid.md#severe-bleeding)).

Result: 3 days of water and food, light, information and first aid, a plan, and a date to check it. Add the second week later.

## Scenario B: packing the car before a winter drive

A storm is forecast for Saturday. You will drive about two hours.

1. **Friday evening.** Fill the gas tank [3]. Check tires and tread, and have the antifreeze, battery and brakes looked at [3].
2. **Pack the car.** Use the [Car kit](#car-kit) lists: add a blanket per person, hand warmers, gloves and a charged portable phone charger [3][4][6][11].
3. **Check the plan.** Tell someone your route and when to expect you [27]. Charge the phone.
4. **If you get stuck.** Stay in the car [4]. Call 911 if you have a signal. Put on the blanket and layers. Use the engine only by the rules in [04_shelter.md](04_shelter.md#vehicle-as-shelter), with the exhaust pipe clear of snow.
5. **After the trip.** Restock whatever you used [13].

## Common mistakes

- Buying gear and never learning to use it. See the first-aid and tourniquet advice above.
- Never testing the radio, flashlight or charger. Test them now.
- Storing medicines in a hot car or damp bathroom [23].
- Water that is never replaced [2][29].
- Using a generator, grill, stove or heater inside. Read [03_fire.md](03_fire.md#fire-indoors-in-tents-and-in-vehicles-the-carbon-monoxide-rule).
- Relying on candles [21].
- No plan. A kit with no plan does not tell anyone where to go or who to call [10].
- Packing a bag too heavy to carry.

## Quick-reference card

**Home kit**
- **Plan first:** alerts, shelter, evacuation, how to reach each other, each person's needs [10].
- **Water:** 1 gallon per person per day, 3 days minimum, 2 weeks if you can. Replace home-stored non-commercial water every 6 months [2][29].
- **Food:** several days of non-perishable food and a manual can opener [1].
- **Light and radio:** a flashlight per person, extra batteries, a battery or hand-crank radio, a NOAA Weather Radio with tone alert, phone chargers and a backup battery [1][5].
- **First aid:** kit at home and in the car, plus a tourniquet and training [13][26]. Tourniquet: at least 2 to 3 inches from the wound, between the wound and the heart, never on a joint, note the time [25]. Steps: [06_first_aid.md](06_first_aid.md#severe-bleeding).
- **Warmth:** a sleeping bag or warm blanket per person, a change of clothes, sturdy shoes [1].
- **Documents and cash:** copies of important documents, electronic or in a waterproof container; cash [1].
- **Hygiene:** soap, hand sanitizer, wipes, moist towelettes, garbage bags [1].
- **Tools:** a wrench or pliers for the shutoffs, duct tape, plastic sheeting [1].
- **Also:** whistle, dust mask, matches in a waterproof container, fire extinguisher [1].
- **CO alarms:** every level and outside bedrooms, battery or battery backup, replace per the maker or every 5 years [18][19].
- **Medicines:** talk to your doctor or pharmacist; several days to 2 weeks depending on source. OTC: label dose only. Expired medicine: do not use it [8][15][16][23].

**Also keep in mind**
- **Insulin:** vials and cartridges, unrefrigerated at 59 to 86 F up to 28 days. Never frozen [24].
- **Devices:** spare battery for a powered assistive device; ask provider about backup power [8].
- **Pets:** 2 weeks of food and water, 1 month of flea, tick and heartworm preventive [17].
- **Car:** full tank; the [Car kit](#car-kit) list [3][11].
- **Stuck in snow:** stay in the car [4].
- **Generators:** outdoors only, at least 20 ft from windows, doors and vents [19][20].
- **Grills, camp stoves, charcoal:** outdoors only, never inside a home, garage or tent [5][18].
- **Candles:** flashlights instead [21].
- **Check the kit:** radio, flashlights, food and water dates every 3 months (this guide's judgment); replace self-stored water every 6 months [2]; kit check every 6 months [12][15][16]; full kit review yearly (this guide's judgment) [1].
