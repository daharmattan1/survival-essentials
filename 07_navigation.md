# Navigation - Staying Found and Finding Your Way

Use this guide before a trip, to avoid getting lost, and when you think you already are. Most navigation trouble is walking farther after you stopped knowing where you were. What hurts you first, in this order:

1. **Moving while lost.** Searchers look along the route you told someone about. "Changing location will make it difficult for authorities to find you" [2].
2. **Nightfall.** A wrong turn at dusk turns a day hike into a night out. Yosemite rangers describe hikers who left the trail near dark and could not be located until morning [4].
3. **Cold, wet and fog.** Hypothermia (dangerously low body temperature) can set in at 50 F when you are wet and cold. In rain, snow or fog you may be safer staying put than traveling [5].
4. **Terrain.** Cliffs, ledges and steep ground off the trail. A 20-year-old visitor who went to scramble on boulders near the Lower Yosemite Fall footbridge went missing, and was found about 48 hours later after a fall of more than 50 feet [4].
5. **Cold water.** Gasping starts in the first 2 to 3 minutes and muscle control can fail within seconds [17].

First aid and cold-water advice here is the commonly taught field response when help is far away. It is not medical advice. If you can reach 911, call.

- You are lost or hurt and your phone has any signal. Give your GPS coordinates if you have them, the trail or area name, your last known location, nearby landmarks, your clothing colors, your equipment and any medical conditions [2]. If you are hurt and have no signal, flag down another visitor and ask them to call from a place with reception [2].
- Someone went into cold water, or is shivering, confused or drowsy afterward. Steps: [`04_shelter.md`](04_shelter.md#someone-is-too-cold-hypothermia-and-frostbite).

## If you think you are lost right now

Do [STOP](01_core_principles.md#stop-stop-think-observe-plan) first. This guide adds the navigation part of "think."

1. **Name the last place you were sure of.** A junction, a lake, a sign. Note the time and the direction you have walked since (this guide's judgment).
2. **Go down this list and take the first that fits.**
   - **Immediate threat to your life:** move only if there is an immediate threat to life [2]. Examples and the rule to go only as far as the nearest safer spot are this guide's judgment: rising water, fire, rockfall, lightning on a ridge. Then start this list again. Lightning: [thunder](#thunder-get-off-the-ridge).
   - **Stay put** if it is near nightfall, you are hurt or worn out, or you are not very, very confident of the route [1]. Bad weather such as rain, snow or fog is also a reason to stay [5]. This is the default.
   - **Retrace your steps** only if it is daylight, you are not hurt, and you can see the way back, such as the trail or your own tracks [2]. Fog that hides the way back means stay put. If you have not reached a known spot in about 30 minutes, stop and stay put (this guide's rule).
   - **Navigate with map and compass** only if you can place yourself on the map within one landmark, there is enough daylight to finish, and nobody is hurt (this guide's judgment). The rest of this guide covers how.
3. **Then be found.** Signals: [`04_shelter.md`](04_shelter.md#be-found-from-the-shelter) and [`08_communication.md`](08_communication.md).

**What goes wrong.**

- "I'll go a little further to check." Each check moves you off the last place you were sure of.
- Following a line of rocks. Three Yosemite hikers followed what they thought were cairns (stacked rocks) and got stuck on a ledge system overnight [4].
- Leaving a trail. If you are on a trail, stay on it. Trails are marked at intersections, though signs can be damaged or stolen [1]. Yosemite rangers call the developed trail the shortest, safest path back [4].

## Before you go

The best navigation is the part you do at home.

1. **Leave a trip plan** with someone who is not going: trailhead, route, return time, your vehicle and where it is parked, and group size [1][2].
2. **Carry a paper map of the area and a compass, and practice with them** [1][2]. The USGS publishes US Topo maps at 1:24,000 scale as free downloads you can print [9].
3. **Do not count on your phone.** There may be no cell coverage [3]. Searching for a signal drains the battery, so use airplane mode until you need to call [3]. Download an offline map and carry a spare battery (general practice). Consider a personal locator beacon [3]. Phone GPS is typically accurate to about 16 feet (4.9 m) under open sky, worse near buildings, bridges and trees [15].
4. **Write the declination on your map** [12]. See [compass and declination](#compass-and-declination).
5. **Set a turnaround time and keep to it.** Plan for uphill to take at least twice as long as the downhill return [3].
6. **Check the forecast for the specific area.** In mountains, thunderstorms usually develop in the early afternoon, so hike early [19].
7. **Carry a headlamp, whistle and warm jacket even on a short, well-marked trail** [4]. Kit list: [`11_essential_gear.md`](11_essential_gear.md).

**What goes wrong.** A map and compass you have never used. A phone map that needs a connection.

## Read a topographic map

A topographic ("topo") map shows the shape of the ground with contour lines.

- **Contour lines** are brown lines joining points of equal elevation. They never cross [8].
- **Close together means steep. Far apart means gentle or level** [8].
- **Contour interval** is the height difference between neighboring lines, printed in the margin. It can be 10 feet or less on flat ground and 100 feet or more in mountains [8].
- **Index contours** are wider and carry the elevation number.
- **Colors:** brown contours, blue water, green vegetation, black for most roads, trails and buildings, red for land grids and important roads. Symbols differ slightly between maps, so read the legend [8].
- **Scale:** 1:24,000 means one inch on the map is 24,000 inches on the ground, or 2,000 feet (arithmetic from [9]). To measure distance, mark a strip of paper at two points and lay it on the bar scale [6].

**Reading the shapes** (Army definitions [6]):

- **Ridge:** a line of high ground. Contour U's or V's point away from high ground, downhill.
- **Valley or draw (a small stream gully):** U's or V's point toward high ground, uphill and upstream. The closed ends of the V's show which way a stream flows.
- **Saddle:** a dip between two high spots, drawn like an hourglass.
- **Cliff:** contours so close they merge or touch.

**What goes wrong.** Ignoring the interval, so a "short" climb is 800 feet. Walking toward lines that merge. Trusting an old map (general practice).

## Compass and declination

A compass needle points to magnetic north (where the needle settles, which is not the North Pole). **Declination** is the angle between magnetic north and true north (the direction of the North Pole). It differs from place to place and changes over time [12]. Maps are drawn to true north or grid north (the direction of the map's vertical grid lines). Ignore declination and you walk a line off by that many degrees.

A **bearing** (the Army says azimuth [7]) is a direction in degrees from north, 0 to 360.

**How much it matters.** One degree of error moves you about 18 m sideways for every 1,000 m walked [7].

**Get the number.**

- Read the declination diagram in the map margin, and check the map's date. The Army says diagrams older than 20 years can be unreliable [7]. Over 20 years, one Alaska post changed by 5 degrees and one Georgia post by 2 degrees [7].
- Better: use NOAA's declination calculator for your place and date [12][14]. The model behind it is revised about every five years [13][14]. In rare places declination changes sharply over a mile or two, and near the magnetic poles it is unreliable [13].
- East declination is positive and west is negative [12].
- If an old map diagram and NOAA's calculator differ, use NOAA's current value (this guide's judgment, following TC 3-25.26's caution about diagrams over 20 years old). Grid north and true north can differ slightly (this guide's judgment).

**Apply it.**

| Declination | Map bearing to compass bearing | Compass bearing to map bearing |
|-------------|-------------------------------|-------------------------------|
| West (negative) | Add | Subtract |
| East (positive) | Subtract | Add |

Sources: [7][10]. Army example: a 14-degree west angle takes a map azimuth of 93 to a compass reading of 107 [7]. Adding when you should subtract doubles your error (this guide's arithmetic). A compass with adjustable declination lets you set the offset once [11].

**Compass parts** (general practice). The **baseplate** is the flat plate you lay on the map, with a direction-of-travel arrow. The **dial** is the turning ring marked 0 to 360. The **orienting arrow** is the outlined arrow inside the dial. The **index mark** is the fixed line where the dial meets the travel arrow.

**Orient the map** (line it up with the land) [10]:

1. Keep metal away from the compass, including steel hidden in concrete [10]. The Army suggests about 55 m from high-tension power lines, 18 m from a truck, and 10 m from barbed wire and phone or telegraph wire [7].
2. Lay the baseplate on the map with its edge along the map's north-south border line, travel arrow toward the top of the map. Turn the dial so its north agrees with map north [10].
3. Correct for declination with the table above, unless your compass has a declination adjustment that is already set (then leave the dial at north). Treat map north as a map bearing of 0 and convert it. West: add, so 10 degrees west means turning the dial to put 10 at the index mark. East: subtract, so 10 degrees east puts 350 there (this guide's arithmetic).
4. Keep the baseplate edge on the map's north-south border line. Rotate the map and compass together in one motion until the red needle overlays the orienting arrow [10].
5. Compare the features around you to the map to find where you are [10].

**No compass?** Line up a straight feature that shows on both map and ground, such as a road. Check with a second feature, because one line can leave the map reversed [6].

**What goes wrong.** The wrong sign. An old diagram. A compass tilted or near metal. Not resetting declination in a new area (general practice).

## Take and follow a bearing

**From the map, with a baseplate compass** (general practice, with the declination rule from [7][10]):

1. Lay the long edge of the baseplate along the line from where you are to where you want to go, direction-of-travel arrow toward the goal.
2. Turn the dial until its north points to map north and its lines run parallel to the map's north-south lines. The number at the index mark is the map bearing.
3. Apply declination to get the compass bearing (table above), then turn the dial so the compass bearing is at the index mark. Example: map bearing 250, declination 10 degrees west, so turn the dial to 260.
4. Lift the compass off the map and hold it flat. Turn your whole body until the red needle sits in the orienting arrow. The travel arrow now points the way.

**Follow it with steering marks.** Do not walk staring at the compass. Sight along the arrow to a distinctive object on the line, put the compass away, walk to it, then repeat [6]. A good mark is hard to mistake, is the farthest or highest object you can see on the line, and stays in view [6]. If nothing is ahead, sight on something behind you along the reverse line [6]. After a detour, count only steps toward the goal [6].

**Count paces to measure distance.**

- An Army pace is one natural step, about 30 inches [7].
- The Army's example: a person who normally takes 120 paces to walk 100 m may need 130 or more going up a slope [7]. Counting only each right-foot step halves those numbers (general practice).
- Calibrate on a measured stretch like the ground you will cross [7].
- A pace lengthens downhill and with a tail wind. It shortens uphill, in a head wind, on sand, gravel, mud or snow, in falling rain, snow or ice, and in fog or darkness. Heavy clothing and poor boots also affect it [7].
- Keep count with a pebble moved to a pocket every 100 m, not in your head [7].

**Reset at every confirmed position.** Errors add up. Confirm your position at every feature that is also on the map and restart the pace count there [6].

**Aim off on purpose.** If your goal is on or beside a long feature that crosses your path, such as a road, stream or ridge, aim about 10 degrees to one side [6]. When you reach the feature you know which way to turn. Ten degrees over 1,000 m puts you about 180 m to one side [7].

**What goes wrong.** A "mark" that looks like everything else. A pace count calibrated on flat ground, used on hills [7]. Forgetting to count after a detour. Keeping a bearing too long: dead reckoning (navigating by bearing and distance alone) is highly accurate only over short distances [6].

## Handrails, catching features and locating yourself

Terrain association means comparing what the map says you should see with what you see. The Army calls it more forgiving than dead reckoning, because a mistake shows up as a mismatch [6].

- **Handrail:** a long feature running roughly along your direction of travel, such as a stream, ridge, road or power line. Keep it on one side and you need only rough compass work [6].
- **Catching feature:** a prominent feature near where your route turns or ends. It also tells you that you have gone too far [6].

**Find your position.** Orient the map first [6][10]. Then use a resection.

**Resection** (locating yourself from bearings): pick two or three distant features you can identify on both land and map. Take a compass bearing to each and convert it to a map bearing. Turn each into a back bearing by adding or subtracting 180 (general practice), and draw it on the map from the feature toward you. Where the lines cross is you. Three lines beat two [7].

**What goes wrong.** The handrail splits or ends and you keep walking. A feature too small for the map. Using one feature and getting the map reversed [6].

## Direction without a compass

These methods give rough direction. Use them to hold a line or orient a map, never as a reason to leave a place you should stay. Treat each as approximate and cross-check with a second method (this guide's judgment).

**The sun.** It rises roughly east and sets roughly west, but rarely due east or west [6]. North of 23.4 degrees north it is due south at local noon and shadows point north. South of 23.4 degrees south, the reverse [6].

**Shadow-tip method** [6]:

1. Push a stick into the ground where it casts a clear shadow. The ground can slope. Mark the tip of the shadow with a stone.
2. Wait 10 to 15 minutes, until the tip has moved a few inches. Mark the new tip.
3. Draw a straight line through both marks. This is an approximate east-west line. The first mark is west.
4. Stand with the first mark on your left. North is in front of you, east on your right, south behind you.

It does not work beyond 66.5 degrees latitude north or south [6]. A longer wait gives a better line (this guide's judgment).

**Watch method (analog watch, Northern Hemisphere temperate zone)** [6]: hold the watch flat and point the hour hand at the sun. South is midway between the hour hand and 12 (use 1 o'clock on daylight saving time). In the Southern Hemisphere temperate zone, point 12 at the sun, and north is midway between 12 and the hour hand (use 1 o'clock instead of 12 on daylight saving time). The Army says this method can be inaccurate, especially in the lower latitudes, and may cause circling [6].

**Polaris, the North Star (Northern Hemisphere)** [6][16]:

1. Find the Big Dipper. The two stars on the outer edge of its bowl are the pointer stars.
2. Extend a line through them, from the lower to the upper, about five times the distance between them. Polaris lies along it [6].
3. Check: the Big Dipper and Cassiopeia (a W-shaped group of five stars) sit on opposite sides of Polaris [6].
4. Facing Polaris, you face true north, not magnetic north [16].

Polaris is not the brightest star [16]. It moves only about 1 degree around the pole [6]. Its height above the horizon about equals your latitude [6]. It is no use in the Southern Hemisphere [16].

**Methods that do not work.**

- **Moss on the north side of trees.** The Army says moss cannot be relied upon to show north, because it grows all the way around some trees [6].
- **Tree rings.** The Army says rings are wider on the side toward the equator, and tells you to compare several stumps [6]. This guide treats that as a tiebreaker at most, never a way to set a course (this guide's judgment).
- **Ant hills and other folk signs.** No source supports them.

**What goes wrong.** Forgetting daylight saving time. Mistaking another star for Polaris. Trusting one sign when two disagree.

## Rivers and streams: following and crossing

**Following water downstream is a last resort, not a plan.** The Forest Service calls it "a very last resort": a difficult path that could lead to a trail or road, but "could be very dangerous" [1]. Olympic National Park calls rivers and creeks among the most dangerous routes there [5]. Stay put.

**Cold water is the risk.** In the first 2 to 3 minutes or longer, sudden immersion can cause gasping and rapid breathing, and drowning if you inhale water. Cold shock can be as severe at 50 to 60 F as at 35 F. Muscle control can start to fail within seconds. Hypothermia begins at a core temperature of 95 F [17]. Six inches of fast-moving water can topple a person [18].

**When not to cross.**

- Water above your knees: do not cross [20].
- Not comfortable for any reason: do not cross. Never cross above rapids or falls [21].
- Rain can raise a river within hours. Snowmelt peaks in afternoon and early evening [21]. Levels can change by about a foot between morning and late afternoon [20].
- Turning back may be your best option [20]. If you are lost, stay put.

**If you must cross** (for example, to escape an immediate threat):

1. **Scout first.** Look downstream for log jams, waterfalls and other traps. Strainers (downed trees and debris that trap people in moving water) are among the most dangerous river hazards. Pick a crossing, preferably where the river splits into several channels, and identify an exit point in case you fall [20].
2. **Cross in the morning**, when levels are usually lowest [20].
3. **Loosen the pack's hip belt and chest strap** so you can shed it quickly. A heavy pack does not float and can snag [20][21]. Never tie yourself in [5].
4. **Keep boots on.** Use a stick or trekking pole for two points of contact. Face upstream, angle across, take short steps, and test each foothold. A soft bottom where rocks seem to drop away is a deep hole, so go back. In a group, cross linked, strongest person upstream [5][20][21].
5. **After the crossing**, you are wet: [`04_shelter.md`](04_shelter.md#stop-heat-loss-ground-wet-and-wind).

## Thunder: get off the ridge

A tent offers no protection from lightning. Avoid hilltops and ridge tops, open fields and tall isolated trees, and wait 30 minutes after the last thunder before moving out [19]. The only completely safe places are a building with plumbing and electricity or a hard-topped metal vehicle [19]; see [`04_shelter.md`](04_shelter.md). More: [`04_shelter.md`](04_shelter.md#check-the-site-before-you-build).

## Worked scenario A: fog on a ridge with a map and compass

You are on a broad, gentle ridge trail. At 1 pm, fog drops. You can see about 30 feet. The trail has faded into grass. Your last sure position is a saddle (a dip in the ridge) about 20 minutes back. You have a topo map, a baseplate compass, a jacket and a whistle. Your phone has no signal.

1. **Stop** [1]. Put on your jacket. Note the time.
2. **Stay put while the fog is down.** In fog you may be safer staying put [5]. Insulate from the ground, add layers and signal. Steps: [`04_shelter.md`](04_shelter.md#be-found-from-the-shelter).
3. **When the fog lifts and you can see the way back, retrace.** Retracing in daylight is allowed if you are not hurt [2]. If it takes more than about 30 minutes, stop and stay (this guide's rule).
4. **If the grass still hides the trail but the ridge is clear, use the compass.** Use it only with enough daylight to finish. The ridge is your handrail: keep it on one side [6].
5. **Get the bearing.** Example: map declination 10 degrees west. The line on the map from you back to the saddle reads 250. West, map to compass, add: 250 + 10 = 260 [7]. Turn the dial so 260 is at the index mark.
6. **Sight and walk.** Use a boulder or shrub clump on the line as each mark. Walk to each in turn [6]. The map distance is about 640 m. At the Army's example of 120 paces per 100 m (use your own count), that is about 770 paces. If any fog remains, your step shortens, so expect more [7].
7. **Confirm at the saddle.** Its contours form an hourglass [6]. If you reach your count and see no dip, stop. Stay put (this guide's rule).
8. **At the saddle**, you are back on the trail. Walk down. If the fog drops again, stop and stay put.

**What goes wrong:** setting out while the fog is still down. Walking on past where the trail faded.

## Worked scenario B: the phone dies at dusk on an unfamiliar trail

You followed a phone map on a trail you do not know. At 5:30 pm the phone dies. Sunset is about 45 minutes away. You have a light jacket, a bottle of water and no paper map. The trailhead is about 2 miles back, and you have been walking mostly downhill.

1. **Stop** [1]. Put on the jacket. Try to restart the phone. If it comes on, call 911 and give coordinates, trail and last known location [2]. Try a text if a call will not go through (general practice). Between tries, keep it warm in an inside pocket (general practice).
2. **Decide: stay or go.** It is near nightfall, you have no light and no map. The Forest Service says to stay put at nightfall [1]. Uphill takes at least twice as long as downhill [3], so the walk out is a long climb in the dark. With a short, obvious walk and a light, walking out would be reasonable (this guide's judgment).
3. **Do not leave the trail.** It is where searchers will look [1][4].
4. **Shelter on the spot.** Pick a site beside the trail, out of drainages and away from dead trees. Steps: [`04_shelter.md`](04_shelter.md#worked-scenario-a-lost-day-hiker-at-dusk-in-cold-rain).
5. **Signal.** Blow your whistle often instead of shouting [2]. The NPS pattern is three blasts, a break, then three again [22]. In the morning, if you are not found and you are unhurt and can see the way, retrace the trail in daylight [2].

**What goes wrong:** the phone was the only map. Carry a paper map, a headlamp and a battery pack [3][4].

## Common mistakes

- **Walking on after you are unsure.** See [if you think you are lost](#if-you-think-you-are-lost-right-now).
- **Skipping declination, or using the wrong sign.** See [compass and declination](#compass-and-declination).
- **Treating moss, tree growth or ant hills as a compass.** See [methods that do not work](#direction-without-a-compass).
- **Following a stream out, or crossing above the knee or in the afternoon.** See [rivers and streams](#rivers-and-streams-following-and-crossing).

- **Relying on the phone alone** [3], or on one method [6].
- **Walking at night or in fog** when you could wait [1][5], or following cairns off a trail [4].

## Quick-reference card (print on one page)

**Lost**

- **Immediate threat to life** (rising water, fire, rockfall, lightning on a ridge): move to the nearest safer spot only.
- **Stay put** if it is near nightfall, you are hurt or worn out, or you are not very, very confident of the route.
- **Bad weather** (rain, snow, fog): stay put. In fog, wait for it to lift.
- **Retrace** only in daylight, when not hurt, and when you can see the way.
- **No known spot in about 30 minutes:** stop and stay put.
- **Any phone signal:** call 911 with coordinates, trail or area name, last known location, landmarks, clothing, gear, medical conditions.

**Before you go**

- **Trip plan:** leave it with someone who is not going.
- **Carry:** paper map, compass, headlamp, whistle, warm jacket, spare phone battery.
- **Turnaround time:** uphill takes at least twice as long as downhill.

**Map and compass**

- **Declination sign:** east is positive, west is negative.
- **West declination:** add going map to compass, subtract going compass to map.
- **East declination:** subtract going map to compass, add going compass to map.

**No compass**

- **Shadow tip:** mark, wait 10 to 15 minutes, mark again. First mark is west. Not valid beyond 66.5 degrees latitude.
- **Sun, watch and Polaris:** see [direction without a compass](#direction-without-a-compass). Do not trust moss, tree rings or ant hills.

**Streams and thunder**

- **Do not follow a stream out.**
- **Do not cross** above the knee or above rapids. Cross early in the day.
- **Cold shock** can start in the first 2 to 3 minutes.
- **Six inches** of fast-moving water can topple you.
- **Thunder:** get off hilltops and ridges. A tent gives no protection; a building with plumbing and wiring or a hard-topped vehicle does. Wait 30 minutes after the last thunder.
