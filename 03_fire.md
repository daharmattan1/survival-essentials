# Fire - Staying Warm, Staying Alive, Not Starting a Second Emergency

Fire keeps you warm, dries your clothes, and gets you found. It can also kill you. This guide gets you a working fire fast, and tells you when not to light one.

**The one rule that matters most: fire goes OUTSIDE.** Never burn anything in a tent, car, garage, or house. The only exception is a stove or fireplace built to vent and kept in working order. Opening a flap or a window does not fix this [1][18].

## When you need this guide, and what goes wrong

Use this guide when you are cold and wet, night is coming, the power is out in winter, or you need to signal for help. If you are cold and wet right now, get out of the wind and rain and into dry clothes first, then fire [15][10].

Three things go wrong around fire.

**1. Cold.** Hypothermia can start above 40 F if rain, sweat, or cold water has you chilled [15]. CDC counted an average of 1,301 US deaths a year associated with excessive natural cold (1999-2011) [16]. Being indoors does not protect you. In Wisconsin in 2014, about a third of hypothermia deaths were found indoors, some in homes with broken or unused furnaces [17].

**2. Carbon monoxide (CO).** CO is made whenever fuel burns. You cannot see or smell it. More than 400 Americans die each year from accidental CO poisoning not linked to fires [1]. In Georgia in 1999, six people died in two zipped tents. One tent had a propane stove running. In the other, a charcoal grill had apparently been brought in to provide warmth [18]. People who are asleep or drunk can die before they feel any symptoms [1].

**3. The wildfire you start.** Nearly 85% of US wildland fires are human-caused. Campfires left unattended are one of the common human causes [8].

If you do not need a fire, or cannot make it safe, do not light it.

### Warming a cold person

- Get them out of wind and rain first. Take wet clothes off only once you are somewhere warm and sheltered; until then, add dry layers and a waterproof layer over them (see [`04_shelter.md`](04_shelter.md#stop-heat-loss-ground-wet-and-wind)).
- Sleepiness or confusion in a cold person: call 911 (see [`06_first_aid.md`](06_first_aid.md)).
- Warm the middle of the body (chest, neck, head, groin), skin to skin under dry blankets. Wrap the head and neck [15].
- Give a warm, high-calorie drink without alcohol only if the person is alert, shivering and can swallow normally. If they are confused, drowsy or not shivering, give nothing by mouth (see [`04_shelter.md`](04_shelter.md#stop-heat-loss-ground-wet-and-wind)). CDC: no alcohol, and no drinks for an unconscious person [15].
- Below 95 F body temperature means medical care [15].
- Do not warm numb hands or feet against a fire or stove. Numb skin burns without hurting [15].

More in [`06_first_aid.md`](06_first_aid.md).

## Decide first: do you need a fire, where, and with what

Work down this list in order.

1. **Are you inside a tent, vehicle, garage, or house?** Do not light a fire there. Go to the [carbon monoxide rule](#fire-indoors-in-tents-and-in-vehicles-the-carbon-monoxide-rule).
2. **Are you in danger right now?** Cold and wet, night coming, or no heat at home: yes. Warm, dry, and rescue close: probably not.
3. **Shelter and dry clothes come first.** Fire does not replace them. Wet clothing is a big part of the problem [15][10].
4. **Is fire allowed here?** Check for burn bans and fire restrictions [2][5][6]. See [burn bans](#burn-bans-and-what-a-fire-can-cost-you): the example Forest Service order cited there (Tahoe National Forest, 2026) lists no survival or emergency exemption [26]. If a ban is on, check whether it covers stoves too (many do). Otherwise rely on layers and shelter.
5. **Is it safe to light?** Not in dry or windy conditions [2][4], and not under low branches, on a steep slope, or in dry grass [4][6].
6. **Consider a stove.** For cooking, a camp stove used outdoors is lower-impact than a wood fire [9]. It will not warm you the way a fire does. Never use a stove or candle lantern inside a tent [21].
7. **Pick your ignition.** A lighter is the easiest tool. A ferro rod (a metal rod you scrape with a steel edge to throw hot sparks) is the most versatile if you have practiced with it. Then matches. Lens only in bright sun. Friction last [10].

## Pick and prepare the site

Pick the site before you gather wood.

1. **Choose flat ground, open overhead, out of the wind.** Stay at least 15 ft from tents, brush, and low-hanging branches. Keep the space above the fire open: at least three times the flame height [2].
2. **Avoid steep slopes and the base of a hill.** An escaped fire runs uphill fast [4]. Also avoid rotten stumps, logs, and dry grass or leaves [6], and snow-laden trees [10].
3. **Clear a circle about 10 ft across** of litter and anything that burns [2][7]. In a true emergency, the bare minimum is about 3 ft of bare soil [10].
4. **Dig down through the duff to bare dirt.** Duff is the layer of rotting needles and leaves on the ground. It lets even a small ember smolder for days, mostly underground [4].
5. **Rocks: be careful.** Wet or porous rock (full of tiny holes that hold water) can crack or burst when heated. Never use wet or porous rock [10][11]. Sources disagree here. One says ring the fire with rocks [2]. Another says do not line the pit with rocks because they can explode [4]. The cautious choice is a ring of bare dirt, with rock only if it is dry and not porous.
6. **On snow or wet ground, build a base.** On snow, lay two layers of green logs, with the top layer crosswise. On wet ground, use flat wood or non-porous rock. This keeps your tinder out of the wet [10].
7. **Add a reflector.** A wall of logs or non-porous rock behind the fire throws heat toward you [10].
8. **Put water and a shovel next to the fire before you light it** [4][5]. Keep spare wood away from the flames [7].
9. **Never burn trash, aerosol cans, pressurized containers, batteries, glass, or aluminum** [2]. Petroleum smoke is toxic [10].

**How this goes wrong:** a ground fire spreading through duff, a spark landing on a tent, a gust of wind. If you see any of these building, put the fire out.

## Build it: tinder, kindling, fuel

Stage everything before you strike anything [10]. This guide's judgment from general practice, not a counted figure: most failed fires fail at this step, not at the spark.

The Army manual sorts fuel into three sizes [10]:

- **Tinder:** dry, fine, fluffy material. Enough to fill a cupped hand.
- **Kindling:** pencil lead thickness, then pencil thickness, then thumb thickness. Pieces 12 to 18 inches long. Gather at least a bundle the size of a soda can for each size.
- **Fuel:** at least as thick as your forearm.

**Tinder that works** [10]: birch bark, dry grass, ferns or moss, seed down (milkweed, cattail, thistle), feather sticks (a dry stick shaved into thin curls that stay attached), dryer lint, paper, cotton balls with petroleum jelly. Dead pine needles work as kindling.

**Char cloth** is cotton cooked without air until it is black. Put cotton cloth in a sealed tin with a small nail hole in the lid. Heat the tin in a fire until the smoke stops. Take it off the fire and let it cool before you open it, away from flame. Opening a hot tin lets in air, and the cloth can flare. Keep it dry [10][11].

**Softwood versus hardwood.** Softwood such as pine lights fast, burns fast, and makes more smoke. It is good kindling. Dense dry hardwood such as oak gives more heat and lasts longer [10][24]. Wood above 20% moisture is harder to light and gives less heat [24].

**Dry wood?** Dead branches that snap when broken are dry. Standing dead wood is usually dry even in rain. Rotten wood on the ground smolders and is poor fuel. Split solid fallen wood to reach the driest inner wood [10].

### The lays (ways to stack the wood)

Add kindling in handfuls, with gaps for air. A thick blanket of kindling smothers a small flame [10].

**Teepee.** Fast to start. Outer sticks fall inward and feed the fire. Burns well even with wet wood [10]. Tinder in the middle, kindling leaned in a cone, and a gap on the side you will light.

```
        /\
       /  \
      / ** \
     /      \
    /__    __\
```

**Lean-to.** Push a green stick into the ground at about 30 degrees, pointing in the direction the wind blows. Put the tinder under the stick. Lean kindling against it [10]. **Light it from the upwind side**, so the wind pushes the flame into the wood and not away from it [10][11].

**Pyramid.** Lay a row of small logs or branches side by side. Lay a second, smaller row crosswise on top, and keep going, each layer smaller than the last, with the starter fire on top. It burns downward, so it needs less tending [10][11]. Never leave it unattended.

```
        * *
      =======
     ===========
    =============
```

**Log cabin.** Stack logs in a square, with tinder and kindling in the middle. Big light and heat, and a bed of coals fast. Also a base for a signal fire [10].

### When the flame keeps dying

- **Flame dies in seconds:** the tinder is damp or packed too tight. Dry it against your body and fluff it [10].
- **Fire dies after the tinder burns:** you need more small kindling first [10].
- **Smoke but no flame:** the kindling is smothered. Open gaps [10].
- **Weak heat:** the wood is too wet or green. Split to the inside and use dead wood [10][24].

At high altitude, one lab study found fires burn at a lower rate [28]. This guide's inference, not the study's: give yourself extra tinder and time.

## Light it: ignition ranked, with failure modes

**Always light from the upwind side** to protect the flame [10][11]. **Carry at least two methods.** Every lighter and match is a limited supply [10].

1. **Lighter.** The easiest tool for most people. A butane lighter works better in wind than a liquid-fuel one. In extreme cold the fuel can freeze, so keep it warm against your body [10]. Keep it dry.
2. **Ferro rod (metal match).** It is not affected by wet or cold [10]. Depending on its quality and your skill, the Army says one rod can start thousands of fires. It is the most useful tool if you are trained in it [10]. Technique [10]:
   1. Set the rod's end on a base right beside the tinder.
   2. Hold the striker (the back of a knife blade with a sharp 90-degree edge works) at 90 degrees to the rod.
   3. Drive the striker down the rod with even pressure.
   4. Stop before the striker reaches the tinder, so you do not disturb it.

   Failure modes: a dull striker edge, scattered sparks, knocked-over tinder.
3. **Matches.** Use waterproof matches in a waterproof case with a striker. Dipping heads in wax helps. Each match that fizzles or blows out is one fewer fire [10]. Hold the match head-down so the flame climbs the stick, and set it at the bottom edge of the tinder without smothering it [10]. Failure modes: damp matches, wind.
4. **Lens.** A lens from binoculars, a camera, a scope, or a magnifier works only in bright direct sun. Hold the bright dot on one spot of tinder until it smolders [10][11].
5. **Battery and steel wool (last resort).** Stretch fine steel wool across a battery's posts so it glows, then move the coal to tinder [10]. A 9-volt battery can start a fire on its own if metal touches both posts, so keep it away from steel wool, foil, and keys [27]. A vehicle battery gives off explosive hydrogen. Keep flame away [10].
6. **Friction (last resort).** Bow drill, hand drill, and fire plow take a lot of practice and energy [10][11]. Use a seasoned (dried) hardwood drill on a seasoned softwood board. Avoid resinous (sappy) woods [10].

**Never pour gasoline or any flammable liquid on a fire** [10].

## Keep it going, including overnight

- **Keep it small** [2]. Add fuel only after the flame is steady.
- **Never leave a fire alone**, "even for a few minutes or if you take a nap" [4]. Even a light breeze can spread it [7].
- **Dry clothes with care.** Hold the back of your hand where the clothes would hang. If it stays comfortable for three seconds or more, the fabric will not scorch there. Keep clothes from falling into the flame. Polypropylene melts near fire [10].
- **Do not bank a fire under soil.** A buried fire can smolder for hours, then escape [4][7]. A fire inside a shelter can cause carbon monoxide poisoning [11].

**Overnight**

- **Default: put the fire out before you sleep or leave** [4][5]. Prefer warmth that does not burn: a better shelter, ground insulation, dry layers, and a reflector wall (build it before the fire is hot, since that is easier [10]) [15].
- **If the choice is a night fire or freezing** (this guide's judgment, not an agency rule): someone stays awake with it, in shifts. Keep it in a cleared pit, at least 15 ft from any tent or shelter [2], with water and a shovel beside it. The Army describes a pyramid lay that burns downward and needs little tending through the night [10]. That saves fuel and effort. It does not make an unattended fire safe. You are taking a risk and may be responsible if it escapes (see [burn bans](#burn-bans-and-what-a-fire-can-cost-you)).

## Put it out

Use "drown, stir, drown, feel" [2][3]:

1. **Drown** the fire with water until the hissing stops. Dirt or sand alone will not do it [2][3].
2. **Stir** the ashes and coals. Scrape sticks and logs so no embers stay exposed, not just the red ones [3].
3. **Drown** it again.
4. **Feel.** Hover the back of your hand over the ashes. Do not touch them. If you feel any heat, keep drowning and stirring [2][3][6].
5. **Check under the rocks** around the ring [6]. Look within 50 ft for escaped embers [4].

The ground under the fire should be cool to the touch before you leave [10].

**With no water,** mix plenty of soil or sand into the embers and keep stirring until nothing feels warm to a hovering hand [7]. Do not just bury it [4][7].

**"If it's too hot to touch, it's too hot to leave"** [2].

## Fire and your body: burns first aid

*This is general first aid, not medical advice. Call emergency services when you can.*

**Step 1. Stop the burning.** Stop, drop, and roll. Smother flames [10].

**Step 2. Call 911 if any of these is true** [10][12][13]:

- Deep, white, charred, or numb skin
- A burn as big as the person's palm or bigger
- Burns on the face, mouth, nose, hands, feet, joints, or groin
- A burn that goes all the way around a limb, the chest, or the neck
- A chemical or electrical burn
- Smoke breathed in (burns on the head, face, neck, eyebrows or nose hairs; coughing; dark, black-stained mucus) [13]
- Signs of shock
- A very young or very old person

**Step 3. Pick the cooling track.** A burn that is deep, white or charred, as big as the person's palm or bigger, or comes with signs of shock goes on the **large or deep track**. Every other burn, including a small burn on a hand or face while you wait for help, goes on the **cool-water track** [10][13][14].

**Large or deep track**

- Do not soak or immerse a large or deep burn in cold water, because that can cause shock [10][13].
- Sources differ: NHS and Red Cross still advise cooling [12][14]; MedlinePlus and the Army advise against cold water for severe burns [10][13].
- This guide's cautious middle (the guide's judgment, not a source's rule): cool the burn itself briefly with cool, not cold, water or wet cloths, keep the rest of the body warm, and stop if the person starts shaking.
- Cover with a dry clean cloth. Raise the burn above the heart if you can.
- To prevent shock, lay the person flat with feet raised about 12 inches, but only if there is no head, neck, back or leg injury [13].
- If they are struggling to breathe, let them sit up (general first-aid practice).
- Keep them warm, because large burns bring hypothermia, especially in children [12].

**Cool-water track**

- Cool the burn with cool running water, not ice-cold. Aim for 20 minutes if you can, ideally within 3 hours of the burn [14].
- Lukewarm water is acceptable if cool water is not available. With no tap, use a cool wet cloth [12][14].
- Sources differ on how long: NHS and the Army say 20 minutes, Red Cross says 5 to 20, MedlinePlus says at least 5 to 30 [10][12][13][14].
- Cover loosely with a clean, dry, non-stick dressing, or cling film [12][13][14].

**Either track**

- Remove rings, watches, and clothes near the burn, but leave anything stuck [12][14].
- No ice. No butter, oil, grease, or creams. Do not pop blisters or use sticky bandages [12][13][14].

## Fire indoors, in tents, and in vehicles: the carbon monoxide rule

This is the one place the full rule is written out. Other sections point here.

- **Never** burn charcoal, a camp stove, a lantern, a grill, a heater, a generator, or an open fire inside a tent, vehicle, garage, or house [1][18][20][21].
- **Opening a flap, door, or window does not make it safe** [18].
- **Generators:** never run one inside a home or garage, even with doors and windows open [1]. Run it outdoors, at least 20 ft from windows, doors, and vents [1][22][23].
- **Never run a car in an attached garage,** even with the door open, and **never heat a home with a gas oven or stove** [1][23].
- **The one exception** is a fireplace or stove built to vent and kept in working order [1][22]. Do not burn anything in an unvented stove or fireplace [1]. A fireplace needs a yearly inspection by a professional [22].
- **Candles, fuel gel, and oil lamps make CO too** [10]. Use flashlights [22].
- **Symptoms:** headache, dizziness, weakness, upset stomach, vomiting, chest pain, confusion [1]. It is easy to mistake for a cold or flu [21]. Sleepers and drinkers may have none [1][21].
- **If you suspect CO:** get everyone into fresh air first, then call 911 [19].
- **Detectors:** a battery or battery-backup CO alarm near every sleeping area. Test it [1][19][22][23].

## Worked scenario A: cold rain, wet wood, dusk

1. **Get under cover and out of wet clothes first** [15].
2. **Find wood.** Standing or leaning dead wood and branches that snap. Split solid wood to reach the dry inside [10].
3. **Make tinder.** Shave a feather stick from a dry stick. Dry your tinder inside your clothing against your body [10].
4. **Prepare the site.** Clear to bare dirt, keep water and a shovel next to it, and make a base of flat wood or non-porous rock so nothing sits in water [4][10].
5. **Lay a teepee** (it burns well even with wet wood) and put a reflector behind it [10].
6. **Light with a ferro rod from the upwind side.** Wet does not stop it [10].
7. **Add kindling in handfuls,** then fuel as the flame steadies. Wet fuel will smoke white until it dries [10].
8. **Keep the fire outside, away from your shelter** [2]. Do not hang a tarp over the flames. Hold any rain cover well clear.
9. **Dry clothes at a distance,** and **watch for hypothermia:** shivering, fumbling hands, confusion, slurred speech [15].

## Worked scenario B: winter, power out at home

1. **Carbon monoxide first.** Follow the [carbon monoxide rule](#fire-indoors-in-tents-and-in-vehicles-the-carbon-monoxide-rule) above, including generators, gas ovens (a gas range is for cooking only, never for heat, with the hood on or a window open while you cook [1][23][29]; see [`09_urban_survival.md`](09_urban_survival.md)), and CO alarms.
2. **Shrink your space.** Pick one small room, shut the door, wear layers, add blankets [15]. If the cold is extreme, go to a community location with power, and ask local officials where warming centers are [23].
3. **Space heater:** only if you have working power for it. Keep it at least 3 ft from anything flammable, plug it into a wall outlet, and never run it while you sleep [22].
4. **Watch for cold and for CO.** Both can look like being tired or sick [15][1].
5. **Food:** a fridge keeps about 4 hours, a full freezer about 48 [23].

More on water and food without power: [`09_urban_survival.md`](09_urban_survival.md).

## Be found, and stay legal

**Signal fires** [11]:

- **Night:** three fires in a triangle or in a line about 80 ft (25 m) apart. The Army manual calls this the international distress signal. If you are alone, one fire.
- **Day:** three columns of smoke. For white smoke, partly smother a large fire with green leaves, moss, or a little water. Choose a smoke that contrasts with the background. Never burn rubber or oil. The smoke is black and toxic [10][11].
- Build signals early and protect them until needed. A tree torch is a last resort: only an isolated tree, never in dry conditions or under a burn ban, because it can start a wildfire [11].

### Burn bans and what a fire can cost you

Check before every outdoor fire [2][5][6]. Rules change by place and week. This is not legal advice.

- **Virginia** bans open-air burning before 4 p.m. from Feb 15 to Apr 30 within 300 ft of woods or dry grass. Campfires count, though a fully contained ring under a fine metal screen, attended, with 20 ft cleared, may be acceptable. The fine is up to $500. The person who started the fire pays suppression costs if it escapes, even if they took all proper precautions [25].
- **A Forest Service order, for example** (Tahoe National Forest, Order 17-26-25, effective October 9, 2026 through November 30, 2026; check current orders for where you are) allows campfires only in listed rings at recreation sites. Penalties run up to $5,000 for a person, up to six months in prison, or both. The only exemptions are officers and rescue or firefighting crews on duty, and holders of one specific Forest Service permit. Campfire-permit holders and fuelwood cutters are not exempt. The order lists no survival or emergency exemption [26].
- **If your negligence starts a wildfire,** you can be liable for suppression costs and damages [4].

Lighting a fire to survive is your call. Do not assume the law will protect you.

## Common mistakes

- **Striking before you stage the fuel, or smothering the first flame** with kindling [10].
- **Lighting from downwind** [10].
- **Burying or "banking" the fire.** It smolders and escapes [4][7].
- **Fire in a tent or car for warmth.** People have died doing this [18][20].
- **Ice or butter on a burn** [12][13]. **Throwing fuel on flames** [10]. **Skipping the burn-ban check** [25][26].

## Quick-reference card (print on one page)

**FIRE: OUTSIDE ONLY.** Never in a tent, car, garage, or house. The only exception is a vented fireplace or stove in working order.

**Before you light**

- Allowed here? Check burn bans.
- Flat, open overhead, out of the wind. 10 ft cleared; 15 ft from tents and low branches.
- Dig to bare dirt. Duff (rotting needles and leaves) burns underground.
- No wet or porous rock.
- Water and a shovel next to the fire.
- Tinder, kindling (pencil lead, pencil, thumb), and fuel (forearm) staged.

**Build and light.** Base, tinder, kindling by handfuls (leave gaps), fuel. Light from the upwind side. Lighter; ferro rod if practiced; matches. Lens only in bright sun. Friction last.

**Keep it.** Small. Never alone. Do not bury or bank it.

**Overnight.** Out before you sleep. Only if the choice is a night fire or freezing: someone stays awake, cleared pit, 15 ft from any shelter, water and shovel beside it.

**Put out.** Drown. Stir. Drown. Feel (back of hand). Check under rocks and within 50 ft.

**Burn.** Stop the burning (stop, drop, roll). Then sort it.

- **Call 911** for: deep, white, charred, or numb skin; palm-size or bigger; face, mouth, nose, hands, feet, joints, groin; all the way around; chemical or electrical; smoke breathed in; shock; very young or old.
- **Large or deep** (deep/white/charred, palm-size or bigger, or shock): no cold soak. This guide's middle: cool the burn itself briefly with cool, not cold, water; keep the rest of the body warm. Dry clean cloth.
- **Every other burn** (including a small burn on a hand while you wait for help): cool running water, ideally 20 minutes (sources say 5 to 30). Cover loosely.
- **All burns:** no ice, no butter.

**CO.** Headache, dizzy, nausea, confused: fresh air now, then 911.

**Home, power out.** Nothing burns inside except a vented fireplace or stove in working order, no gas oven (a gas range is for cooking only, never for heat; hood on or window open while cooking), generator outdoors at least 20 ft from windows, doors, and vents, battery CO alarm. See the CO section and [`09_urban_survival.md`](09_urban_survival.md).

**Cold.** Out of wind and rain; wet clothes off only once somewhere warm. Sleepy or confused: call 911. Warm the middle of the body. Warm drink, no alcohol, only if alert, shivering and able to swallow; otherwise nothing by mouth. Under 95 F body temperature means medical help.

**Signal.** Night: three fires (alone: one). Day: three smoke columns, white smoke.
