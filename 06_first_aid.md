# First Aid: What to Do Until Help Arrives, or When It Cannot

This is not medical advice. It is commonly taught field response, for the hours when professional care is late or out of reach. If you can call 911, call first, then use this page while you wait. Reading is not training; take a hands-on course (Red Cross, American Heart Association, Stop the Bleed) [34][33].

The fastest dangers are heavy bleeding, no breathing, and a severe allergic reaction (this guide's judgment, following the Army's massive-bleeding-first order [41]), then shock. The slower ones are cold, heat, lost fluid, and infection. This guide takes them in that order.

**Start here**

- **Heavy bleeding:** [severe bleeding](#severe-bleeding), then [shock](#shock).
- **Not breathing, collapsed, or choking:** [not breathing and choking](#not-breathing-and-choking).
- **Hives, swollen face or tongue, trouble breathing after a sting, food or medicine:** [allergic reaction](#severe-allergic-reaction-anaphylaxis).
- **Face droop, slurred speech, chest pain, or unresponsive but breathing:** [stroke, heart attack and the recovery position](#stroke-heart-attack-and-an-unresponsive-person-who-is-breathing).
- **Cold, shivering, confused:** [cold](#cold-hypothermia-and-frostbite). **Hot, dizzy, confused:** [heat](#heat-illness).
- **Cut, fall, burn:** [wounds](#wounds-infection-and-tetanus), [broken bones and sprains](#broken-bones-and-sprains), [burns](#burns).
- **Vomiting or diarrhea:** [dehydration and diarrhea](#dehydration-and-diarrhea).
- **Snakebite or tick bite:** [Mid-Atlantic and Appalachian hazards](regional/mid-atlantic-appalachian/hazards/_index.md).
- **Short version:** the [quick-reference card](#quick-reference-card).

## Call 911 first, and when to get the person to care

Call 911 (or your local emergency number) before you start, or have someone call while you work. Alone, use speaker and keep working. The dispatcher can talk you through next steps (general practice).

Call 911 or move toward care now for any of these:

- Bleeding that is spurting, soaking through cloth, or will not stop with firm pressure [3][5]
- A person who is unresponsive, not breathing normally, or only gasping [34]
- Confusion, fainting, or drowsiness with any injury or illness [7][12][23]
- Trouble breathing, or swelling of the face, lips or tongue [29][30]
- Body temperature below 95 F, or any sign of hypothermia [12][13]
- Confusion or fainting in the heat [17][18]
- Sudden face droop, arm weakness, slurred speech, or chest discomfort lasting more than a few minutes [47][48]
- A suspected broken bone in the head, neck, back, pelvis or upper leg, or a bone through the skin [27]
- Wounds to the neck, chest, belly, pelvis or groin [5]
- Fever or red streaks with a wound, or any sign of sepsis [9][11]
- A deep, large, face, hand or groin burn, or any chemical or electrical burn (full list in [03_fire.md](03_fire.md#fire-and-your-body-burns-first-aid))

If phones are down, send a person to reach help and start care without waiting.

## What to do first, in order

This follows the Army's "massive bleeding first" sequence [41], simplified (this guide's judgment).

1. Check for danger to you (traffic, fire, live power).
2. Stop life-threatening bleeding. It comes before the airway [41].
3. Unresponsive and not breathing normally: chest compressions [32][34].
4. Choking: back blows and thrusts [35].
5. Anaphylaxis: epinephrine auto-injector if the person has one, and call 911 [29][31].
6. Shock: keep the person lying down and warm [6][7].
7. Everything else.

## Severe bleeding

**Recognize it.** Blood spurting or pulsing, pooling on the ground, a bandage soaked through, or bleeding that does not slow with firm pressure. Bleeding from the neck, groin, chest, belly or pelvis is an emergency even if it looks small [3][5].

**Do this, in order.** Life-threatening arm or leg bleeding: go to step 5.

1. **Call 911** and get a first aid kit if one is near [3].
2. **Expose the wound.** Cut or tear clothing away [2].
3. **Press hard, straight on the wound,** with gauze, cloth, clothing or your bare hand, even if it hurts the person [5][6].
4. **If blood soaks through, add more cloth on top and keep pressing.** Do not lift the first layer to look [5].
5. **Life-threatening arm or leg bleeding: put a tourniquet on now.** Do not keep trying pressure alone. Keep pressing on the wound while you get it in place [2][3][6].
6. **Where a tourniquet will not go** (groin, neck, shoulder, scalp, back): press hard with both hands and keep pressing. Pack the wound only if you have learned how. Packing means stuffing gauze or cloth tightly into the wound to press on the bleeding [1][3].
7. **Keep pressure on until help takes over.** Switch with another person when you tire [6].

**Tourniquet: how**

1. Place it **2 to 3 inches above the wound, between the wound and the heart,** not on a joint. If the wound is at or near the elbow or knee, go above the joint. Over clothing is fine [2][4][5].
2. Pull the strap **as tight as you can,** then twist the rod (windlass) until the bleeding stops or you cannot twist further. Secure the rod so it cannot unwind [2][4].
3. **Write down the time** and tell responders. Too long on can damage tissue [2][5].
4. **Do not loosen or remove it.** Only a medical professional does that. If bleeding continues after you have tightened it as far as it goes, put a second tourniquet above the first, closer to the heart [4].
5. No commercial tourniquet? MedlinePlus describes a band 2 to 4 inches wide, wrapped several times and knotted, with a stick between two knots, twisted until bleeding stops, then secured [5]. The Army calls belts and zip ties a last resort [41].

**Also.** Leave a stuck object in place; pad and tape it [3][5]. An amputated part: damp cloth, bag, set the bag in ice water, not on ice [42]. Raising the limb slows bleeding but does not replace pressure [41].

**What goes wrong.** Pressing lightly or peeking. A tourniquet on a joint or too loose [2]. Forgetting the time. Waiting on a limb that is pouring blood. Missing the shock that follows.

## Not breathing, and choking

**Take a hands-on CPR course.** This is a reminder, not training [33][34].

**Adult or teen who suddenly collapses and is not responding or breathing normally.** The American Heart Association describes hands-only CPR as two steps: call 911, then push hard and fast in the center of the chest [32]. MedlinePlus: heel of one hand between the nipples, press about 2 inches, 100 to 120 per minute [33]. Keep going until the person shows a clear sign of life, an AED is ready, you cannot continue, or EMS (emergency medical services) arrives. Switch with another person every one to two minutes if you can [34]. An AED (automated external defibrillator) talks you through its use; you can use one untrained [33][34].

**Child, baby or drowning victim.** Call 911 and follow the dispatcher's CPR instructions (general practice). Take a hands-on course to learn child and infant CPR.

**Choking, awake adult or child:** cannot speak, cry or cough, or turns pale or blue. Call 911. Give 5 firm back blows between the shoulder blades, then 5 upward and inward abdominal thrusts below the ribs, and alternate. Use chest thrusts if the person is pregnant or too large to reach around. If they go limp, lower them to the floor and start CPR [35].

**Choking, baby under 1:** 5 back blows with the baby face down along your forearm, head lower than the chest, then 5 chest thrusts with two fingers at the center of the chest. No abdominal thrusts [36]. Call 911. If the baby becomes unresponsive, start infant CPR as the dispatcher directs [36].

## Severe allergic reaction (anaphylaxis)

**Recognize it.** After a sting, food, medicine or an unknown cause: hives, swelling of the face, lips or tongue, trouble breathing, wheezing, hoarse voice, fast heartbeat, dizziness, fainting, stomach pain. It can start within minutes and can kill [29][31].

1. **Call 911** right away [29][30].
2. **If the person has a prescribed epinephrine auto-injector, use it right away, as prescribed** [29][31]. Inject into the middle of the outer thigh, through clothing if needed. Hold it for the time on the device's label (for example, EpiPen: 3 seconds). A second device may be used if symptoms continue or return [31]. Give the used device to the responders [30].
3. Lay the person flat and raise the feet about 12 inches, unless a head, neck, back or leg injury is suspected, it causes discomfort, or they are vomiting. Let them sit if that helps breathing, and no pillow if breathing is hard [29][30].
4. **Nothing by mouth** if breathing is difficult [29]. If breathing stops, start CPR [29].
5. Scrape a bee stinger off with a card or fingernail. Do not squeeze it [29].

This guide gives no epinephrine dosing beyond the label and prescription. Learn the device before an emergency [31].

## Shock

Shock means the body is not getting enough blood flow. Signs: pale, gray, cool or clammy skin; fast, weak pulse; fast breathing; confusion, restlessness or drowsiness; nausea [7][8]. It follows heavy bleeding, severe burns, severe infection and allergic reaction. Call 911.

1. Control any bleeding you can [8].
2. Have the person lie on their back. If there is no head, neck, back or leg injury, raise the legs about 12 inches. If raising the legs hurts, keep them flat [7].
3. Keep the person warm, with something between them and the cold ground. Loosen tight clothing [6][7].
4. If they vomit, turn the person on their side [7][8]. If a spine injury is possible, roll them keeping the head, neck and back in line [7].
5. Check breathing at least every 5 minutes until help arrives [7].
6. **Give nothing to eat or drink** [7][8].

If a conscious person is struggling to breathe, let them find the position that helps [6].

## Stroke, heart attack, and an unresponsive person who is breathing

**Stroke.** Sudden trouble with balance or seeing, a drooping face, an arm that drifts down, slurred or strange speech, or a severe headache with no known cause. CDC's BE FAST check covers Balance, Eyes, Face, Arms, Speech, Time. Call 911 right away and note when it started [47].

**Heart attack.** Chest discomfort in the center or left side lasting more than a few minutes; pain in the jaw, neck, back, arm or shoulder; shortness of breath; feeling weak, light-headed or faint. Nausea and unusual tiredness are more common in women. Call 911 right away and be ready to start CPR [48].

**Unresponsive but breathing.** Call 911. If you do not suspect a spine injury, roll the person toward you onto their side, with the top leg bent at hip and knee and the head tilted back to keep the airway open. If breathing or pulse stops, roll them onto their back and start CPR. With a possible spine injury, leave them where they are; if they vomit, roll the whole body at once [46].

## Cold: hypothermia and frostbite

**Hypothermia** is body temperature below 95 F. Signs: shivering, exhaustion, confusion, fumbling hands, memory loss, slurred speech, drowsiness [12][13]. Shivering that stops, a glassy stare and rigid muscles mean it is getting worse [14]. **Call 911 whenever you suspect it** [12].

1. Move the person out of the wind and rain and off the cold ground, gently and with little exertion [12]. Once you are somewhere warm and sheltered, replace wet or tight clothing with dry, loose clothing [12][13]. In the open, put dry layers and a waterproof layer over the wet clothes instead (see [`04_shelter.md`](04_shelter.md#stop-heat-loss-ground-wet-and-wind)). Cover head and neck [12][13].
2. Body heat and dry blankets first: warm the center, chest, neck, head and groin, skin to skin under dry blankets [13]. If you have heat packs, wrap them and put them on the chest, armpits and back, never on bare skin (Wilderness Medical Society guidance, cited in [`04_shelter.md`](04_shelter.md#stop-heat-loss-ground-wet-and-wind)). See also [03_fire.md](03_fire.md#warming-a-cold-person).
3. Give a warm, sweet drink only if the person is alert, shivering and can swallow normally. No alcohol or caffeine [12][14]. If they are confused, drowsy or not shivering, give nothing by mouth (this guide's rule, matching [03_fire.md](03_fire.md#warming-a-cold-person)). Stay with them.

**Do not** warm quickly or directly: rapid rewarming can cause dangerous heart rhythms [14]. No hot water, heat lamp, stove or fire, and no alcohol [12][13][14]. Do not assume someone found motionless in the cold is dead. If they are unconscious and not breathing, start CPR and keep warming them [12][13].

**Frostbite.** Pins and needles, then numbness. Skin hard, pale and cold, or a white or grayish-yellow patch that feels firm or waxy. Redness or pain may be the first sign, and the person may not feel it [13][15].

1. Treat hypothermia first [15]. Get out of the cold. Remove wet clothing and tight jewelry [15][16].
2. **Rewarm only if it can stay thawed.** Refrozen tissue is damaged worse. If you cannot prevent refreezing, wait until you reach a warm place [15][16].
3. Soak the part in warm, not hot, water, about 99 to 104 F, for 15 to 30 minutes [15][16]. Aim for the cooler end and test the water with your own hand: numb skin cannot feel a burn (this guide's choice).
4. Wrap loosely in dry sterile dressing, with gauze between fingers or toes [16].
5. Blisters, black skin or signs of hypothermia mean call 911 [16].

**Do not** rub frostbitten skin, hold it near a fire, stove or heating pad, break blisters, or walk on frostbitten feet unless you must [13][15][16].

**What goes wrong.** Rewarming a foot, then walking out on it. Alcohol "to warm up." Calling a drowsy, confused person just tired.

## Heat illness

**Heat exhaustion:** heavy sweating, headache, dizziness, nausea, weakness, cool moist pale skin [19].

1. Move to a cooler place with moving air. Loosen or remove clothing [19].
2. Cool wet cloths or cool water, and fan [19].
3. If responsive and able to swallow, small sips of a sports drink or water, not fast [19]. Rest for several hours after symptoms stop [19].
4. **Call 911** if they vomit, cannot take fluids, faint, do not improve, or their responsiveness changes [19].

**Heat stroke is an emergency:** confusion, slurred speech, fainting, seizure, fast pulse, body temperature above 103 F [20][45]. Do not wait for a thermometer (this guide's judgment).

1. **Call 911.**
2. **Cool at once.** Best is immersion to the neck in cold water. If you cannot, use ice packs, cold wet towels, a cold shower, or misting with fanning [18][21].
3. Keep cooling for about 20 minutes, or until the person improves or EMS arrives [18]. Keep their face above water (general practice).
4. **Give nothing by mouth** [45].

**Do not** give salt tablets [17], fever medicines such as aspirin or acetaminophen [17], alcohol or caffeine [17]. A house without power: [09_urban_survival.md](09_urban_survival.md#warm-or-cool).

## Wounds, infection and tetanus

**Clean a minor wound**

1. Wash your hands. Stop bleeding with direct pressure [9].
2. Wash the cut well with mild soap and water. For a puncture, run water over it for about 5 minutes, then wash with soap [9].
3. Leave a stuck object in place. Do not poke at debris [9].
4. Apply antibacterial ointment and a clean non-stick bandage [9].

Do not try to clean a major wound. Control the bleeding, cover the wound, and get medical help [9]. Do not squeeze or cut a pus pocket yourself (this guide's rule). Antibiotics are a clinician's decision, and this guide gives no names or doses. If a clinician prescribed one for this wound, take it exactly as prescribed.

**Get medical care if:** the wound is large, deeper than a quarter inch, on the face, or reaches bone; it is a bite; it came from a rusty object, nail or fishhook; something is stuck in it; or you cannot feel or use the area below it [9].

**Infection signs:** warmth, redness, throbbing pain, swelling, fever, red streaks spreading from the wound, or pus [9]. Contact a clinician. Add clammy skin, confusion, fast heart rate or weak pulse, shortness of breath, or shivering and feeling unusually cold, and it may be sepsis, a life-threatening reaction to infection [11]. Treat it as an emergency.

**Tetanus.** CDC's rule once the vaccine series is complete: a clean, minor wound needs a booster if the last dose was 10 or more years ago. A dirty or major wound (puncture, soil, saliva or bites, burns, crush injury, frostbite) needs one at 5 or more years. Unknown or incomplete history means vaccinate. Antibiotics do not prevent tetanus [10]. A clinician or pharmacist gives the booster; plan for it when you reach care.

**What goes wrong.** Skipping the wash. Ignoring red streaks.

## Burns

**Scald or contact burn:** cool it under cool running water first, then follow the track that fits the burn in [03_fire.md](03_fire.md#fire-and-your-body-burns-first-aid), which also covers when to call 911 and what not to apply. Two cases need a first step.

**Electrical.** Do not touch the person with bare hands while their body is still in contact with the power. Turn off the power if you can do it safely: unplug the cord, shut off the breaker, or pull the fuse. Switching off an appliance may not cut the current. If you cannot, push the person away with a dry non-conducting object such as a broom or rubber mat, never a wet or metal one. Stay at least 20 feet from a person held by high-voltage lines until the power is off. Then call 911, check breathing, and start CPR if needed [39].

**Chemical.** Get the person away from the chemical. Remove contaminated clothing and jewelry without contaminating yourself. If the chemical is dry, brush off the excess first, away from the eyes. Then flush the skin with cool running water for 15 minutes or more. Dry lime and reactive metals such as sodium or lithium are exceptions to flushing first. Call 911 for any chemical burn (matches [03_fire.md](03_fire.md#fire-and-your-body-burns-first-aid)). Call Poison Control (1-800-222-1222) for swallowed or inhaled chemicals. No ointment, and do not try to neutralize the chemical without advice from poison control or a clinician [40]. A chemical in the eye: flush with running water for at least 15 minutes and get medical help right away [40].

## Broken bones and sprains

**Treat every muscle or bone injury as a possible fracture until a clinician says otherwise** [27]. **Call 911** if the person cannot or will not move the area, it looks bent, it is cold, numb or tingling, there is bleeding or exposed bone, the injury is in the head, neck, spine, pelvis or upper leg, or there are signs of shock [26][27]. Do not move a person with a possible spine injury [26].

**Splint** if you must move them or help is far:

1. Control bleeding with direct pressure. Cover an open wound with a clean dressing. Do not try to line up a broken bone, and do not straighten it unless circulation is blocked and no trained help is near [26].
2. Splint the joint above and the joint below the break [26][41]. Pad the splint and tie it snugly, not tight (general practice).
3. Check the part beyond the splint. Pale, blue, cold, numb or tingling means a problem [26][41].
4. Cold pack in a thin dry cloth, 20 minutes or less, then wait 20 minutes [27].

**Sprain:** wrap snugly but not tight, raise above the heart, rest several days, cloth-wrapped ice. Pain relievers at the label dose, no aspirin for children [28]. A pop with immediate trouble using the joint, or a joint out of position: get emergency care, as for a possible break [28]. See a clinician if swelling is not improving in 2 days [28].

**What goes wrong.** Testing the bone. Splinting only the break. A splint tied tight over swelling.

## Dehydration and diarrhea

Fluid loss is the danger, not the diarrhea. More in [02_water.md](02_water.md#dehydration-what-it-looks-like-what-to-do).

**Early:** thirst, dry mouth, dark or less urine, tiredness. **Worse:** dizziness, fast heartbeat, very little urine. **Emergency:** confusion, fainting, no urine, rapid breathing, shock. **Babies:** no wet diaper for 3 hours or more, no tears, sunken eyes, unusual sleepiness [23].

**Rehydration drink (ORS):** 1 liter of treated or boiled-and-cooled water, 6 level teaspoons of sugar, 1/2 teaspoon of salt. Do not guess the amounts [22]. A packaged ORS mix removes the measuring (general practice).

1. Give small sips, again and again. Water alone is not enough once someone is dehydrated [22].
2. After vomiting, wait a few minutes, then give smaller sips (general practice).
3. Keep feeding when the person can eat. Bland foods are fine [44][24].
4. **Loperamide (Imodium):** label dose only. Not for children under 2, and not with fever or blood or mucus in the stool. Too much can cause deadly heart rhythm problems. Stop and call a doctor if diarrhea lasts past 48 hours [25]. The Red Cross says no over-the-counter antidiarrheals for young children or infants [24].

**Get help now** for vomiting that keeps fluids from staying down, confusion or drowsiness, muscle cramps, weakness or dizziness, black or bloody stool, sharp or lasting belly pain, or shock [22][24]. Severe dehydration needs intravenous fluids [23]. Start ORS while you arrange care; WHO says it is among the most important actions to prevent death from cholera [22]. Adults with symptoms past 2 days should contact a clinician. Children and infants sooner: no urine in over 6 hours, no wet diaper in 3 hours, or unable to keep fluids down [24].

**What goes wrong.** Plain water only. Changing the recipe. Big gulps.

## Head injury and blisters

**Head injury.** Call 911 for loss of consciousness, confusion, nausea or vomiting, worsening headache, seizure, vision change or unusual behavior. Keep the person still, in the position found. No painkillers, because they can hide signs of a serious injury. Do not assume they must stay awake, but get a clinician to evaluate them before they sleep or return to activity [37].

**Blisters.** Leave them intact, because the skin keeps out infection. Cover with a bandage and remove the rubbing. Friction blisters only (MedlinePlus): drain if large, painful, or about to pop. Watch for pus, spreading redness, warmth or fever [38]. Never pop frostbite blisters [16] or burn blisters (see [`03_fire.md`](03_fire.md#fire-and-your-body-burns-first-aid)).

## Medicines you depend on

Do not change, skip, split, stretch or ration a prescription dose without a clinician. Dose questions go to a prescriber, a pharmacist or 911. The FDA says insulin vials and cartridges can stay unrefrigerated at 59 to 86 F for up to 28 days, and frozen insulin must not be used [43]. Storage, emergency refills and warning signs: [chronic medication continuity](substrate/06_first_aid/entries/chronic-med-continuity.md) and [09_urban_survival.md](09_urban_survival.md#medicines-and-power-dependent-devices).

## A basic kit

General practice, not a sourced list. A commercial tourniquet, gauze, pressure bandage, tape, gloves, scissors; ointment and non-stick pads; an elastic wrap and a splint; oral rehydration salts; your prescriptions, including epinephrine if prescribed; a space blanket and a pen.

## Worked scenario A: deep leg cut, far from help

On a hike, a fall onto a sharp branch opens a companion's thigh. Blood pulses through a shirt. No signal.

1. **Send others for help** or toward signal. Alone, shout and try the phone from high ground.
2. **Press** a folded shirt hard onto the wound with both hands [2][5].
3. **The bleeding does not stop.** Tourniquet 2 to 3 inches above the wound on the thigh, between the wound and the body. Twist the rod until the blood stops, secure it, write the time on it [2][4].
4. **Treat shock.** Lay the person flat. Do not raise the legs, because this is a leg injury. Cover them and put a layer under them. Nothing to eat or drink [7].
5. **Do not loosen it** [4]. If blood still seeps, twist further and press a pad on the wound. If it keeps going, put a second tourniquet above the first [4].
6. **Move toward care.** Do not release it. Get carried out and tell responders the time [9][10].

## Worked scenario B: diarrhea on day 3 of a boil-water notice

A boil-water notice has run three days. A family member started having watery diarrhea last night. Use [02_water.md](02_water.md#worked-scenario-a-day-2-of-a-boil-water-notice-at-home) for the water rules.

1. **Check urgency first.** Confusion, drowsiness, vomiting everything, no urine, blood or black stool, severe belly pain, or high fever: call 911 or a clinician now and start ORS while you wait [22][24].
2. **Otherwise** mix ORS with boiled-and-cooled or bottled water: 1 liter, 6 level teaspoons of sugar, 1/2 teaspoon of salt, measured [22].
3. **Sips, over and over.**
4. **Feed them:** rice, bread, crackers [44][24].
5. **Check every few hours:** more urine, lighter color, moist mouth, alert [23].
6. **Loperamide** only for an adult with no fever and no blood, at the label dose [25].
7. **Escalate:** an adult not better after 2 days should call a clinician [24]. A small child or baby, sooner, and no loperamide [24][25].

## Where sources differ

- **Tourniquet.** The Red Cross lists a tourniquet "if trained." Stop the Bleed says apply one when pressure fails and the dressing soaks through. This guide's reading: do not keep trying pressure alone [2][3]. Stop the Bleed teaches "pack the wound and press"; the Red Cross says pack if trained [1][3].
- **Releasing it.** The Army manual says an isolated casualty may release a tourniquet after two hours and retighten if bleeding continues [41]. The Red Cross says do not loosen it; Stop the Bleed gives no release step [2][4]. This guide's rule: do not release it. If no help can be reached, get the person moving toward care.
- **Heating pads for hypothermia.** MedlinePlus says avoid them [12]. The Red Cross allows a pad or bottle wrapped in thin dry cloth if you are far from care [14].
- **Frostbite rewarming.** The step uses the Red Cross range, 99 to 104 F [16]. MedlinePlus gives 104 to 108 F [15], and the Red Cross FAQ gives 100 to 105 F. This guide takes the cooler range because numb skin burns easily.
- **Heat stroke temperature.** The step uses above 103 F [20][45]. MedlinePlus uses above 104 F [17].

## Common mistakes

- A tourniquet put off on a limb that is pouring blood, or loosened later: [bleeding](#severe-bleeding).
- Food or drink for a person in shock: [shock](#shock).
- Waiting to see if an allergic reaction passes: [anaphylaxis](#severe-allergic-reaction-anaphylaxis).
- Fast direct heat on a hypothermic person, or frostbite rewarmed where it may refreeze: [cold](#cold-hypothermia-and-frostbite).
- Calling a drowsy, confused person "just tired": [cold](#cold-hypothermia-and-frostbite).
- Plain water alone for diarrhea: [dehydration](#dehydration-and-diarrhea).

## Quick-reference card

- **Always:** call 911 first; do not become a second patient.
- **Collapsed adult or teen:** call 911; push hard and fast in the center of the chest, about 2 inches, 100 to 120 per minute.
- **Child, baby or drowning:** call 911 and follow the dispatcher's CPR instructions. Take a hands-on course to learn child and infant CPR.
- **Choking:** adult, 5 back blows then 5 abdominal thrusts; baby, 5 back blows then 5 chest thrusts.
- **Heavy bleeding:** press hard; if blood soaks through, add cloth and keep pressing.
- **Life-threatening arm or leg bleeding:** tourniquet now, 2 to 3 inches above the wound, not on a joint.
- **Tourniquet:** as tight as you can; twist until bleeding stops; write the time; do not loosen or remove. Bleeding continues: second one above the first.
- **Tourniquet will not go:** press hard with both hands; pack the wound only if you have learned how.
- **Allergic reaction:** call 911; epinephrine auto-injector as prescribed, outer thigh, hold for the time on the device's label (EpiPen: 3 seconds).
- **Shock:** lie flat, legs up about 12 inches unless head, neck, back or leg injury; keep warm; nothing by mouth.
- **Stroke or heart attack:** call 911 for face droop, arm weakness, slurred speech, or chest discomfort. Unresponsive but breathing: roll onto the side.
- **Hypothermia** (below 95 F, or sleepy or confused): call 911; out of wind and rain; dry clothes once somewhere warm.
- **Warming:** body heat and dry blankets first; wrapped heat packs on the chest, armpits and back, never on bare skin; no alcohol. Warm sweet drink only if alert, shivering and able to swallow.
- **Frostbite:** warm (not hot) water, about 99 to 104 F, 15 to 30 minutes, tested with your own hand, only if it cannot refreeze; do not rub.
- **Heat stroke** (above 103 F): call 911; cold water to the neck, or ice and wet towels, for about 20 minutes or until the person improves or EMS arrives; nothing by mouth.
- **Cut:** soap and water; puncture, running water about 5 minutes. Tetanus booster if the last dose was 10 or more years ago, or 5 or more years for a dirty or major wound.
- **Scald or contact burn:** cool under cool running water; full steps in [03_fire.md](03_fire.md#fire-and-your-body-burns-first-aid).
- **Electrical burn:** turn off power, do not touch the person, then 911. **Chemical burn:** call 911; brush off dry chemical, flush 15 minutes or more.
- **Fracture:** splint the joint above and below; do not straighten it unless circulation is blocked and no trained help is near; cold pack 20 minutes or less.
- **Rehydration drink:** 1 liter treated water, 6 level teaspoons sugar, 1/2 teaspoon salt, small sips.
