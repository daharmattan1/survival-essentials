# Key Resources: What to Have on Hand and Where to Learn More

Use this chapter before an emergency, to decide what to keep on paper, what to download, which classes to take and which sources to trust. It does not replace training.

First aid information in this repository is general information, not medical advice. In an emergency, call 911.

## Poisoning: call first

**Poisoning: 1-800-222-1222 (America's Poison Centers),** free and open 24 hours a day [39]. Call 911 if the person collapses, has a seizure, has trouble breathing or cannot be woken [39][40]. The poison.org site belongs to one center, which says it closed its call center on March 31, 2025 [40]. Use the 1-800 number or poisonhelp.org [39].

## What hurts first

1. **Depending on one device and one connection.** Federal guidance puts a battery-powered or hand-crank radio, extra batteries, a phone charger and a backup battery in the basic kit [1].
2. **Making a life-safety decision from a bad source.** A random video, an AI answer with no links, or an app that needs a connection can be wrong, old, or gone when you need it (this guide's judgment). Check the source before you act ([how](#how-to-judge-a-source-in-a-hurry)).
3. **Never having practiced.** Reading about a tourniquet is not the same as applying one. Hands-on classes fix that ([training](#training-to-take-before-you-need-it)).

## The sequence: what to do and in what order

1. **Turn on alerts.** Wireless Emergency Alerts are free, but some phones require you to enable them in settings. The FEMA app sends National Weather Service alerts for up to five locations [5]. Ask local officials whether your area has its own alert sign-up; Ready.gov points to them for alert questions [5] (this guide's judgment).
2. **Write a household plan on paper.** Use the fillable Family Emergency Plan form and the Family Communication Plan card from Ready.gov [2][3].
3. **Print the short documents,** not whole books ([how](#build-an-offline-library)).
4. **Put digital copies on every adult's phone** and test them in airplane mode.
5. **Get a radio and spare power** [1].
6. **Take the classes.** First aid and CPR first, then Stop the Bleed ([training](#training-to-take-before-you-need-it)).
7. **Check on a schedule.** Check radio, flashlights and food and water dates every three months (this guide's judgment); replace self-stored water every six months (Ready.gov) [50]; do a full kit review once a year (this guide's judgment). See the [practice schedule](#training-to-take-before-you-need-it).

## Build an offline library

### What goes in it

- This repository's quick-reference cards (see [Use this repository offline](#use-this-repository-offline)).
- From Ready.gov: the Emergency Supply List, the Family Communication Plan card, the Hazard Information Sheets and the "Are You Ready?" guide [3]. That guide is FEMA publication P-2064, dated September 2020, 36 pages [4].
- Your household plan, medicine list, allergy list and phone numbers, on paper.
- Local maps, and optionally one book ([books](#books-and-manuals-what-each-one-is)).

### Steps

1. **Download on Wi-Fi.** Save each file to the phone's storage, not just as an open browser tab. If a file shows a cloud or download icon, it is not stored yet.
2. **Test in airplane mode.** Turn on airplane mode, close the app and open each file.
3. **Print the short items,** double-sided, in a large font. Ready.gov's checklist has a printable version [1].
4. **Store paper with the kit,** in a waterproof, portable container; Ready.gov says the same for important family documents [1].
5. **Keep two digital copies:** the phone, plus a second device or a USB drive. Ready.gov suggests a password-protected jump drive or the cloud for documents, and a scanning app for IDs, insurance and medical records [6]. The cloud is a backup, not your offline copy.
6. **Download offline maps** for your area and test them in airplane mode. One example is Organic Maps, a free app whose listing says search and navigation work fully offline [46].
7. **Program the weather radio** (setup is in [`08_communication.md`](08_communication.md)).
8. **Write the date** on each copy. Replace it when a newer version exists.

### Rights: what you may copy

- U.S. federal government works are not eligible for copyright [45]. Ready.gov says its publication files are free to download, may be reproduced unaltered, and must not be used to suggest FEMA endorses a product [3].
- Red Cross, American Heart Association and commercial book content is copyrighted (general practice). Link to it and print for your own household; do not republish it.
- Hesperian's "Where There Is No Doctor" is copyrighted. Its license allows non-commercial copying with credit, and asks for permission for any digital format or more than 100 print copies [42].

### What goes wrong

- **A link, not a file, or everything on one phone.** Bookmarks do nothing offline. Save the file, and keep paper and a second copy.
- **Old copies.** Ready.gov states its publication files are current as of September 30, 2025 and will not be regularly updated after that date [3]. For anything with a date, check the live page's "last updated" line when you can [1][7].

## Books and manuals: what each one is

None is required. Read this repository first, then add one.

- **ATP 3-50.21, Survival (U.S. Army, 2018).** It revises the Army's older FM 3-05.70, and the Internet Archive copy carries a Public Domain Mark [41]. Prefer it to the older FM 21-76 and FM 3-05.70. It was written for soldiers in isolated situations; its medical chapter follows Army combat-casualty drills, and chapter 4 includes a plant edibility test [41]. This repository does not teach that test: positive ID or do not consume. For first aid, use [`06_first_aid.md`](06_first_aid.md).
- **SAS Survival Handbook, Third Edition, by John "Lofty" Wiseman.** Mariner Books, November 2014, 672 pages [43]. It is a copyrighted commercial book: buy or borrow it, do not photocopy it.
- **Where There Is No Doctor, by David Werner.** Published by Hesperian Health Guides (copyright 1977, 1992; 22nd printing, March 2025) [42]. It is a village health care handbook for places without clinics, with a PDF on Hesperian's English resources page [49]. This repository gives no prescription-drug doses; for any medicine, follow your clinician and the product label (this guide's rule).
- **A field guide to edible wild plants of Eastern and Central North America, by Lee Peterson.** Houghton Mifflin, 1978. It is not public domain, and the Internet Archive copy is a restricted, borrow-only scan [44]. Buy used. No book replaces an expert for a plant or mushroom with a deadly look-alike (this guide's rule).

## Training to take before you need it

- **First aid, CPR and AED** (automated external defibrillator; see [`06_first_aid.md`](06_first_aid.md)). The Red Cross Adult First Aid/CPR/AED blended course has an online part (about 2 hours 5 minutes) and an instructor-led skills session; the digital certificate is valid for two years [15]. The American Heart Association's Heartsaver CPR AED course comes in classroom, blended and self-guided formats, each with a card valid for two years; blended and self-guided still require a hands-on skills session [20]. Prices vary by provider.
- **Stop the Bleed** (American College of Surgeons). It teaches direct pressure, wound packing and applying a tourniquet. In-person instructors keep working with you until you show the skill, and a virtual course is also offered [14].
- **Wilderness and Remote First Aid (Red Cross).** For places without quick EMS (emergency medical services, the ambulance response), including urban disasters. You need current adult CPR/AED certification and must be 14 or older [16].
- **CERT (Community Emergency Response Team).** FEMA says basic training covers fire safety, light search and rescue, team organization and disaster medical operations [11]. FEMA's free online course IS-317 takes 2 hours and does not replace classroom training [11][13]. FEMA's pages do not state the class hours or cost, and programs are run locally, so ask your local emergency management office [11][12]. Local programs often run about 20 hours (general practice).
- **Amateur radio.** ARRL (see above) lists three license classes (Technician, General, Extra). The Technician exam has 35 questions, and a license is good for 10 years [36]. Fees apply: the FCC application fee is $35 for a new license, a renewal and a new vanity call sign [47]. ARRL also describes a reduced $5 exam session fee, paid to the exam team, for candidates under 18 [37]; fees change, so confirm current amounts before you go.
- **Hunter education,** if you hunt. Rules differ by state; the IHEA-USA (International Hunter Education Association-USA) state map lists requirements and classes [38].

**Practice schedule.** Check radio, flashlights and food and water dates every three months (this guide's judgment); replace self-stored water every six months (Ready.gov) [50]; do a full kit review once a year (this guide's judgment). The yearly review: open every file in airplane mode, replace batteries, update numbers, swap in new editions, and run a few hours without power using only your kit. Learn the skills in [`02_water.md`](02_water.md), [`03_fire.md`](03_fire.md), [`04_shelter.md`](04_shelter.md), [`06_first_aid.md`](06_first_aid.md) and [`07_navigation.md`](07_navigation.md) in calm, with someone who knows them.

## How to judge a source in a hurry

Ask these five questions. Two "no" answers mean do not act on it for a life-safety decision (this guide's rule). Weigh them: an official publisher and specific guidance count more than a date, so an official page with no date is not a "no" on that alone.

1. **Who published it?** A government agency, a university or extension service, the Red Cross or a medical society is an official source. USA.gov says a .gov website belongs to an official government organization in the United States [48]. A video channel, forum or social post is not official, however confident.
2. **How old is it?** Look for "last updated" or "last reviewed." Ready.gov pages show one [1][7]. If there is none, treat it as old (weighting above).
3. **Does it show its evidence?** Good sources name where the numbers come from, or give specific, checkable instructions.
4. **Does a second official source agree?** Skip this one if no other official source covers the topic. Agencies sometimes differ on small details. When they do, this repository says so and picks the safer choice.
5. **Is it free of a sales pitch and fear-selling?** A sales pitch or a scare is a "no" (this guide's judgment).

**Videos.** Use them to learn a skill in calm, then practice it. Do not decide from one during an emergency.

**AI assistants.** An assistant is a way to find things, not an authority (this guide's rule). Ask it to quote the exact file and the source behind the answer. This repository's answer policy requires it to cite the entries it used and to lead with uncertainty on anything you might eat. No source shown: do not act on its answer for water, food, medicine or injury. Source named: open it and check that it says what the assistant claims; assistants can invent citations.

## Use this repository offline

1. **Get the whole thing.** On Wi-Fi, download the repository as a ZIP from its GitHub page (github.com/daharmattan1/survival-essentials) or clone it on a computer. About 15 MB on 2026-10-21: about 1.2 MB of text and about 13.6 MB of photos in `_media` (this guide's measurement).
2. **Keep the folder structure.** Links between files are relative, so a file moved alone loses its links.
3. **Open it with any text or markdown viewer.** Start with [`README.md`](README.md).
4. **Print the cards.** Each guide ends with a quick-reference card: [`01_core_principles.md`](01_core_principles.md), [`02_water.md`](02_water.md), [`03_fire.md`](03_fire.md), [`04_shelter.md`](04_shelter.md), [`05_food.md`](05_food.md), [`06_first_aid.md`](06_first_aid.md), [`07_navigation.md`](07_navigation.md), [`08_communication.md`](08_communication.md), [`09_urban_survival.md`](09_urban_survival.md) and [`11_essential_gear.md`](11_essential_gear.md), plus this chapter's card below. Print the ones for your likeliest emergencies and keep them with your kit.
5. **For an AI assistant,** point it at [`AGENTS.md`](AGENTS.md) first (layers, retrieval recipes, answer policy). Food, plant and mushroom questions go through [`regional/_index.md`](regional/_index.md).
6. **Region packs.** Plants, mushrooms, game and local hazards change by region. The only pack today is Mid-Atlantic and Appalachian. Elsewhere, do not use it for food or hazards (this guide's rule); use your state wildlife agency and extension service.
7. **The no-power layer.** The [README](README.md#the-no-power-backup-physical-book-layer) lists the paper and PDF backups.

## Worked scenario A: a household binder in one afternoon

You have three hours on a Saturday and a printer. The goal is one waterproof folder per household, plus a copy on each adult's phone.

1. **Download (30 minutes, on Wi-Fi).** Everything under [What goes in it](#what-goes-in-it) [3][4], plus this repository as a ZIP.
2. **Fill in the plan (45 minutes).** Use the Family Emergency Plan form: contacts, meeting places, medical information [2]. Add the Poison Help number, 1-800-222-1222 [39].
3. **Print (30 minutes).** The plan, the supply list, and the cards for water, first aid and home outages. Double-sided, large font.
4. **Sort (15 minutes).** Folder one: plan, contacts, medicine and allergy list. Folder two: cards. Keep the 36-page guide on the phone and print only the pages for your top hazards [4].
5. **Phone test (30 minutes).** On each phone, save the files, switch to airplane mode and open each. Download your area's offline map [46].
6. **Radio and power (30 minutes).** Check the radio's batteries and your county's SAME (county alert) code. Put a charged backup battery in the folder bag [1][22].
7. **Date and calendar.** Write the date on each copy. Set a yearly reminder for the full review (this guide's judgment), a six-month reminder to replace self-stored water (Ready.gov) [50], and a three-month reminder for the radio, flashlights, and food and water dates (this guide's judgment).

**What goes wrong.** Files saved as links that fail in airplane mode. A folder only one person can find. Tell everyone where it is.

## Worked scenario B: a phone at 20 percent in a long outage

The power has been out for hours. Your only working device is a phone at 20 percent. No one is hurt.

1. **Use the phone only for what you need.** Turn on low power mode or airplane mode, and keep use to a minimum [6].
2. **Use paper first.** Read the printed card for what you face, such as [home outages](09_urban_survival.md). The card costs no battery.
3. **Open only the saved file you need.** It was downloaded in advance, so it needs no signal. Do not open a browser.
4. **Text, don't call.** Texts need less bandwidth and often get through when calls fail [6].
5. **Get news from the radio,** not the phone: a battery-powered or hand-crank radio or a NOAA Weather Radio [1][5].
6. **Charge without creating a carbon monoxide risk.** Use a backup battery from your kit [6]. Never run a car in a garage, even with the door open (this guide's rule; the CDC says never leave the motor running in a vehicle parked in an enclosed or partially enclosed space, such as a garage [8]). If you charge from a car at all, park it outdoors with the exhaust clear. Best is a power bank charged before the outage. The full rules are in [`08_communication.md`](08_communication.md) and [`09_urban_survival.md`](09_urban_survival.md).
7. **Write down what you decide and the time,** on paper. If the phone dies, the plan survives.
8. **Hurt or in danger?** Call 911 if you can. Do not wait for the battery to be higher.

**What goes wrong.** Scrolling for news and draining the battery. Searching for a procedure you could have saved. If you must search, apply the [five questions](#how-to-judge-a-source-in-a-hurry).

## Worked scenario C: a confident post about a wild mushroom

A neighbor texts a photo and a post saying the mushroom in their yard is good to eat. A chatbot agrees.

1. **Do not eat it, and tell the neighbor not to.** Positive ID or do not consume, and a photo or chatbot is not a positive ID (this guide's rule).
2. **Check the source.** Who wrote the post? When? What evidence? Two "no" answers mean stop ([judging a source](#how-to-judge-a-source-in-a-hurry)).
3. **Route to the right place.** For a mushroom, open [`regional/_index.md`](regional/_index.md) and the mushroom doctrine for your region. With no pack for your region, use your state extension service and an expert in person.
4. **If someone already ate it,** call Poison Help at 1-800-222-1222 [39]. Call 911 if the person collapses, has a seizure, has trouble breathing or cannot be woken [39][40]. Keep a sample and the photo for the clinician (general practice).

**What goes wrong.** Counting a post and a chatbot as two sources. They may both be wrong in the same way (this guide's judgment).

## Common mistakes

- **Treating the internet as a backup.** Download and print before you need it ([build](#build-an-offline-library)).
- **Reading instead of practicing.** Take the classes ([training](#training-to-take-before-you-need-it)).
- **Using an unofficial source for a life-safety choice** ([judging](#how-to-judge-a-source-in-a-hurry)).
- **Copying a dose, time or distance from memory.** Use the matching guide or the product label.
- **Buying books instead of getting trained** (this guide's judgment).
- **Using a region pack that is not yours** for food or local hazards.
- **Never checking.** Check radio, flashlights and food and water dates every three months (this guide's judgment); replace self-stored water every six months [50]; do a full kit review once a year (this guide's judgment). See the [schedule](#training-to-take-before-you-need-it).

## Quick-reference card (print on one page)

**CALL FIRST**

- Emergency: 911.
- Poison: 1-800-222-1222 (America's Poison Centers), free, 24 hours a day [39]. Collapsed, seizing, trouble breathing or cannot be woken: 911 [39][40].

**HAVE ON PAPER AND ON THE PHONE**

- Household plan, Family Communication Plan card, supply list [2][3].
- "Are You Ready?" guide (FEMA P-2064, 36 pages) [4].
- Medicine and allergy list, phone numbers, local map [1][33].

**BUILD THE OFFLINE LIBRARY**

1. Download on Wi-Fi: files, not links; this repository as a ZIP, folders kept together.
2. Test every file in airplane mode.
3. Print the cards and short items, double-sided, large font.
4. Store paper with the kit in a waterproof container [1]. Keep a second digital copy on another device or USB drive [6]. Date each copy.
5. Check yearly (see CHECK ON A SCHEDULE).

**POWER AND RADIO**

- Battery or hand-crank radio, extra batteries, charger, backup battery [1].
- NOAA Weather Radio: SAME (county-level alerts), AC adapter plus batteries, all seven frequencies (162.400 to 162.550 MHz) [21][22].
- SAME code: 1-888-NWR-SAME (1-888-697-7263) [22].
- Low power or airplane mode. Text rather than call family when networks are busy, unless you are in danger: call 911 first [6].
- Turn on Wireless Emergency Alerts. FEMA app: up to five locations [5].

**TRAIN**

- First aid, CPR, AED: certificate valid two years (Red Cross, AHA) [15][20].
- Stop the Bleed: direct pressure, wound packing, tourniquet [14].
- CERT: ask your local emergency management office [11][12].
- Amateur radio: Technician exam is 35 questions; license good for 10 years [36].

**JUDGE A SOURCE**

- Who published it? How old? Evidence shown? Second official source agrees? Free of selling and scaring?
- Two "no" answers: do not act on it (this guide's rule). An official publisher and specific guidance count more than a date.
- An AI answer with no source is not a source. Open any source it names and check it.

**CHECK ON A SCHEDULE (the three-month and yearly parts are this guide's judgment)**

- Every 3 months: check radio, flashlights, and food and water dates.
- Every 6 months: replace self-stored water (Ready.gov) [50].
- Yearly: full kit review. Replace batteries, update numbers, open every file in airplane mode, swap in new editions.
