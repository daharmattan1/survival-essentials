# Core Principles: What to Do First, and How to Use This Guide

Read this guide first. It tells you what to do, in what order, when something has gone wrong: you are lost on a trail, stuck in a car in snow, or sitting at home with the power out. The other ten guides hold the details. This one tells you which to open and when.

First aid in this guide is commonly taught field response for when help is delayed. It is not medical advice. If you can reach 911, get out of immediate danger and call.

Bleeding: [`06_first_aid.md`](06_first_aid.md#severe-bleeding). Not breathing: [`06_first_aid.md`](06_first_aid.md#not-breathing-and-choking).
- **You are stuck in a car right now:** stay in it. See [vehicle as shelter](04_shelter.md#vehicle-as-shelter) and [Scenario B](#worked-scenario-b-a-winter-storm-warning-for-tomorrow).
- **You are lost outdoors:** [STOP](#stop-stop-think-observe-plan), then [stay put and be found](#lost-outdoors-stay-put-and-be-found), then [Scenario A](#worked-scenario-a-a-lost-day-hiker).

- **You are hurt but not bleeding badly:** [`06_first_aid.md`](06_first_aid.md).

## What hurts you first

Four things hurt people in a survival situation, and they work on very different clocks.

1. **Severe bleeding or a blocked airway.** These can kill in minutes (this guide's judgment).
2. **Temperature.** Cold, wet and wind, or heat, work in hours. Hypothermia (dangerously low body temperature) can start above 40 F if rain, sweat or cold water has you chilled [9]. About 20% of cold-related injuries happen inside the home [9].
3. **Not drinking.** This works in days, not weeks [5][16]. Hot weather raises how much you need [16].
4. **Not eating.** This works in weeks, but your strength and judgment fall long before that [4].

A fifth thing hurts you in all four: acting too fast. The Army survival manual warns: "Don't move just for the sake of taking action" [4].

## The order of priorities

Call 911 the moment you can reach it, once you are out of immediate danger. The list below is the order for what your hands do. This order is this guide's synthesis of the Army survival manual, the FAA's survival course and National Park Service lost-person guidance [1][4][5]. Where the guides below are linked, they hold the steps.

1. **Immediate danger.** Get out of it, then call 911 if you can. Get away from fire, rising water, traffic, a gas smell, carbon monoxide (CO, an invisible gas from burning fuel), or anything about to fall (this guide's rule). Then stop and think.
2. **Life-threatening injury.** Treat it before anything else below. The Army manual puts it first: "If injured, first aid has top priority no matter what climate you are in" [4].
   - Severe bleeding: press directly on the wound with a clean cloth or clothing, and keep pressing until the bleeding stops. For life-threatening bleeding from an arm or leg, put on a tourniquet, and note the time you put it on [10][11]. Get emergency help [10]. Steps: [severe bleeding](06_first_aid.md#severe-bleeding).
   - The three actions taught by Stop the Bleed (a program of the American College of Surgeons) are direct pressure, packing the wound, and a tourniquet [11]. Take a course before you need it.
   - Someone not breathing or unresponsive: call 911. Start CPR (chest compressions) if you are trained, or follow the dispatcher's instructions (general practice). Steps: [not breathing](06_first_aid.md#not-breathing-and-choking).
3. **Temperature and shelter.** Get out of the wind, rain and sun. The Army manual notes that in some places the need for shelter takes precedence over the need for food and possibly even water [4]. If someone is sleepy, confused or slurring speech in the cold, call 911 right away [9]. See [`04_shelter.md`](04_shelter.md) and [`03_fire.md`](03_fire.md).
4. **Being found.** If you have not called yet, call 911 if you have service. Signal. Stay where searchers will look. See [lost outdoors](#lost-outdoors-stay-put-and-be-found) and [`08_communication.md`](08_communication.md).
5. **Water.** Keep drinking. Do not ration water you have; ration effort instead. Treat questionable water first. See [`02_water.md`](02_water.md).
6. **Fire, as a tool.** Use it for warmth, signaling and treating water. Fire is not a need in itself, and it creates carbon monoxide and wildfire risk. See [`03_fire.md`](03_fire.md).
7. **Food.** Eat last. See [`05_food.md`](05_food.md). This guide never says a wild plant, mushroom or animal is edible. Any question about eating something from the wild goes to [`regional/_index.md`](regional/_index.md) and your region's `food_hub.md`. Positive identification, or do not consume.

**Reorder when the situation changes.** The Army manual says to change your survival pattern "to meet your immediate physical needs as the environment changes" [4]. If you are warm and sheltered, water moves up. If you are rescued, nothing on this list matters.

**What goes wrong.** People skip to step 5 to 7 because water, fire and food feel like action. They also build a fire before dealing with a bleeding wound or wet clothes. Go in order.

## STOP: Stop, Think, Observe, Plan

STOP is a pause before you act. The Forest Service teaches it as Stop, Think, Observe, Plan [3]. The FAA survival course teaches the same letters as Sit, Think, Observe, Plan [5]. The Army manual does not use STOP. It uses the word SURVIVAL (Size up the situation, Undue haste makes waste, Remember where you are, Vanquish fear and panic, Improvise, Value living, Act like the natives, Live by your wits) [4]. The idea is the same in all three: slow down before you move.

1. **Stop.** As soon as you realize you may be lost: "stop, stay calm, stay put." The Forest Service calls panic your greatest enemy [3].
2. **Think.** Recall how you got here and what landmarks you should have seen [3]. Ask whether anyone is hurt and whether you are in danger right now (general practice).
3. **Observe.** Check injuries and take stock of what you carry [5]. Note the weather and how much daylight is left (general practice). Use a compass if you have one, and do not walk aimlessly [3].
4. **Plan.** Consider your options and act on one. If you are not confident of the route, stay put. If it is nightfall, you are injured, or you are near exhaustion, stay in place [3]. Keep the plan small: one to three things you can do in the next hour (this guide's judgment).

**What goes wrong.**

- Skipping STOP and running. The Army manual warns that acting in haste can lose equipment and leave you disoriented [4].
- Observing without acting. Finish with a first task, such as putting on layers.
- Planning something too big, such as "walk out by dark." Pick the next hour.

Repeat STOP when the situation changes or when you notice panic returning (this guide's judgment).

## The rule of threes: a teaching tool, not physiology

You will see this rule everywhere. In its common form you can last about **3 minutes without air, 3 hours without shelter in harsh conditions, 3 days without water and 3 weeks without food**. It is a teaching heuristic (a memory aid for setting priorities), not a measurement. The sources disagree.

- The FAA's survival course: air 3 minutes, shelter 3 hours, rest 30 hours, water 3 days, food 30 days [5].
- The Army survival manual has no such numbers. It says only that you can live "several weeks" without food, and warns not to count on going for days without it [4].
- Colorado State University Extension: weeks without food, only a few days without water [16].
- The real times depend on heat, cold, wet, age, health and effort (this guide's judgment). The FAA says life expectancy without water in extreme conditions is three days [5].
- The 3 minutes in icy water that some versions mention is not a survival time. The National Weather Service describes cold shock: gasping and rapid breathing in the first 2 to 3 minutes or more, which can cause drowning even in confident swimmers. Loss of muscle control starts quickly, and hypothermia begins at a core temperature of 95 F [8].

**How to use it.** Do not memorize the numbers. Remember the order they imply: what stops the body fastest comes first. Then add what the rule leaves out: injury first, and signaling so that someone can find you.

## Lost outdoors: stay put and be found

Help can take hours or days depending on your location, weather and resources [1][2]. For downed aircraft, FAA data put the average time from last known position to rescue at about 27.3 hours [5]. This guide's judgment for hikers: plan for a night out.

1. **STOP.** If you know the way back and the route is clear, you can retrace your steps [2]. If you are not sure, the Forest Service says to stay put [3]. Where they differ, take the more cautious choice: stay (this guide's rule).
2. **Call 911 if you have service.** Give them: GPS coordinates from your phone, the trail or area where you started, your last known location, what you can see around you, your clothing, your equipment and any medical conditions [2].
3. **Stay put.** "Changing location will make it difficult for authorities to find you." Move only if there is an immediate threat to your life [2]. Teach children the Hug-A-Tree rule: find a tree, hug it, and stay put [1].
4. **Shelter and warmth.** Find a place out of the sun, rain or snow. Put on every layer. Check what you packed [2]. Do not build a fire if fire risk is high or it is wildfire season [2].
5. **Signal.** Blow a whistle three times, rest, and blow three times again. This saves energy compared to shouting. Flash a flashlight in the SOS pattern: three times quickly, three slowly, three quickly. Use the flashlight sparingly. Flash a mirror at planes and helicopters. Lay out shelter material or rocks in a shape an aircraft can see [2].
6. **Conserve energy.** Wait [2]. Do not sweat in the cold; sweat leads to a chill and hypothermia [7].
7. **Keep the group together.** Set a pace that works for everyone so no one is left behind [1]. If someone in your group becomes lost, stop, retrace your steps as a group to the last place you saw them, wait there, do not search off trail, and call 911 if you have service [1].

**Before you go.** Fill out a trip plan and leave it with someone who is not going [1]. Tell them the trailhead, the route and when you expect to return [3]. Tell them when to call for help if you have not checked in (general practice). Carry a map, a whistle, a mirror, bright clothing and the 10 Essentials (the standard list of outdoor safety items, see [`11_essential_gear.md`](11_essential_gear.md)) [2].

**What goes wrong.**

- Walking farther "to get a signal" or to find a road at dusk.
- Using up the phone battery. Low-power mode and text messages help (general practice).
- Staying put does not guarantee a quick rescue. It makes you findable. Keep warm, dry and visible.

## Stay or go

Official orders override everything in this section.

- **Evacuation order:** go. Always follow the instructions of local officials. Leave early enough to avoid being trapped by severe weather. Follow recommended evacuation routes, because shortcuts may be blocked. Do not drive into flooded areas [12].
- **Shelter in place** (stay where you are, indoors) fits when outside conditions are dangerous, for example when authorities say the air is badly contaminated. Use common sense and available information to judge whether there is immediate danger, and keep following local authorities [13].
- **Stranded in a vehicle in a winter storm:** stay in it. "If you leave your vehicle, you will become disoriented quickly in wind-driven snow and cold" [7]. See [Scenario B](#worked-scenario-b-a-winter-storm-warning-for-tomorrow).
- **Home, power out:** stay if the building is intact, you can keep warm, and no order has been given. Go when ordered, or when the home becomes hazardous (CO alarm, gas smell, fire, flood), or when you cannot keep warm or cool or run a medical device (this guide's judgment). The trigger table is in [`09_urban_survival.md`](09_urban_survival.md).
- **Lost outdoors:** stay, as above.

**What goes wrong.** People wait past an order because the house still looks fine. People also leave a working shelter during a storm to look for something better. Either way, make the choice before you are cold and tired.

## Fear, stress and the will to survive

Fear is expected. The Army manual says there is no shame in it, and that fear can help because it makes you cautious. It can also freeze you [4].

**What stress does.** Your body prepares to fight or flee: breathing and heart rate rise and muscles tense. Fear, anxiety, anger, frustration, guilt, depression and loneliness are all possible reactions to survival stress [4]. You cannot hold that level of alertness forever. Stressors add up, and exhaustion follows [4]. Signs of too much stress include trouble making decisions, angry outbursts, forgetfulness, constant worry, careless mistakes, and withdrawing from others [4]. Thoughts of suicide appear on the Army's list of distress signs too [4]. If you or someone with you has them, call or text 988 [17].

**What helps.**

- **Name the reaction.** Fear, anxiety, anger, frustration, guilt and sadness are normal reactions to an abnormal situation [4]. Do not try to argue yourself out of the feeling. Do the next task anyway (this guide's judgment).
- **Be realistic.** "Hope for the best, prepare for the worst" [4].
- **Keep your mind busy with useful work.** The Army manual advises finding ways to keep your mind productively occupied during loneliness and boredom [4]. The FAA advises working the mind toward positive and productive thoughts [5].
- **Keep a routine.** Regular meals, enough rest, a sleep routine, little caffeine, and reaching out to people who help you cope all appear in NIMH's stress guidance [17]. Eat and sleep when you can (this guide's judgment).
- **Slow your breathing.** NIMH lists deep breathing among relaxation exercises [17]. No special count is needed.
- **Will to survive.** The Army manual calls it "the refusal to give up" [4]. The FAA describes it as hope, determination, goals or a positive mental attitude, and says your chances are greatly diminished without it [5]. It is not a replacement for shelter, water or signaling.

**Groups.** The FAA advises splitting equipment and tasks among survivors by responsibility area, such as signaling, first aid and water [5]. Add one more role (this guide's judgment): name one person to make the final call when the group disagrees. Check on each other. The Army lists withdrawing from others as a distress sign [4].

**What goes wrong.** Anger and frustration lead to impulsive choices [4]. Depression turns "What can I do" into "There is nothing I can do" [4]. Stop, name what is happening, and take one small action.

## Before it happens

Most of survival is done before the emergency (this guide's judgment).

- **Plan with your household.** Ask how you will get alerts, where you will shelter, your evacuation route, how you will contact each other, and whether your kit needs updating. Pick a meeting place. Practice the plan [14].
- **Build a kit for several days.** Ready.gov says you may need to survive on your own for several days. Store one gallon of water per person per day, a several-day supply of non-perishable food, a radio, a flashlight, a whistle, a first aid kit and phone chargers. Keep a kit in your car [15]. See [`11_essential_gear.md`](11_essential_gear.md).
- **Test your gear and skills.** Practice with your compass or GPS. Pack extra batteries [2].
- **Learn bleeding control and first aid** from a course [11].
- **Two ways for critical jobs** (general practice): two ways to light a fire, two ways to signal, two ways to treat water.

## Worked scenario A: a lost day-hiker

*An illustration.* It is a cool October afternoon, about 45 F. Dana is on a day hike in a park, about three miles from the trailhead. She left a trip plan with a friend and expects to be back by 6 p.m. At 2 p.m. she realizes she has not seen a trail marker in a while.

1. **Stop.** She sits down, breathes, and stops walking [3]. She is not hurt.
2. **Think and observe.** She recalls a junction with a sign about 20 minutes back. She checks her map and phone. She is not sure which way it is. She has a jacket, a whistle, a mirror, a space blanket, two liters of water and snacks [2][3]. About four hours of daylight remain.
3. **Plan.** She is not confident of the route, so she stays [3]. She has one bar of service and calls 911. She gives the coordinates from her phone, the trailhead, her last known location, a description of what she sees, her clothing and gear, and her medical conditions [2]. She puts the phone in low-power mode (general practice).
4. **Shelter.** She puts on every layer and sits on her pack. Fire risk is high in the park that week, so she does not light a fire [2].
5. **Signal.** She blows the whistle three times, rests, and blows three more. She keeps the flashlight for dark [2].
6. **Dusk.** She is cold but dry. She wraps in the space blanket and stays still to avoid sweating [7]. She drinks as she needs to rather than rationing (see [`02_water.md`](02_water.md)).
7. **Evening.** Searchers hear the whistle and find her about 4 hours later (an illustration, not a statistic).

**What went right.** A trip plan, a call that gave exact details, and not moving. **What went wrong.** She did not check the map at the junction, which is where the trouble started. The NPS advises checking junctions and landmarks and confirming your route often [2].

## Worked scenario B: a winter storm warning for tomorrow

*An illustration.* Your town is under a winter storm warning that starts tomorrow morning. You live in a house with a gas furnace and a car.

A winter storm watch means a blizzard, heavy snow or heavy freezing rain is possible, usually issued 12 to 48 hours ahead. A warning means hazardous winter weather is imminent, usually issued 12 to 24 hours ahead. Under a warning, find shelter right away [6].

**Today**

1. Gather supplies for several days without power [6]. Store one gallon of water per person per day [15]. Charge phones and power banks.
2. Fill the car's gas tank. Put blankets, warm clothes, sand and jumper cables in the car [6]. Carry a charged phone and a charger [7].
3. Check that CO alarms work on every level of the house [6]. If the power may go out, the rule is: generators and fuel outdoors at least 20 ft from windows, doors, vents and attached garages, and never heat the home with a gas stovetop or oven [6]. The full rule is in [`03_fire.md`](03_fire.md).
4. Refill needed medicines and plan for devices that need power (general practice; see [`09_urban_survival.md`](09_urban_survival.md)).
5. Decide now: if you must drive, tell someone your route and destination [7]. The better choice is to stay off the roads [6].

**During the storm**

6. Limit time outside. Wear layers. Avoid overexertion when shoveling [6]. Sweating can lead to a chill and hypothermia [7].
7. Staying indoors reduces the risk of crashes and falls, but there are hazards inside the home too [18]. Keep warm in one room (general practice). Watch everyone for sleepiness, confusion or slurred speech, and call 911 right away if you see it [9].
8. If the power goes out, follow [`09_urban_survival.md`](09_urban_survival.md): the fridge clock, water, and when to leave for a warming location.

**If you are stranded in the car**

9. Stay inside the car [6][7]. Run the engine about 10 minutes each hour for heat [7]. Keep the exhaust pipe clear of snow first; that is the main CO control [7]. A slightly open window for fresh air lowers the risk but does not remove it (this guide's rule, matching [`04_shelter.md`](04_shelter.md)). Never sleep with the engine running, and if drifting snow keeps burying the pipe, leave the engine off. The engine runs outdoors only, never in a garage. Full vehicle rules: [`04_shelter.md`](04_shelter.md#vehicle-as-shelter).
10. Make the car easy to find. Use the dome light at night while the engine runs, tie a bright cloth to the antenna or door, and raise the hood once the snow stops [7].
11. Move your arms, legs, fingers and toes regularly [7]. Use blankets, floor mats and clothes for insulation (general practice).

**What goes wrong.** People leave the car on foot and get disoriented [7]. They run the engine with the tailpipe blocked. They wait too long to prepare.

## Common mistakes

- **Treating the rule of threes as exact.** See [the rule of threes](#the-rule-of-threes-a-teaching-tool-not-physiology).
- **Moving when lost** instead of staying put [2][3].
- **Leaving a stranded vehicle on foot** [7].
- **Skipping the injury** to get started on fire, water or food [4].
- **Rationing water** instead of effort. See [`02_water.md`](02_water.md).
- **Burning anything indoors** or in a tent or vehicle. See [`03_fire.md`](03_fire.md).
- **Waiting past an evacuation order** [12].
- **Eating anything wild because a book or a photo suggests it.** See [`regional/_index.md`](regional/_index.md).
- **No trip plan** [1].

## Quick-reference card (print on one page)

**FIRST, ALWAYS**

- Get out of immediate danger, then call 911 if you can.
- Then, in order: bleeding or breathing, cold or heat, being found, water, fire as a tool, food.
- Sleepy, confused or slurring speech in the cold: call 911 right away [9].

**FASTEST KILLERS**

- Severe bleeding: press hard on the wound with clean cloth and keep pressing [10][11].
- Life-threatening bleeding from an arm or leg: put on a tourniquet and note the time [10][11]. Steps: [`06_first_aid.md`](06_first_aid.md#severe-bleeding).
- Not breathing: call 911. Start CPR if trained, or follow the dispatcher (general practice). For an adult who collapses, hands-only CPR is pushing hard and fast in the center of the chest. Steps: [`06_first_aid.md`](06_first_aid.md#not-breathing-and-choking).

**STOP**

- Stop, Think, Observe, Plan [3]. The FAA says Sit, Think, Observe, Plan [5].
- Not sure of the route, nightfall, injured or exhausted: stay put [3].

**RULE OF THREES**

- A teaching shortcut, not a medical rule. Remember the order, not the numbers [4][5][16].

**LOST OUTDOORS**

- Stay put. Move only for an immediate threat to life [2].
- Call 911: coordinates, trailhead, last known location, surroundings, clothing, gear, medical conditions [2].
- Whistle: three blasts, rest, three again. Flashlight SOS: three quick, three slow, three quick. Mirror at aircraft [2].
- No fire if fire risk is high [2].
- Help can take hours or days [1][2].
- Leave a trip plan with someone who is not going [1].

**STAY OR GO**

- Evacuation order: go. Leave early. Follow officials and official routes [12].
- Stranded in a car in a winter storm: stay in it [6][7].
- Engine about 10 minutes each hour, exhaust clear first [7]; a cracked window lowers the risk but is not a fix (this guide's rule). Never sleep with it running.
- Generators outdoors at least 20 ft from windows, doors and attached garages [6].

**STORM TIMING**

- Watch: usually 12 to 48 hours ahead. Warning: usually 12 to 24 hours ahead. Under a warning, find shelter right away [6].

**WATER AND KIT**

- Store one gallon per person per day, for several days [15][16].

**MIND**

- Fear is normal. Name it, then do one small task [4].
- Keep a routine: meals, sleep, other people [17].
- Thoughts of suicide: call or text 988 [17].
