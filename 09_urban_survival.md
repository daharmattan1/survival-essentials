# Urban Survival: Riding Out a Power, Water, Heat or Cold Failure at Home

Use this guide when the power is out, the water is off or under a notice, or the weather is dangerous and you are staying put. It is written for households that may include kids, older relatives, or someone who depends on medicine or a machine. Nothing here needs the internet.

> **Act now**
>
> - **Carbon monoxide (CO) alarm sounds, or CO symptoms** (headache, dizziness, weakness, upset stomach, vomiting, chest pain, confusion): everyone outside now, then call 911 [28][12][11].
> - **Heat stroke signs** (confusion, slurred speech, hot dry skin or heavy sweating, seizures, a fast strong pulse, or a body temperature above 103 F): call 911 [3][21].
> - **Hypothermia signs** (sleepiness, confusion, slurred speech, fumbling hands, or a body temperature below 95 F): call 911 right away [2][36].
> - **A household member's powered device cannot run:** go where there is power [9]. Breathing trouble: call 911.
> - **You smell gas:** leave now without flipping switches or using a phone; call the gas company from outside [28].
> - **Evacuation order:** go [6].
>
> One-page summary to print: [quick-reference card](#quick-reference-card-print-on-one-page).

Outages can disrupt phones, water service and transport for a long time. They can also close stores, gas stations, ATMs and banks [9]. In 2024 the average US customer lost about 11 hours of power, about 9 of them from major events such as hurricanes [35]. That is a yearly average, and a single outage can run longer.

---

## What hurts you first at home

1. **Carbon monoxide (CO).** You cannot see or smell it. More than 400 Americans die each year from CO that does not come from house fires [12]. About 100 a year die from portable generators alone [28]. Sleeping people can die before they feel anything [11][12].
2. **Cold or heat.** Hypothermia can happen indoors, especially to older adults [36]. Extreme heat means temperatures above 90 F for two to three days [3]. Heat illness can start before then.
3. **Food that has warmed.** A closed fridge holds its cold for about 4 hours [11][13]. The discard rule is in [Food](#food-what-to-keep-and-what-to-throw-out).
4. **Water that has germs in it.** A loss of pressure can let contamination into pipes, which is why boil-water notices follow outages [31].
5. **Fire from candles and heaters.** The US Fire Administration, citing NFPA data, reports about 20 home candle fires a day [32].
6. **Lost medicine or a machine that needs power** [1][11].

For water treatment doses, see [`02_water.md`](02_water.md). For fire and heat outdoors, see [`03_fire.md`](03_fire.md).

## The sequence: first hour, first day, days 2 to 7, and when to leave

### First hour

1. List who in the house needs power or refrigerated medicine (see [Medicines and devices](#medicines-and-power-dependent-devices)) [1][5]. If a powered device cannot run, go where there is power [9].
2. Keep every fuel-burning thing outside. No generator, car, grill or camp stove in a garage or any indoor space, and no gas stove or oven for heat [1][2][11][12][28].
3. Check your CO alarms. You need battery or battery-backup alarms on every level and near sleeping areas [1][12].
4. Check your breaker, neighbors and utility. Ask local officials about open warming or cooling locations [1].
5. Close the fridge and freezer and keep them closed [1].
6. Find flashlights, one per person. Use flashlights and battery lanterns, not candles [1][11][32].
7. Fill clean containers with drinking water while the tap still runs. Aim for at least 1 gallon per person per day [16]. Then fill the bathtub, which is for flushing and washing only (see [Water](#water-store-it-find-it-treat-it)).
8. Put phones in low-power mode, and text instead of calling. Texts need less bandwidth and send more reliably [8].
9. Check on neighbors, especially anyone who lives alone or has a chronic condition [22].

### First day

1. Start a 4-hour clock on the fridge [13]. Put a thermometer in it if you have one. The discard rule is in [Food](#food-what-to-keep-and-what-to-throw-out).
2. Before the 4 hours are up, move perishables into a cooler with ice or frozen gel packs and keep them at 40 F or below [13][27].
3. Set up your toilet plan before the toilet stops working (see [Toilets](#toilets-and-hygiene-when-the-water-stops)).
4. Choose your heating or cooling plan (see [Warm or cool](#warm-or-cool)).
5. Keep a battery or hand-crank radio on for news [4].

### Days 2 to 7

1. Freezer clock: about 48 hours if full, about 24 hours if half-full [11][13]. Eat and cook thawing food first, then shelf-stable food (see [Food](#food-what-to-keep-and-what-to-throw-out)).
2. Watch for a boil-water notice. Treat any water you did not store [17].
3. Keep handwashing going and waste contained (see [Toilets](#toilets-and-hygiene-when-the-water-stops)).
4. Keep checking on neighbors. FEMA's Community Emergency Response Team (CERT) program trains community volunteers in basic disaster response skills [10].

### When to leave

"Leave" means a warming or cooling location, a friend with power, or a shelter. If an evacuation order comes, it overrides everything in this chapter. Always follow local officials, leave early, and leave a note saying when you left and where you went [6].

| Trigger | Action |
|---|---|
| Officials order evacuation | Go. Keep the gas tank at least half full. Contact your out-of-state person [6] |
| Heat illness signs or a home too hot to cool | Go to air conditioning. Call 911 for heat stroke [3][21] |
| You cannot stay warm | Go to a community warming location [1] |
| A household member's powered device cannot run | Go where there is power [9]. Breathing trouble: call 911 |
| CO alarm sounds or CO symptoms | Everyone outside now, then call 911 [28] |
| You smell or hear gas | Leave now; no light switches or phones until outside; call the gas company [28] |

If there is trouble outside your home, follow instructions from local officials and keep monitoring local media [7]. Know your evacuation routes and check on your neighbors [6][10].

## Carbon monoxide and fire: heat, light and cook without hurting anyone

**Generators.** Run one outdoors only, at least 20 ft from windows, doors, vents and attached garages. Never in a garage, basement, shed, crawlspace or any other enclosed or partly enclosed space, and opening doors or windows does not fix it [1][11][12][28]. Keep it dry, use a heavy-duty extension cord, and let it cool before refueling [1]. Battery or solar "power stations" make no fumes and can be used indoors [30].

What goes wrong: moving the generator "just inside the garage door" because of rain.

**Cars.** Never run a car in an attached garage, even with the door open [12][29]. Charge power banks before the outage instead [8].

**Never burn indoors:** charcoal, a grill, a lantern or a camp stove inside a home, tent or camper [11][12]. Never heat a home with a gas stove or oven [1][2][11][12][30]. Do not run an unvented fuel-burning heater (one with no vent to the outdoors, such as a kerosene or propane space heater) inside a home, tent, camper or vehicle. CPSC warns most strongly against one where people sleep [29], and EPA warns against any gas-burning heater in a closed or poorly vented room [30]; this guide's rule is no unvented heater indoors at all.

**Electric heaters.** Use one with automatic shut-off [20][28]. Keep it:

- off furniture and away from water
- 3 ft from beds, clothes and curtains
- plugged into a wall outlet, never a power strip

Do not leave it running while you sleep or when you leave the room [28].

**Fireplaces.** The fireplace must be code-compliant. Keep the damper (the movable plate in the chimney that opens and closes it) open until the ashes are cool. Never burn charcoal in it [11][30].

**Light.** Flashlights and battery lanterns, not candles [11][32].

**Cooking.** Eat cold food first. Grill or camp stove cooking goes outdoors, away from doors, windows and air intakes [30]. A vented appliance (one that sends its fumes outside through a flue or chimney) works only if that vent needs no power [30]. A gas range is for cooking only; never use it or the oven to heat the home [1][2][11][12][30]. While you cook on it, run the range hood if it vents outdoors [46] and open a window slightly if the weather allows, because EPA says ventilation matters especially while cooking [30]. Keep a working CO alarm. That is cooking practice, not a way to make burning fuel for heat indoors acceptable. Check your stove's manual for how to light it during an outage (general practice, not from a cited source).

If you smell or hear gas leaking, leave right away. Do not turn lights on or off or use a phone until you are outside. Call the gas company from outside [28]. Keep a fire extinguisher [4][11].

**CO alarms, before you need them.** Put battery or battery-backup CO alarms on every level and outside sleeping areas. Do not mount them in kitchens, near heating vents, or where furniture or drapes cover them [28][29]. Test monthly and check batteries when the clocks change. Replace alarms per the maker's date, or every 5 years [12][28]. A chirp means a dead battery (general practice, not from a cited source). Replace it.

**CO symptoms:** headache, dizziness, weakness, upset stomach, vomiting, chest pain and confusion [12]. If you suspect CO: get everyone into fresh air, then call 911 [28]. Poison Control is 1-800-222-1222 [11].

## Food: what to keep and what to throw out

**Before:** freeze water jugs to fill gaps in the freezer. Put a thermometer in the fridge (40 F or below) and the freezer (0 F or below) [13].

**The rule.** Keep doors closed. A closed fridge keeps food cold about 4 hours. A full freezer keeps it about 48 hours. A half-full freezer keeps it about 24 hours [11][13][25][27].

- No thermometer: once the power has been out longer than 4 hours, throw out perishables [13][25][27].
- Thermometer shows food at 40 F or higher for 2 hours or more: throw it out [1].
- Perishables left at room temperature for 2 hours go out, or 1 hour when it is above 90 F [25].
- Food kept in a cooler with ice at 40 F or below can stay [13][27].
- When in doubt, throw it out [27].

**Cold storage.** Do not put food in the snow [9][27]. Outside temperatures vary, and food can be exposed to unsanitary conditions and animals [27]. Make ice instead: fill buckets or cans with water and leave them outside to freeze [27].

**After power returns:** use a thermometer, not your nose. Never taste food to check it [13]. Frozen food with ice crystals, or at 40 F or below, can be cooked or refrozen [13][25][27]. For perishables, use the rule above.

**Throw out after 4 hours without power:** raw or cooked meat, poultry and fish, eggs, deli meat, leftovers, casseroles, cut fruit and vegetables, milk, soft cheese and creamy dressings [27].

**Shelf-stable:** mustard, ketchup, pickles, jams, whole fresh fruit and vegetables, dried fruit, nuts, hard cheese, butter, and bread without cream filling [27]. Honey keeps too, but never give it to a child under 12 months [24].

## Water: store it, find it, treat it

Plan for at least 1 gallon per person per day for 3 days. Two weeks is better. More if anyone is pregnant or sick, or it is hot [16][4].

**Drinking water comes from:** bottled water, stored water, the water-heater tank, the pipes, ice made from clean water, and the liquid in canned fruit and vegetables [15][34]. Clear toilet **tank** water (never the bowl) is a last resort, and only if it has no colored or chemical cleaner. Treat it first [15]. Red Cross guidance says to skip toilet tanks entirely [34].

**Never drink:** pool or spa water [15], bathtub water [33], or water from a radiator or boiler in a home heating system [14]. Pool and spa water is for flushing and washing [15]. Tub water is for cleaning and flushing [23][33]. Do not use water contaminated by fuel or chemicals. Boiling does not remove chemicals [14][15].

**Pipes:** shut the main valve to keep contaminants out. Open a faucet at the highest level to let air in. Draw water from the lowest faucet [34][15].

**Water heater:** the capacity is on the tank label (general practice, not from a cited source).

1. Turn the electricity off first [34]. For a gas heater, set the gas to pilot unless the heater's label says otherwise (see [`02_water.md`](02_water.md)).
2. Close the tank's water-intake valve (where cold water enters the tank) [34].
3. Open a hot-water faucet [34].
4. Open the drain at the bottom into a container. The water can be very hot, so wear gloves (see [`02_water.md`](02_water.md)).
5. Refill the tank before turning the gas or power back on. If the gas was shut off, a professional relights it [34].

**Boil-water notice.** A notice means tap water is or could be contaminated. Use bottled or boiled water for drinking, cooking, food prep and brushing teeth. Boil even if you filter [17].

- **Boil rule:** follow your utility's notice [31]. Otherwise: rolling boil (large bubbles that keep rising from the bottom of the pot) for 1 minute; 3 minutes if you are above 5,000 ft or (this guide's rule) not sure of your elevation (see [`02_water.md`](02_water.md)) [14][43].
- Clear cloudy water through a cloth or coffee filter first [14].
- Do not use ice or water from fridge dispensers during a notice, and discard ice made recently from the affected supply [17][31].
- For bleach doses, see [`02_water.md`](02_water.md).

**If you drank affected water:** if symptoms persist, seek medical advice [31]. Also call a clinician for diarrhea that will not stop, bloody stool, fever or signs of dehydration (general practice, not from a cited source).

What goes wrong: drinking tub water; filtering rainwater and calling it treated (filters miss viruses) [14]; assuming a notice is over because the water looks clear.

## Toilets and hygiene when the water stops

If your sewer still works (no backups, no officials telling you to stop flushing), pour a bucket of water into the bowl to flush (general practice, not from a cited source). If the sewer is down, use a bag toilet.

**Bag toilet:**

1. Turn off the toilet's water valve and flush out the remaining water [38].
2. Line the bowl with a plastic kitchen garbage bag, inside a second bag [38].
3. Put an absorbent (kitty litter, peat moss, or lime) in the inner bag [38].
4. When it is full, tie it off and replace it [38].
5. Store used bags away from kids, pets, wildlife and food [38][39].

A lined bucket works too [38]. Oregon State's design uses a heavy-duty 13-gallon bag, filled no more than half, then double-bagged and sealed [39]. Do not put human waste in regular trash, and ask local officials how to dispose of it [39].

**Hands:** wash with soap and water for 20 seconds. Build a handwashing station from a jug [18]. Use sanitizer (at least 60% alcohol) only when there is no soap and water. It does not work on visibly dirty or greasy hands [18].

**Dishes:** wash, rinse, then sanitize in 1 tablespoon unscented bleach per gallon of clean water [19][27]. Air dry. Never mix bleach with ammonia or any other cleaner [19]. During a boil-water notice, CDC's dish soak is weaker: 1 teaspoon per gallon for 1 minute (see [`02_water.md`](02_water.md)).

## Warm or cool

### Cold

1. Pick one small room and close off other rooms to avoid wasting heat [44]. Wear layers of loose, warm clothing and a hat indoors [44]. Use blankets and sleeping bags [11].
2. For older adults, keep the home at 68 to 70 F or warmer. Even 60 to 65 F can lead to hypothermia [36].
3. **Hypothermia signs:** shivering, exhaustion, confusion, fumbling hands, memory loss, slurred speech, drowsiness. Call 911 whenever you suspect hypothermia, and right away for sleepiness, confusion, slurred speech or fumbling hands, or a body temperature below 95 F [2][36][47]. While you wait, warm the chest, neck, head and groin first, keep the person dry, and wrap them in blankets [2].
4. **Frozen pipes:** let taps drip and open cabinet doors under sinks. Thaw with a hair dryer, never a torch [20].

### Heat

Extreme heat is above 90 F for two to three days. Older adults, children and people with chronic conditions are at higher risk [3].

1. Do not rely on a fan. Fans help only when the room is below 90 F. Above 90 F a fan can raise body temperature [22].
2. Take cool showers or baths. Wear loose, light-colored clothing. Close drapes and shades. Use the oven less [3].
3. Open windows only when the air outside is cooler than the air inside, and mind home security. If it is hotter outside, keep windows closed [30].
4. Drink plenty of fluids. Pale urine is a hydration check [22].
5. Go to a library, mall or cooling center. Dial 2-1-1 or call the health department for locations [3][22].
6. Check on older adults and neighbors who live alone [3][22]. Never leave people or pets in a closed car [3].

**Heat exhaustion:** headache, nausea, dizziness, weakness, thirst, heavy sweating. Move out of the heat, sip cool water, use cold compresses, and seek evaluation [21]. Call a provider if symptoms worsen or last more than an hour [3].

**Heat stroke:** confusion, slurred speech, hot dry skin or heavy sweating, seizures, a pulse that is fast and strong, or a body temperature above 103 F. Call 911. Move the person to shade, remove outer clothing, and cool quickly with cold water, wet skin and cold packs on head, neck, armpits and groin [3][21]. Body temperature can reach 106 F within 10 to 15 minutes [11][21].

## Medicines and power-dependent devices

This chapter gives no dosing advice. Dosing questions go to a pharmacist, your clinician, or 911.

**Make a plan with your provider before an outage.** Ask how long each medicine can stay warm, and what to do about powered devices [1][9].

**Refrigerated medicine.** Do not throw out a life-sustaining medicine (insulin, transplant drugs, etc.) until you have called a pharmacist or clinician. For other refrigerated medicine, Ready.gov says to discard it if the power is out for more than a day, unless the label says otherwise [1]. Insulin is the common exception (next paragraph). If a life depends on a refrigerated drug that has already warmed, do not decide alone whether to use it: contact a pharmacist, healthcare provider or the drug's manufacturer now [45], and if you cannot reach one, call 911 or go to an emergency department (this guide's rule).

**Insulin.** FDA says unopened insulin is stored at about 36 to 46 F. Vials and cartridges, opened or unopened, can stay unrefrigerated at 59 to 86 F for up to 28 days and keep working. Check your pen's label. Heat reduces effectiveness, so keep insulin out of direct heat and sun. When cooling it with ice, never let it freeze, and never use frozen insulin [26]. If the house passes 86 F, call a pharmacist or clinician.

**Power-dependent devices.** Oxygen concentrators, CPAP machines, powered beds and similar equipment need a backup battery and a place to go. Ask your equipment supplier and clinician what backup power the device has and how long it lasts [9].

- Ask your power provider about a priority-restoration list (a utility's list of customers to restore first), and tell local emergency management, which may keep a voluntary registry for people with disabilities [42]. A list is not a guarantee of power (general practice, not from a cited source).
- HHS's emPOWER program (a federal tool that helps officials plan for people on power-dependent medical equipment) is not something a household signs up for [37].
- A generator is never the answer indoors or in a garage [28]. A battery or solar power station is [30].
- If you cannot power the device, leave to where there is power [9].

## Worked scenario A: three days in January

*An illustration.* A family of four in a house. The power goes out at 6 p.m. in a hard freeze. No medical devices.

1. **Hour 0.** Close the fridge. Flashlights out. Check the CO alarm batteries. Fill drinking-water jugs, then the tub for flushing [1][16][23]. Everyone dresses in layers [11].
2. **Hour 3.** Before the 4-hour mark, perishables go into a cooler with frozen jugs. They do not go in the snow [9][27].
3. **Night 1.** Everyone sleeps in one room with sleeping bags. The gas oven stays off, even to warm the kitchen [1][11]. The generator stays outside, 20 ft away [1][12]. A flashlight sits by each bed.
4. **Day 2.** An older relative is drowsy and shivering. Those are hypothermia signs, and sleepiness alone is a reason to call 911 [2][36]. They call 911 right away. While they wait, they get the relative warm and dry [2]. Once the relative is in medical care, the family moves to a community warming location [1].
5. **Day 2, pipes.** Taps drip and cabinet doors stay open [20]. A boil-water notice arrives, so they use stored water [17].
6. **Day 3.** Power returns. The fridge thermometer has read 40 F or higher for 2 hours or more, so perishables go out [1]. Freezer food with ice crystals is cooked or refrozen [13][25].

## Worked scenario B: a heat wave, an insulin user and an oxygen concentrator

*An illustration.* Two adults. One uses insulin, the other a plug-in oxygen concentrator. The power fails at 2 p.m. in a heat wave, with days above 90 F ahead.

1. **Minute 0.** Call the oxygen supplier. Ask how long the backup lasts [9].
2. **Hour 1: the decision.** The oxygen supply cannot be guaranteed, so they decide to leave for a place with power [9]. They pack insulin, medicines, the concentrator's cord, phone charger, ID and a note [6]. A generator on the porch is not an option [28].
3. **Until they go.** The house is heating up. Past 90 F a fan will not help [22]. They take cool showers, close the shades, and dial 2-1-1 for cooling centers [3][22].
4. **Insulin.** The house passes 86 F, the top of the range where insulin keeps working for up to 28 days [26]. They keep it as cool as they can, out of direct sun, and never let it touch ice enough to freeze [26]. They do not throw it out and do not decide alone: FDA notes that warmed insulin may still be needed in an emergency [26], so they call a pharmacist or the prescriber now [26][45], and would call 911 or go to an emergency department if they could not reach one (this guide's rule). Dosing questions go to the pharmacist.
5. **Hour 2.** One of them feels dizzy and nauseated and is sweating heavily. Those are heat exhaustion signs. They move to a cool place, sip water and use cold compresses [21]. If they became confused, with hot dry skin, they would call 911 [3].

## Common mistakes

- Running a generator or a car in the garage, even with the door open [12][28].
- Using the stove or oven for heat [1][2][11].
- Opening the fridge to check [13].
- Drinking tub or pool water [15][34].
- Using a candle when a flashlight will do [32].
- Relying on a fan above 90 F [22].
- Ignoring a CO alarm, or waiting for symptoms [11][28].
- Waiting past an evacuation order [6].

## Quick-reference card (print on one page)

**CALL 911 OR GET OUT**

- CO alarm sounds or CO symptoms (headache, dizziness, weakness, upset stomach, vomiting, chest pain, confusion): everyone outside, then call 911 [28][12].
- Heat stroke (confusion, slurred speech, hot dry skin or heavy sweating, seizures, fast strong pulse, or body temperature above 103 F): call 911 [3][21].
- Hypothermia (sleepiness, confusion, slurred speech, fumbling hands, or body temperature below 95 F): call 911 now [2][36].
- Gas smell: leave now, no switches or phone inside, call from outside [28].

**LEAVE WHEN**

- Evacuation order: go [6].
- Heat illness signs or home too hot to cool: go to air conditioning [3][21].
- You cannot stay warm: go to a community warming location [1].
- A powered device cannot run: go where there is power. Breathing trouble: call 911 [9].

**FOOD**

- Closed fridge about 4 hours. Full freezer about 48 hours. Half-full freezer about 24 hours [13].
- Fridge 40 F or below. Freezer 0 F or below [13].
- No thermometer and power out over 4 hours: throw out perishables [13][25][27].
- Thermometer shows 40 F or higher for 2 hours or more: throw out [1].
- Ice crystals, or 40 F or below: cook or refreeze [13][25][27].
- Never taste-test. When in doubt, throw it out [13][27].
- No food in the snow [9][27].

**CO AND FIRE**

- Generator outdoors, 20+ ft from windows, doors, vents. Never in a garage or other enclosed space [28].
- No car running in an attached garage [12].
- No stove or oven for heat. A gas range is for cooking only, never for heat, with the hood on or a window open while you cook [1][2][11][12][30].
- No unvented fuel-burning heater indoors [29][30].
- No grill or camp stove indoors [11].
- CO alarms on every level and outside bedrooms, battery backup, replace per maker or every 5 years [12][28].
- Flashlights and battery lanterns. No candles [32].

**WATER**

- 1 gallon per person per day, 3 days minimum, 2 weeks better [16].
- Tub and pool water: flush and wash only [15][33]. Never drink radiator or boiler water [14].
- Boil-water notice: follow your utility. Otherwise rolling boil 1 minute; 3 minutes above 5,000 ft or if unsure of elevation [14][43].
- No fridge-dispenser ice during a notice [17].

**COLD AND HEAT**

- Older adults: keep home at 68 to 70 F. 60 to 65 F can be dangerous [36].
- Fan only if the room is below 90 F [22].
- Cooling center: dial 2-1-1 [22].

**MEDICINE**

- Do not throw out insulin or other life-sustaining medicine until you call a pharmacist or clinician (this guide's rule; contacting a pharmacist, provider or manufacturer is FDA's advice [45]).
- Insulin vial or cartridge: 59 to 86 F for up to 28 days. Never freeze [26].
- Plan with your provider before an outage [1].

**TOILET AND HANDS**

- Sewer working: pour-flush. Sewer down: two bags plus absorbent, store away from kids and pets [38].
- Wash hands 20 seconds with soap. Sanitizer (60% alcohol or more) only if no soap [18].

**PHONE**

- Text, do not call. Charge ahead. Low-power mode [8].
