# Family Communication Plan

> Communication planning is general information. Bracketed numbers point to `## Sources`; "(general practice)" and "(this entry's rule)" mark points no opened source states. The short version of the plan is in [08_communication.md](../../../08_communication.md#family-communication-plan) and in [mesh-and-emergency-comms](mesh-and-emergency-comms.md). This entry goes deeper: the card, schools and work, people with extra needs, pets, and practice. For radios, see [radio-basics-and-bands](radio-basics-and-bands.md).

## Goal & When To Use

A family communication plan answers one question before a disaster: if we are not together and the phones are jammed, how do we find out who is OK, and where do we meet? Ready.gov starts from the fact that "your family may not be together if a disaster strikes" [1]. The plan has three parts: who to contact, where to meet, and practice [3].

Do it on a calm day, with everyone who lives in the home. Redo it when the household changes. If you are in danger right now, skip this entry and call 911 ([08_communication.md](../../../08_communication.md#call-911-well)).

## Steps

**1. Meet as a household and list what each person needs.**
Include children in making the plan [5]. Ready.gov asks you to think about disabilities or access and functional needs (including devices and equipment), pets or service animals, and households with school-aged children [1]. Write each person's needs down. The rest of the steps use that list.

**2. Pick one out-of-town contact.**
Pick one person and have every family member contact that same person. It may be easier to reach someone who is out of town [2][3]. Pick a contact who lives out of state if you can, and at the least outside your own county or metro area (this entry's rule). Tell them they were chosen (general practice). Make sure every family member, and the contact, can send a text. Texts and other data services are less likely to hit network congestion than voice calls [4].

**3. Choose meeting places.**
FEMA's card asks for two: one in the neighborhood and one regional, away from home [2]. Ready.gov says to pick a meeting place that is "familiar and easy to find" [1], and its kids page says to decide on "safe, familiar, accessible places" where the family can go to reunite [3]. Its kids page lists places in the house, in the neighborhood, and outside your city or town [3]. A spot just outside the home covers a sudden emergency such as a fire (general practice). If you have pets or service animals, choose places that allow them [3]. If officials tell you to shelter or evacuate somewhere else, follow them first (general practice).

**4. Write the card.**
FEMA's Family Communication Plan card has these fields [2]:
- the out-of-town contact: name, home and cell numbers, email
- each workplace: address, phone, and its evacuation location
- each school: address, phone, and its evacuation location
- the neighborhood and regional meeting places
- each family member: name, date of birth, important medical information
- medical contacts, including the pharmacist and the veterinarian or kennel
- insurance companies and phone numbers

The card also has fields for Social Security and policy numbers. Nothing makes you fill them in, and a lost card is a risk. This entry's rule: leave off numbers that would hurt you if a stranger read the card.

Copies: one in your purse or briefcase, your car, your office and your disaster kit [2]. Each family member keeps a copy in a safe place such as a backpack, wallet or notebook [3]. Save the same numbers in your phone and keep contacts updated and synced [3][9]. Paper matters because a dead phone takes the numbers with it. Review the card every year and keep it up to date [2].

**5. Plan for school and work.**
- Ask the school or childcare center how it will reunite children with families. CDC says these places have such plans, and that parents can ask the principal and safety officials about the safety drills [5].
- Make an emergency contact card for each child to carry in a school bag or backpack. Help older children memorize important names and phone numbers [5].
- Put each school's and workplace's evacuation location on your card [2].
- Who a school will release a child to varies by school. Ask, and keep your emergency contacts there current (general practice).
- CDC also points to the Red Cross Reconnecting Families program for reunification help [5].

**6. Plan for people with disabilities and access needs.**
- Build a support network of family, friends and others who can help. Practice the plan with them [7], and tell them where your supplies are kept [6][7]. Ready.gov also says at least one of them should know how to use lifesaving equipment or give medicine, so they can act if you cannot [7].
- If you have a communication disability, carry printed cards or store the information on your devices so responders know how to communicate with you. Plan a backup in case your communication equipment fails. Laminated cards with phrases or pictograms are one option [6].
- Keep pen and paper handy. A battery lantern helps with sign language or lip reading when the power is out [6].
- Decide how you will take assistive devices with you if you evacuate, and how you will get transportation if you need help [7].
- A weather radio with a text display and flashing alert helps people who are deaf or hard of hearing [6]. See [emergency-alerts-and-noaa-weather-radio](emergency-alerts-and-noaa-weather-radio.md).

**7. Plan for pets.**
If officials ask you to evacuate, your pet should evacuate too. Many public shelters and hotels do not allow pets, so know a safe place for them before the emergency and line up a backup caregiver with a neighbor, friend or relative [8]. Microchip your pet and keep your contact details current. Keep a collar with an ID tag, a harness or leash, and a picture of you with your pet to help prove ownership [8]. Write the pet-friendly place on your card (this entry's rule).

**8. Set the phone rules.**
- Unless you are in danger, send a text. Texts may get through when calls do not, and you do not tie up lines emergency workers need [2][3]. If you are in danger, call 911 first.
- Keep calls short. If a cell call fails, wait ten seconds before redialing [4].
- Send one short message to the out-of-town contact, such as "I'm OK. At the library." (general practice). The contact tells the rest.
- Check whether your home phone works in a power outage and how long its battery backup lasts [10]. Have a way to charge your phone [9].

**9. Practice and review.**
Ready.gov says to practice your plan with your household [1] and to hold regular household meetings to review and practice it [3]. FEMA says to look over the card every year [2]. This entry's rule: practice at least once a year, and again after any change: a new phone number, move, job, school, baby or pet.

A practice session can be short:
1. Everyone reads the contact's number off the card, not from the phone.
2. Each person sends the test text to the out-of-town contact, and the contact replies.
3. Walk or drive to the neighborhood meeting place.
4. Ask the children where they would go and whom they would call.

## Failure Modes & Fixes

- **The plan lives only in the phone.** Phones die. Keep paper copies in the places listed in step 4 [2].
- **Family members call different relatives.** Nobody can tell who is safe. One shared contact fixes this [2].
- **The out-of-town contact does not know they were chosen, or cannot text.** Ask them, and test it (general practice).
- **A child at school has no card.** Make one for the school bag [5].
- **The meeting place does not allow the dog.** Choose places that take pets, and name a backup [3][8].
- **The meeting place is cut off.** Name a second one in a different direction (general practice).
- **The card is out of date.** Review it every year and after each change [2][3].
- **The plan was never practiced.** People forget. Practice [1][3].
- **A call does not go through.** Wait ten seconds before redialing [4]. If a text does not go through, try again in a moment (general practice). Do not hold up a call to 911 for a text.
