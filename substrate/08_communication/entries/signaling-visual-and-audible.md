## Goal & When To Use

When you are lost or hurt outdoors and rescue is possible, being easy to find is the job. The National Park Service (NPS) says help can take hours or days depending on location, weather and resources [1][2]. Signal and conserve energy while you wait.

Order of action (NPS) [1][2]:
1. Stop and work out where you are. Retrace your steps only if it is safe. If you cannot, and you have phone coverage, call 911 and give your location.
2. **Stay put.** Changing location makes it harder for rescuers to find you. Move only if something threatens your life [1][2].
3. Find shelter, keep warm, and inventory your gear.
4. Signal with the tools below. Do not build a fire when fire risk is high or in wildland fire season, because it could cause a wildfire [2].

Visual signals (mirror, smoke, ground symbols) work in daylight. The Army says fire is the most effective visual signal in darkness [4]. A whistle carries farther than a shout and saves your voice (general practice); NPS says to blow it often [1].

## Steps

### Audible

**1. Whistle** [1][2][4]
- **Blow your whistle often.** It saves your energy compared with yelling [1][2].
- Blow it **three times, take a break, then blow three times again** (NPS graphic) [2].
- The Army says whistles are an excellent close-up signal and in documented cases have been heard up to 1.6 kilometers (about 1 mile). A manufactured whistle carries farther than a human whistle [4].
- If you have no whistle, use your voice sparingly, because yelling costs energy [1]. NPS tells you to "make yourself big" when searchers are near: blow the whistle, wave your arms, flash the flashlight [1].

**2. Groups of three** [2][3][4][5]
- The NPS Yosemite page says any signal done three times in a series is a universal distress call [5].
- Whistle: three, break, three (NPS) [2].
- Flashlight: SOS pattern, three quick flashes, three slow, three quick. Use the flashlight wisely because it drains the batteries [1][2].
- Fire: three fires in a triangle are recognized as distress [3]. The Army also gives three fires in a straight line about 25 meters (about 80 feet) apart. If you are alone and three is too hard to keep going, keep one [4].
- Smoke: the Army calls three columns of smoke the international distress signal [4].
- Gunshots: the Army says three shots at distinct intervals usually mean distress [4]. (This guide does not recommend firing a gun to signal; a whistle is safer.)

### Visual

**3. Signal mirror** [1][3][4]
- The Army says on a sunny day a mirror is your best signaling device. If you have no mirror, polish a shiny object such as a canteen cup [4]. Practice before you need it [4].
- The FAA says flashes from signal mirrors have been spotted from over 25 miles, and a mirror is still effective on cloudy and overcast days. Even if you do not hear an aircraft, sweep the flash toward the horizon [3].
- Use the aiming method printed on a commercial mirror. If you cannot tell where the aircraft is, flash toward the sound [4].
- **Cautions (Army):** do not flash rapidly, because a pilot may mistake it for hostile fire. Do not hold the beam on the cockpit for more than a few seconds, because it may blind the pilot. Haze, ground fog and mirages can hide a flash; if you can, get to the highest point nearby [4].
- A mirror does not run out of battery [1]. It is useless in the dark.

**4. Fire and smoke** [2][3][4]
- **Day: use smoke that contrasts with the background.** The Army says dark smoke against a light background and light smoke against a dark one [4]. The FAA says in light or snow-covered terrain use black smoke, and in dark terrain or dense foliage use white smoke [3].
- **White smoke:** green wood, green or dry leaves, moss, peat or ferns burned on a fire [3]. The Army says smother a large fire with green leaves, moss, or a little water and it makes white smoke [4]. You do not need white-hot coals; you need a large fire to smother.
- **Black smoke** comes from petroleum products, rubber or plastic, or evergreens, and the FAA warns that smoke from rubber or petroleum may contain toxic compounds and to keep out of it [3]. This guide does not tell you to burn tires or plastic (this guide's rule).
- **Night:** three fires in a triangle, or in a line about 25 meters apart. Build them as soon as time and the situation allow and protect them until you need them [4]. Any fire in the open is effective [3].
- The hotter the fire, the higher the smoke rises, and height makes it visible [3]. The Army says smoke signals work only on comparatively calm, clear days; high wind, rain or snow disperse smoke [4].
- Keep your signal fire in the open, away from tents and dry brush. Never burn a fire inside a tent, vehicle or shelter. Fire and carbon monoxide rules are in `03_fire.md`.
- **When to light it** (this guide's judgment): have the fire built and ready, and light it when you hear or see a search aircraft or a rescue party. Do not leave it burning unattended.

**5. Ground-to-air symbols** [2][3][4]
- Make shapes of rocks, branches, mounds, cleared earth or shelter material, big and straight-edged, in the open, with contrast against the ground [2][3][4]. The Army suggests building mounds that cast shadows; in snow, tramp letters and fill them with twigs or branches [4].
- **Size.** The FAA says use a 6 to 1 ratio, preferably 18 feet by 3 feet [3]. The Army says make symbols at least 1 meter wide and 6 meters long, and keep the same 1 to 6 ratio if you go larger [4].
- **Symbols.** The FAA list includes: I for doctor needed, X for cannot continue, F for water or food needed, K for which direction, LL for everything is OK, N for no and Y for yes [3]. Other lists differ (some use X for medical help), and a "V" does not appear in the FAA list [3]. When in doubt, spell out **SOS** or **HELP** in large letters (this guide's judgment).

**6. Bright cloth and clothing** [1][4]
- Spread contrasting clothing or cloth on the ground or in the top of a tree. Choose colors that contrast with the surroundings and arrange them in a large geometric pattern [4]. Bright orange or violet cloth works as a panel; flash it to catch the eye [4].
- Wear bright clothing from the start of the trip so searchers can see you [1].

## Failure Modes & Fixes

**Mirror gives no flash.** Heavy cloud, rain or darkness. A mirror still works through thin or overcast cloud [3]. Pivot to whistle, flashlight and ground symbols.

**Smoke is the wrong color or too thin.** Use contrast: white smoke in dark terrain, black against light or snow [3]. For white smoke, smother a big fire with green leaves, moss or ferns [3][4]. A small fire makes a thin column. Wet fuel burned alone only smolders (general practice).

**No whistle.** Use your voice sparingly, a flashlight at night, waved arms, and spread cloth [1][4]. Lean on the visual signals.

**Rescuers are near but did not see you.** The signal may be too small, wrong contrast or in the wrong spot. Check each signal's condition, contrast, location, straight lines and size [3]. Move to higher, open ground only if you can do it without leaving the area where you told 911 you were (this guide's judgment).

**Weak or intermittent signals read as background activity.** Repeat the pattern: three, break, three [2]. Do not signal continuously to the point of exhaustion; the NPS says to conserve energy [1][2].

**Survival fire mistaken for a signal, or a signal fire for a cook fire.** Keep a separate signal fire built and ready (this guide's judgment).

**No phone, no whistle, nobody near.** Other people: if there is no reception, flag down a hiker or visitor and ask them to go where there is reception and call 911 [2].
