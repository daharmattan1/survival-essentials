## Goal & When To Use

Being found is the strongest survival strategy when rescue is reasonably possible. Signaling—both visual and audible—trades minimal energy expenditure for maximum visibility. Use these methods when:

- **Rescue is likely** (you filed a flight plan, have an agreed check-in time, or are within a monitored area).
- **You are immobilized** (injury, illness, or sheltering in place due to weather).
- **Early contact matters** (first 24–48 hours set the pace; a late rescue is a cold rescue).

Visual signals (mirror, fire, smoke, ground-to-air symbols) work best **by day**. Audible signals (whistle, three-of-anything) work **in any weather** and pierce background noise better than voice. The discipline is **pre-arrangement and redundancy**: a charged radio, a signal kit staged before the emergency, and an agreed contact plan (out-of-area person, expected check-in, escape window) beat improvising in the moment.

## Steps

### Visual Signaling

**1. Signal Mirror (day, clear visibility to 10+ miles)**
- Position: back to sun, face toward rescue direction.
- Hold mirror flat (or tilted slightly) at arm's length; aim sun reflection onto target (aircraft, distant person, ridge).
- The flash is visible at great distance and pierces mist; even a credit-card-sized mirror works.
- Practice aiming before an emergency (glint off water, a rock, a signal).
- Limit: works only when sun is high and target is in sight; useless at night or in heavy cloud.

**2. Fire & Smoke (day & night)**
- **Day:** smoke is the signal. Lay a bright, smoky fire where it can be seen (hilltop, open ground, not in a canyon). Add green branches, damp wood, or vegetation to generate thick, dark smoke that contrasts against ground and sky.
- **Night:** the flame itself signals; fire should be visible from a distance. Three fires in a triangle or line is the classic distress signal.
- Timing: light fires only when you hear/see potential rescue (aircraft sound, distant vehicle).
- Fuel: pre-gather dry kindling and larger wood the day before if possible; wet wood smolders and wastes effort if you're in poor condition.

**3. Ground-to-Air Symbols (day, aircraft overhead)**
- Arrange rocks, logs, or cleared earth into large, high-contrast symbols in an open area.
- Distress symbols: **V** (need assistance), **X** (cannot proceed), **SOS** (three characters), or an arrow pointing to your location.
- Size: minimum 10 feet per character; larger is better (50+ feet if resources allow).
- Contrast: use bright objects (white cloth, reflective material, cleared dirt) against dark ground, or dark objects (logs, rocks) against light ground.

**4. High-Visibility Clothing & Fabric**
- Spread bright cloth (orange, red, yellow) where it can be spotted (atop shelter, on open rock, rigged from a tree).
- A parachute or tarp signals urgently; wave it only if rescue is confirmed (false signals waste rescuer energy).
- Personal high-vis vest or arm band keeps you visible in a search grid.

### Audible Signaling

**1. Whistle (any weather, day & night)**
- **Three short blasts** = international distress signal; easily heard over 1/2 mile in quiet conditions.
- A whistle carries better and farther than voice; it is unmistakably human and signals without tiring your voice.
- Rhythm: blow three sharp blasts, pause 2–3 seconds, repeat. Pause 5–10 seconds, then repeat the whole sequence.
- Timing: whistle sparingly during the day (save energy, attract attention only when rescue is near); whistle at night if you hear others.
- Range: ~1/2 mile in forest, farther in open ground and wind-aided conditions.

**2. Three-of-Anything Rule (distress convention)**
- **Three signals** = universal distress marker (three whistle blasts, three fires, three ground symbols, three shouts spaced minutes apart).
- Twice that spacing confirms distress over accidental activity.
- **Avoid overuse**: signaling continuously draws no distinction between distress and background noise; three + pause + three is the discipline.

**3. Voice (least reliable; use when whistle fails)**
- Shout only if rescue is confirmed; voice exhausts you and carries poorly through wind and forest.
- Three shouts, pause, three shouts (follow the rule of three).
- Yell distinctive calls ("Help!" not background noise) and pause to listen for response.

## Failure Modes & Fixes

**Mirror fails to reflect sun.** Heavy cloud, rain, or night makes the mirror useless. **Pivot to fire and whistle.** During day, an intermittent cloud break gives brief reflection chances; aim repeatedly.

**Fire won't produce smoke.** Wet wood, insufficient heat, or poor tinder. **Dry your materials**: break wood into thin pieces, expose to air and any residual sun, and lay small pieces first to build heat. Add green material only after the core fire is established and hot (white-hot coals needed for smoke production).

**Whistle lost or broken.** A stick notched and blown produces a whistle-like tone (low reliability). **Use three-of-anything audible**: shout, bang rocks, clap. Stay with visual signals (mirror, fire).

**Rescue nearby but hasn't seen you.** Your signals were too small, wrong contrast, or wrong location. **Move to higher, more open ground if safe to do so.** Wave cloth actively if you see aircraft. Reposition ground symbols perpendicular to the expected search direction.

**False rescue signals attract no one.** Intermittent or weak signals read as background activity. **Maintain the three-fold rule**: three deliberate signals + clear pause + repeat, every 5–10 minutes if conditions are promising.

**Confusion with survival activity.** Smoke from cooking fires may not be seen as distress. **Keep survival fires separate from signal fires.** Only light the signal fire when you have reason to expect rescue.
