# Vehicle Emergency Kit

> Gear advice below is general practice unless a bracketed number points to a source in `## Sources`. First-aid items are general information, not medical advice; for steps use [06_first_aid.md](../../../06_first_aid.md). The chapter this entry supports is [11_essential_gear.md](../../../11_essential_gear.md#car-kit). The cold-weather stuck-in-a-car rules are in [04_shelter.md](../../../04_shelter.md#vehicle-as-shelter).

> ⚠ **CARBON MONOXIDE IN A VEHICLE: it has no smell and it kills people who are asleep**
>
> - Nothing burns inside a vehicle. No camp stove, lantern, charcoal or fuel-burning heater, ever [8][9][10]. No candles either (this guide's rule, from [04_shelter.md](../../../04_shelter.md#vehicle-as-shelter)). CDC says opening tent flaps, doors or windows is not enough to prevent CO build-up in a tent or camper [10]. This guide applies the same rule to a vehicle: a window or door does not fix it.
> - Stuck in snow, clear the tailpipe before you start the engine, and again whenever it runs. A blocked tailpipe lets carbon monoxide (CO) build up quickly inside the vehicle and poison everyone in it [7].
> - Run the engine only in short runs, about 10 minutes each hour, with one window cracked [4][6]. A clear pipe and a cracked window lower the risk. They do not remove it (this guide's rule, from [04_shelter.md](../../../04_shelter.md#vehicle-as-shelter)).
> - Never sleep with the engine running. People who are asleep can die before they notice symptoms [8].
> - Headache, dizziness, weakness, upset stomach, vomiting, chest pain or confusion are CO signs [8]. Shut the engine off, get everyone into fresh air, then call 911 (this guide's rule, from [03_fire.md](../../../03_fire.md#fire-indoors-in-tents-and-in-vehicles-the-carbon-monoxide-rule)).
> - Never run a vehicle in a garage attached to a house, even with the door open [8].

## What & Why

A vehicle kit covers a breakdown, a crash scene, or being stuck in snow. It lives in the car so it is there when you are stranded far from your other kits. Ready.gov says to keep an emergency supply kit in your car "in case you are stranded" [1]. The Red Cross says to keep a first aid kit in your car as well as your home [18].

In winter the best plan is to stay in the vehicle if you are trapped [2][4]. A car is shelter, so the kit is mostly about warmth, being seen, calling for help, and getting moving again. It does not replace judgment: a kit with nothing in the tank behind it does little, so keep the gas tank full. Ready.gov says to keep the tank full, NHTSA says close to full, and Ready.gov adds that a full tank keeps the fuel line from freezing [1][11].

This entry builds on [bug-out-tier2-kit](bug-out-tier2-kit.md) and the on-body items in [edc-tier1-kit](edc-tier1-kit.md). First aid contents are in [individual-first-aid-kit](individual-first-aid-kit.md).

## Selection Criteria

Items are grouped by the job they do. No brands and no prices.

**Be seen and warn others**
- Flares or a reflective triangle [1][3].
- A brightly colored cloth, preferably red, to tie to the antenna or door [4].
- A flashlight with spare batteries [2][3][12].

**Stay warm**
- A blanket for each person [1][2][3], and extra warm clothes [2][3].
- Hats, gloves or mittens, and warm socks [12]. Hand warmers and an emergency sleeping bag or blanket (the Red Cross list names Mylar ones) [3].

**Get moving again**
- Jumper cables [1][3][12].
- An ice scraper [1] and a snow shovel and broom [11].
- Sand or cat litter for traction [1][3][12].
- A tire repair kit with an inflator [3].
- Tire chains or studs where your area requires them [1].

**Call for help and stay informed**
- A car phone charger [1][12] and a portable power bank [3]. Keep them charged (this guide's rule, from [11_essential_gear.md](../../../11_essential_gear.md#car-kit)).
- An emergency radio [3]. A weather band is worth having; see [emergency-alerts-and-noaa-weather-radio](../../08_communication/entries/emergency-alerts-and-noaa-weather-radio.md).
- A paper map [1].

**Water and food**
- Water and non-perishable food [2][3][12]. Red Cross says to store at least one gallon of water per person for 24 hours [3]. An extension article uses one gallon per person for every three days of planned travel [13]. This entry follows the Red Cross figure, the larger one for a short trip. Plan more for a long trip or hot weather, since water needs can double in very hot temperatures [15].
- High-calorie, non-perishable food such as trail mix or energy bars [5][12]. Choose food you can eat without cooking (general practice). If you pack canned food, pack a can opener [13].
- Do not eat snow. It can lower your body temperature [13].

**First aid and medicines**
- A first aid kit [3][12]. See [individual-first-aid-kit](individual-first-aid-kit.md).
- Your own medicines are not a car item by default. NHTSA's winter list includes "any necessary medicine" [11], so carry what you need with you. FDA says to store medicine in a cool, dry place and to check the label, since some medicines must avoid heat [17]. A parked car swings hot and cold, so do not leave your medicines in it (general practice).

**Tools and other**
- Duct tape, a multi-tool, cash, and a car-safe fire extinguisher are on the Red Cross list of additional items [3].
- Do not add candles or matches for warmth. The NWS Amarillo winter kit list includes them [5]. This guide's rule is that nothing burns inside a vehicle, so this entry leaves them out.

**Before the season**
- Have a mechanic check antifreeze, battery and ignition system, brakes, exhaust system, fuel and air filters, heater and defroster, lights, oil, thermostat, and wipers and washer fluid [1]. CDC says to have the exhaust system checked every year, since a small leak can let CO build up inside [8].
- Put on good winter tires with enough tread [1].

**Where to keep it.** Consider the back seat. A trunk can jam or freeze shut [12]. If you get stuck in snow, move what you need from the trunk to the passenger area [6].

### Seasonal swaps

- **Winter:** blankets, sleeping bag, hats, gloves, hand warmers, shovel, sand or litter, ice scraper, chains where required, red cloth [1][3][4][12].
- **Hot weather:** more water [15]. Never leave a child, a disabled person or a pet locked in a car, in any season [16]. Children can die in a hot car in 10 minutes [16].
- **Wet weather:** the kit does not help in a flood. Do not drive through flooded areas: six inches of water can make a vehicle lose control or stall, and a foot can float many cars [1].
- **Year-round:** the "get moving," "call for help" and first aid items stay in the car.

### Water in a car

- Water kept in a car meets neither CDC's range of 50 to 70 F nor its "away from direct sunlight" advice [14]. A car is hot in summer and below freezing in winter. This entry's rule: use sealed, commercially bottled water, and check it each time you swap the kit.
- CDC says not to use glass for stored water. Use durable, unbreakable, food-grade containers [14].
- In cold weather, keep the water in the passenger area so it does not freeze [13]. A frozen bottle can split (general practice), so look for cracks and leaks before you drink.
- Rotation follows the home rule. Replace water that was not commercially bottled every six months [14][15]. For store-bought water, follow the date on the bottle [14]. This entry's rule: also replace it at each seasonal swap, and any time a bottle looks warped, cloudy, leaking or was left in the sun or frozen solid.

## Multi-Use

- **Blankets and clothes:** warmth, padding, ground insulation, and a signal when spread in the open (general practice).
- **Red cloth:** a distress signal on the antenna [4]. Spread on the ground it is a ground signal; see [signaling-visual-and-audible](../../08_communication/entries/signaling-visual-and-audible.md).
- **Sand or cat litter:** traction under a stuck wheel [1].
- **Shovel:** digging out wheels and clearing the tailpipe [11]. A blocked tailpipe is the CO risk [7].
- **Power bank:** charges a phone, so it is your light, map and radio too (general practice).
- **Duct tape:** hose and bumper fixes, and holding a splint or dressing in place (general practice).

## Maintenance / Failure Modes

- **The kit is buried under luggage or frozen in the trunk.** Keep it in the passenger area [12].
- **A dead phone charger or power bank.** Check and charge it (this guide's rule, from [11_essential_gear.md](../../../11_essential_gear.md#car-kit)). Check and restock the winter supplies before you leave [6].
- **Dead flashlight batteries.** Keep spares [3] and test the light.
- **Water that is frozen, overheated or never replaced.** See the water section above.
- **Used items never replaced.** Restock right away after any use [18].
- **Engine run all night, or run with the tailpipe buried.** This is how people die in stuck cars. See the CO block above [7][8].
- **Leaving the vehicle.** In wind-driven snow and cold you will become disoriented quickly. Stay in the vehicle [4].
- **Relying on the car for heat.** The car could break down [6]. Layers and blankets are the heat.
- **A fuel-burning heater "just for a few minutes."** Never. CPSC warns against using fuel-burning portable heaters or lanterns while sleeping in enclosed areas such as tents, campers and other vehicles [9]. This guide's rule is stricter: nothing burns in a vehicle, awake or asleep.
- **Medicines in a hot or cold car.** See above [17].
