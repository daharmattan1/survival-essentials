# Home Stockpile Tier 3 — Shelter-in-Place, 2-Week Supply

> Gear-selection advice below is general practice unless a bracketed number points to a source in `## Sources`. First-aid items are general information, not medical advice; use [06_first_aid.md](../../../06_first_aid.md) for steps. This tier contains items that can hurt you if misused (generators, fuel, bleach, anything that burns). The rules for them are in [03_fire.md](../../../03_fire.md#fire-indoors-in-tents-and-in-vehicles-the-carbon-monoxide-rule) and [09_urban_survival.md](../../../09_urban_survival.md#carbon-monoxide-and-fire-heat-light-and-cook-without-hurting-anyone). The chapter this entry supports is [11_essential_gear.md](../../../11_essential_gear.md#home-supply-the-base-kit).

## What & Why

Tier 3 is the supply you keep at home to stay put through an outage, storm or a disrupted supply line. Weight does not matter; volume, a place to keep it, and a habit of checking it do. Ready.gov's basic kit says "several days" of supplies [1]. CDC's water guidance reaches 2 weeks: "store at least 1 gallon of water per person, per day for 3 days" and "try to store a 2-week supply if possible" [3]. Because water guidance reaches 2 weeks, this guide suggests aiming for 2 weeks of food at home too (this guide's judgment).

If one system fails (water, power, a route to the store), a second source keeps you going. The discipline is the same as at the other tiers: practice with what you own, check it on a schedule, and replace what ages out.

## Selection Criteria

**Water storage**
- At least 1 gallon per person per day, for drinking and sanitation [1][2]. Plan for at least 3 days, and 2 weeks if you can [3]. Store more for pregnancy, illness, pets and hot weather [2][3]. Pets need water on top of the household amount [3].
- Containers: food-grade, a tight-closing top, unbreakable (not glass), a narrow opening if possible. Never reuse containers that held bleach, pesticides or other toxic chemicals [3].
- Store between 50 and 70 F, out of direct sunlight, away from gasoline and pesticides [3].
- Replace water you stored yourself every 6 months. Follow the date on commercially bottled water [2][3].
- Move test: a full container is heavy, about 8 pounds per gallon (general practice). Use containers you can lift, and do not stack them high.
- **Bleach and stored water.** Do not add bleach to stored tap water that came from a treated public supply; it needs nothing added. That rule, how to sanitize containers, and the one dose for treating doubtful water are in [02_water.md](../../../02_water.md#storing-water) and [02_water.md](../../../02_water.md#bleach). This entry does not restate a dose.

**Food (non-perishable base)**
- At least a several-day supply of non-perishable food, and a manual can opener [1]. Aim longer, up to 2 weeks (this guide's judgment). This entry sets no calorie target; no source in this guide gives one for storage.
- Keep canned food in a cool, dry place. Keep boxed food in tightly closed plastic or metal containers [1].
- Buy what your household will eat without cooking. Include foods for infants, pets and anyone on a special diet (see [11_essential_gear.md](../../../11_essential_gear.md#special-needs)).
- **Cans.** Do not eat from cans that are swollen, dented or corroded, even if the food looks fine [9]. CDC says to throw out canned food that is bulging, open or damaged [10]. When in doubt, throw it out.
- What to keep and throw away after the power goes out is in [09_urban_survival.md](../../../09_urban_survival.md#food-what-to-keep-and-what-to-throw-out).

**Power**
- Ready.gov: extra batteries, a phone with chargers and a backup battery, and a backup power source on hand [1][14]. Keep a portable phone charger in your car [14].
- Spare batteries of the type your flashlights and radio use. This entry gives no quantity because no source does.
- **Generator (optional).** Use it outdoors only, at least 20 feet from windows, doors and vents [5]. Ready.gov says the same distance and includes attached garages [4]. CPSC: outside only, at least 20 feet from the house, exhaust facing away; never inside a home, garage, basement, crawlspace, shed or other enclosed space [7]. Opening doors and windows does not fix it [5][6]. Look for a model with a CO shut-off [7]. Keep fuel outside the living space (general practice).
- **Battery CO alarms.** Install battery-operated or battery-backup CO alarms on every level and near every sleeping area [5][6]. Test them monthly [7]. Replace them following the maker's instructions or every 5 years [5]. Check the batteries when you change your clocks [5].
- A power station or power bank that runs on a battery makes no fumes (see [09_urban_survival.md](../../../09_urban_survival.md#carbon-monoxide-and-fire-heat-light-and-cook-without-hurting-anyone)).

**Warmth (no fuel-burning heater indoors)**
- Warmth comes from blankets, sleeping bags, layers of clothing and hats, a change of clothes and sturdy shoes [1]. Pack them for every household member (general practice).
- **Do not run a propane heater, kerosene heater, camp stove, grill, charcoal or any other fuel-burning heater inside a home, garage or tent.** CDC: never use a portable gas camp stove indoors, never burn charcoal indoors, never use a flameless chemical heater indoors, and never heat your house with a gas oven [5]. Ready.gov and CDC also say not to heat with a gas stove or oven [4][5]. A "propane heater, outdoor only" does not heat the house; do not buy one for that job.
- CDC: do not cook or burn anything on a stove or fireplace that is not vented [5]. A fireplace or wood stove in working order, vented to the outside, is the only exception. See [09_urban_survival.md](../../../09_urban_survival.md#carbon-monoxide-and-fire-heat-light-and-cook-without-hurting-anyone).
- Electric heaters do not work in an outage. Rules for them are in [09_urban_survival.md](../../../09_urban_survival.md#carbon-monoxide-and-fire-heat-light-and-cook-without-hurting-anyone).

**Light (no candles)**
- A flashlight for every household member [1][4]. Add a headlamp or battery lantern (general practice).
- Use flashlights, not candles, in a power outage [8]. On average 20 home candle fires are reported each day [8]. If you want a candle-like glow, use battery-operated flameless candles [8].

**Sanitation**
- Moist towelettes, garbage bags and plastic ties, plus personal hygiene items [1]. Feminine supplies and infant or adult-care items as your household needs them [1]. This entry gives no roll or bag counts because no source does.
- Toilets, waste and washing when the water stops are in [09_urban_survival.md](../../../09_urban_survival.md#toilets-and-hygiene-when-the-water-stops).
- **Bleach.** Keep plain, unscented household bleach on hand for disinfecting [3]. Bleach is not wound care. For water-treatment and sanitizing doses, use [02_water.md](../../../02_water.md#bleach) and [09_urban_survival.md](../../../09_urban_survival.md). Use bleach that is less than a year old; old bleach loses strength (see [02_water.md](../../../02_water.md#bleach)).

**Medical**
- First aid kit: start from the Red Cross list (dressings, bandages, tape, gauze pads, roller and triangular bandages, gloves, tweezers, ointment and antiseptic wipe packets, a thermometer, an emergency blanket, a breathing barrier, a cold compress, instructions) [15]. Check it regularly, replace anything used or out of date, and keep one at home and one in your car [15]. Scale the quantity to your household.
- **Add a purpose-made tourniquet, and take a Stop the Bleed or first aid course** [16]. The Red Cross list does not include a tourniquet [15]. See [06_first_aid.md](../../../06_first_aid.md#severe-bleeding).
- Wound care is mild soap and clean water [11]. Do not use household bleach on a wound.
- Over-the-counter medicines your household already uses: follow the label dose. This entry gives no doses and no counts.
- **Prescriptions.** An emergency can make refills hard, or the pharmacy may be closed [1]. Keep a supply of each person's prescriptions and ask your pharmacist about emergency refills [12]. Ready.gov says "several days" [12]; CDC says at least 1 week [13]. Choose the longer supply if your prescriber, insurer and pharmacist allow it (this guide's judgment). Keep a written list with the diagnosis, dose, frequency, medical supply needs and allergies [12]. This entry does not say how to stretch or change a dose. Questions go to a pharmacist, a prescriber or 911.
- Medicines that need cold: a cooler and chemical ice packs [12]. See [09_urban_survival.md](../../../09_urban_survival.md#medicines-and-power-dependent-devices).
- If someone is seriously hurt or ill, call 911. A kit does not replace emergency care.

**Tools**
- A wrench or pliers to turn off utilities; plastic sheeting, scissors and duct tape for sheltering in place [1].
- A second multi-tool or knife is a backup (general practice). Zip ties and tape for quick repairs (general practice).
- A fire extinguisher [1]. Matches in a waterproof container [1].

## Multi-Use

- **Water storage:** drinking and sanitation [2]. A second use is flushing or washing if the taps stop; see [09_urban_survival.md](../../../09_urban_survival.md#toilets-and-hygiene-when-the-water-stops).
- **Canned and dry food:** meals that need no refrigeration; mix pantry staples into full meals (general practice).
- **Batteries and power banks:** run flashlights, the radio and phone chargers [1][14].
- **Blankets and sleeping bags:** warmth at home, and bedding if you leave [1].
- **Bleach:** water treatment and surface cleaning at the doses in [02_water.md](../../../02_water.md#bleach). Not for wounds, and never mixed with other cleaners.
- **Duct tape and plastic sheeting:** sheltering in place [1], plus patches and repairs (general practice).
- **First aid supplies:** treat minor injuries and manage a problem until you can reach care. They do not replace a doctor, an urgent care or 911. A tourniquet is single-purpose.

## Maintenance / Failure Modes

**Check the kit every 6 months** and reassess your needs every year [1][13].

**Water storage**
- Failure: old water, a cracked or leaking container, a container that once held chemicals. Fix: empty it and refill. Replace home-stored water every 6 months [2][3]. If you need to treat doubtful water, use [02_water.md](../../../02_water.md). Do not add bleach to stored tap water from a treated public supply.
- Prevention: store cool, out of sun, away from gasoline and pesticides [3]. Date every container.

**Canned goods**
- Failure: swelling, bulging, dents, rust or leaks. Fix: throw the can out; do not taste the food to test it [9][10]. See [09_urban_survival.md](../../../09_urban_survival.md#food-what-to-keep-and-what-to-throw-out).
- Prevention: keep them in a cool, dry place [1]. Use the oldest first and buy new (general practice).

**Dry staples**
- Failure: damp, pests, spoilage. Fix: keep boxed food in tightly closed plastic or metal containers [1]. Throw out anything that smells or looks off.

**Batteries and lights**
- Failure: dead or corroded batteries. Fix: replace them. Prevention: test the lights and the radio at each check, and remove batteries from gear you will not use for a long time (general practice).

**Generator**
- Failure: it will not start, or someone moves it close to the house to keep it dry. Fix: follow the maker's manual for fuel and maintenance, and run it only outdoors at least 20 feet from windows, doors and vents [5][7]. Keep it dry [4]. Never run it in a garage even with the door open [5][6].
- Prevention: working CO alarms with fresh batteries before you need the generator [5][6][7].

**Fuel-burning gear**
- Failure: it is used inside "just this once." Fix: there is no fix; carbon monoxide has no smell. Prevention: keep stoves, grills, charcoal and heaters outdoors only [5]. If you feel headache, dizziness, nausea or confusion, get into fresh air and call 911 (see [09_urban_survival.md](../../../09_urban_survival.md#carbon-monoxide-and-fire-heat-light-and-cook-without-hurting-anyone)).

**Medicines and first aid**
- Failure: expired or heat-damaged. Fix: replace anything expired or used. Keep medicines in a cool, dry place, not a hot car or a damp bathroom. Prevention: check the dates at each 6-month check [13][15]. Ask your pharmacist about disposal and replacement.

**Bleach**
- Failure: it loses strength with age. Fix: use bleach less than a year old (see [02_water.md](../../../02_water.md#bleach)). Prevention: write the purchase date on the bottle and store it sealed, away from sun and heat (general practice).
