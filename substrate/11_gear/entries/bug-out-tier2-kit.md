# Bug-Out Tier 2 Kit — 72-Hour Grab-and-Go

> Gear-selection advice below is general practice unless a bracketed number points to a source in `## Sources`. First-aid items are general information, not medical advice; use [06_first_aid.md](../../../06_first_aid.md) for steps. The chapter this entry supports is [11_essential_gear.md](../../../11_essential_gear.md#grab-and-go-bag).

## What & Why

Tier 2 is the lightweight kit for leaving home fast: evacuation, fire, flood, a long drive. Ready.gov names no day count; it says to keep supplies for "several days" [1]. The Red Cross describes 3-day (72-hour) kits as built for "the crucial initial period after an emergency" [4]. That is where the "72-hour" label comes from here.

It assumes you carry your [Tier 1 kit](edc-tier1-kit.md) and adds what Tier 1 cannot hold: water, no-cook food, shelter, a radio, and documents. Ready.gov says to keep supplies in one or two easy-to-carry containers [1]. The Red Cross warns against being "bogged down by bulky, unwieldy items" when you evacuate [4]. A rule of thumb that is this guide's judgment: pack it, then carry it for a mile. If you cannot, cut weight. Cut things that do one job and keep things that do two (general practice).

## Selection Criteria

**Water (storage and treatment)**
- The standard is at least 1 gallon per person per day for drinking and sanitation [2]. CDC: at least 3 days, and try for 2 weeks if possible [3].
- Three days for one person is 3 gallons. At about 8 pounds per gallon (general practice), that is about 24 pounds for one person, too heavy to carry far. If you will travel by vehicle, carry the full amount. If you may go on foot, carry what you can and add a way to treat water (this guide's judgment). Treatment methods and what each does not remove are in [02_water.md](../../../02_water.md).
- A carry of 2 to 3 liters is a hiking amount, not an emergency plan. Do not treat it as a day count.
- Replace water you stored yourself every 6 months. Follow the date on store-bought water [2][3].
- Containers: food-grade, tight lid, unbreakable (not glass). Never reuse containers that held bleach, pesticides or other toxic chemicals [3].
- Bleach dose for treating water is in [02_water.md](../../../02_water.md#bleach). This entry does not restate it.

**Shelter and warmth**
- NPS: emergency shelter means a tent, tarp, bivy or emergency blanket [5]. Extra clothing is also on the list [5].
- Ready.gov: a sleeping bag or warm blanket for each person, a change of clothes for your climate, sturdy shoes [1].
- Tarps tear and plastic sheeting can turn brittle in sun (general practice). Check them for holes before packing. Details in [04_shelter.md](../../../04_shelter.md).
- Strong cord for lashing and repairs. Not a tourniquet; see First aid below.

**Fire (extended)**
- NPS: waterproof matches or a lighter, plus fire starters [5]. Ready.gov: matches in a waterproof container [1].
- Carry at least two methods, and keep them dry (general practice). Technique is in [03_fire.md](../../../03_fire.md).
- Fire stays outdoors. Nothing burns in a tent, vehicle or garage. See [03_fire.md](../../../03_fire.md#fire-indoors-in-tents-and-in-vehicles-the-carbon-monoxide-rule).

**Light**
- A flashlight or headlamp, and extra batteries [1][5]. Not candles; use flashlights or battery lanterns.

**Food**
- At least a several-day supply of non-perishable food, and a manual can opener if any of it is canned [1]. NPS suggests no-cook food for the outdoors [5].
- Pack what you will eat without cooking, and what you have eaten before (general practice). This entry gives no calorie target because none of the sources do.

**Radio and information**
- Ready.gov: a battery-powered or hand-crank radio, plus a NOAA Weather Radio with tone alert [1]. NOAA Weather Radio needs a receiver made to pick up its signal, on seven frequencies between 162.400 and 162.550 MHz, and broadcasts around the clock [9]. An ordinary AM/FM radio may not receive it.
- Phone, charger and a backup battery or portable charger [1][11]. Charge the phone before an outage and keep a backup power source [11].
- Whistle to signal for help [1].
- **Two-way radios.** FRS radios need no license (see [08_communication.md](../../../08_communication.md)). GMRS requires an individual FCC license; a licensee may let immediate family members use their GMRS station [10]. Rules, channels and range are in [08_communication.md](../../../08_communication.md).

**Navigation**
- Ready.gov: local maps [1]. NPS: map, compass and GPS [5]. Learn to use them before you need them. See [07_navigation.md](../../../07_navigation.md).

**Documents, cash and hygiene**
- Copies of important documents in a waterproof container or saved electronically, cash or traveler's checks [1]. A paper list of phone numbers is general practice; Ready.gov's family communication card is a way to write one [14].
- Moist towelettes, garbage bags, plastic ties and personal hygiene items [1].
- Dust mask; plastic sheeting, scissors and duct tape for sheltering in place; wrench or pliers to turn off utilities [1].

**First aid**
- Start from the Red Cross kit list: absorbent dressings, bandages, tape, gauze pads, roller bandages, triangular bandages, gloves, tweezers, an emergency blanket, a breathing barrier, a cold compress, a thermometer, ointment and antiseptic wipe packets, first aid instructions [6]. Check the kit regularly and replace used or out-of-date items [6].
- **Add a purpose-made tourniquet, and take a Stop the Bleed or first aid course.** The Red Cross list does not include one [6]. Stop the Bleed teaches direct pressure, wound packing and a tourniquet [8]. Place it at least 2 to 3 inches from the wound, between the wound and the heart, never on a joint, and note the time [7]. A belt, cord or paracord is a last resort and is not a kit item; see [06_first_aid.md](../../../06_first_aid.md#severe-bleeding).
- Over-the-counter medicines your household already uses. Follow the label dose; this entry gives no doses.
- Prescriptions: keep a supply of your own, and ask your pharmacist about emergency refills [1][12]. Ready.gov says "several days"; CDC says at least 1 week [13]. The longer is the better choice if your prescriber and insurer allow it (this guide's judgment). No dosing or stretching advice is given here.
- Wound care is mild soap and clean water, not bleach; see [06_first_aid.md](../../../06_first_aid.md).

## Multi-Use

- **Tarp or emergency blanket:** rain shelter, ground cloth, wind block, a bright signal (general practice).
- **Cord:** lashing a shelter, securing a load to the pack, repairs. Not a tourniquet.
- **Tourniquet:** one job. Do not borrow it for lashing or repairs; it stays in the first aid kit.
- **No-cook food:** needs no fire or water to prepare [5].
- **Radio:** weather and hazard alerts [9], news, and the family check-in channel if you carry two-way radios.
- **Headlamp and backup light:** hands-free work after dark, reading a map, first aid, and signaling (general practice).
- **Map and compass:** route planning and staying oriented [5].
- **Whistle:** signals for help at a distance [1].
- **Water container:** stores treated water; a metal one can also be used to boil it (general practice).

## Maintenance / Failure Modes

**Check the whole bag every 6 months.** The Red Cross says to review and update a survival kit every six months and confirm items are intact, working and in date [4]. CDC says the same for emergency kits [13]. Ready.gov says to reassess your needs every year [1].

**Water**
- Failure: stored water is old or a container has cracked or leaked. Fix: replace home-stored water every 6 months and follow the date on bottled water [2][3]. Keep it cool and out of direct sun [3].

**Tarp, blanket and cord**
- Failure: tears, abrasion, fraying. Fix: tape or patch, re-tie, and cut away damaged cord. Prevention: inspect for holes before packing (general practice).

**Batteries and lights**
- Failure: dead batteries, corrosion on the contacts. Fix: replace the batteries. Prevention: test the light at each check, and remove batteries from gear you will not use for a long time (general practice).

**Radios**
- Failure: nobody has turned the radio on since it was packed, or the batteries are dead. Fix: turn on the radio and the NOAA Weather Radio at each check. Replace batteries on the schedule above (general practice).

**Water filter or treatment**
- Failure: a filter clogs or cracks, tablets are out of date. Fix: follow the maker's cleaning steps, check expiry, and use another method from [02_water.md](../../../02_water.md) if you doubt the water.

**Food and medicines**
- Failure: damaged packaging, heat exposure, expiry. Fix: replace anything past its date. Do not use medicine past its date; ask your pharmacist if you are unsure. Keep medicines in a cool, dry place, not a hot car or damp bathroom. Rotate food on the 6-month check [4].
- Do not eat from cans that are swollen, dented or corroded; throw them out [15]. CDC also says to throw out damaged or bulging cans after a disaster [16].
