# Individual First Aid Kit

> This is general information, not medical advice. Call 911 first for any life-threatening injury or illness. Reading is not training: take a hands-on course. Bracketed numbers point to `## Sources`; "(general practice)" and "(this entry's rule)" mark points no opened source states. The steps for using what is in the kit are in [06_first_aid.md](../../../06_first_aid.md#a-basic-kit) and, for bleeding, [bleeding-control-and-shock](../../06_first_aid/entries/bleeding-control-and-shock.md). The chapter this entry supports is [11_essential_gear.md](../../../11_essential_gear.md#first-aid-kit).

## What & Why

A first aid kit holds the supplies for the first minutes of an injury, before or instead of professional care. The Red Cross says to keep one in your home and in your car [1]. The National Park Service lists first-aid supplies among the Ten Essentials for the outdoors, checked for expiry [4]. Ready.gov lists a first aid kit among its basic supplies [2].

The kit is only as good as the person using it. A tourniquet nobody has practiced with, or a bandage nobody can find, does little. Skills matter more than contents; see [skills-to-practice-now](../../10_key_resources/entries/skills-to-practice-now.md). This entry lists contents by purpose, then covers the tourniquet, personal medicines, checking the kit, and what goes wrong.

Where kits live: on you ([edc-tier1-kit](edc-tier1-kit.md)), in the grab bag ([bug-out-tier2-kit](bug-out-tier2-kit.md)), in the car ([vehicle-kit](vehicle-kit.md)), and at home ([home-stockpile-tier3](home-stockpile-tier3.md)).

## Selection Criteria

### Start from the Red Cross list

The Red Cross recommended first aid kit list [1]:
- 2 absorbent compress dressings (5 x 9 inches)
- 25 adhesive bandages, assorted sizes
- 1 adhesive cloth tape (10 yards x 1 inch)
- 5 antibiotic ointment packets and 5 antiseptic wipe packets
- 2 hydrocortisone ointment packets
- 2 pairs of non-latex gloves
- 1 roller bandage 3 inches wide and 1 roller bandage 4 inches wide
- 5 sterile gauze pads (3 x 3 inches) and 5 (4 x 4 inches)
- 2 triangular bandages
- Tweezers, an oral thermometer (non-mercury, non-glass), 1 emergency blanket, 1 breathing barrier with a one-way valve, 1 instant cold compress
- First aid instructions
- 2 packets of aspirin: on the Red Cross list, but do not give aspirin to anyone just because it is in the kit, and never to a child unless their provider says to [12]. Read "Medicines" below before you stock it

The Red Cross says this list is for a family of four [14]. For one person, scale the counts by judgment (general practice), but do not cut the bleeding-control items below.

### By purpose

**Stop heavy bleeding.** Absorbent compress dressings, sterile gauze pads, roller bandages, triangular bandages and cloth tape [1], plus a purpose-made tourniquet (next section). Stop the Bleed teaches three ways to control bleeding: apply pressure with your hands, pack the wound and press, and apply a tourniquet [13]. Wound packing is taught as a trained skill [8]. The Red Cross severe-bleeding course also covers a hemostatic dressing [7]. Learn it in class before you buy one (general practice).

**Clean and cover small wounds.** Adhesive bandages, antiseptic wipes, antibiotic ointment packets and sterile gauze [1]. For a minor cut: wash your hands, wash the cut thoroughly with mild soap and water, press to stop the bleeding, apply antibacterial ointment, and cover with a clean bandage that will not stick [10]. Do not try to clean a major wound [10]. Non-stick pads are this guide's addition (general practice).

**Protect yourself.** Non-latex gloves and a breathing barrier with a one-way valve [1].

**Support an injured limb.** Triangular bandages and roller bandages [1]. An elastic wrap and a splint are this guide's additions (general practice). Splinting steps are in [fractures-sprains-and-splinting](../../06_first_aid/entries/fractures-sprains-and-splinting.md).

**Cold and heat injuries.** An emergency blanket and an instant cold compress [1].

**Check and cut.** Tweezers [1]. NPS lists scissors under its repair kit and tools item [4]. Scissors or trauma shears for clothing are this guide's addition (general practice), and a pen to write down the time (general practice, from the guide's [A basic kit](../../../06_first_aid.md#a-basic-kit)).

**Information.** First aid instructions [1], your emergency phone numbers [1], and a list of each person's prescriptions with diagnosis, how much and how often as the label states, medical supply needs and allergies [3].

**Dehydration.** Oral rehydration salts are this guide's addition (general practice). The recipe and steps are in [dehydration-and-diarrhea](../../06_first_aid/entries/dehydration-and-diarrhea.md).

### The tourniquet

The Red Cross list does not include a tourniquet [1]. This guide adds one, with training.

1. **Buy a purpose-made windlass tourniquet.** Follow the manufacturer's instructions for the one you have [7]. This is the kit item.
2. **A headwrap, belt, scarf or cord is not a kit item, and it is not a substitute for a purpose-made tourniquet.** Use a tourniquet from a first-aid kit if there is one [9]. Only if you have none and the limb bleeding is life-threatening, MedlinePlus describes a last-resort band 2 to 4 inches wide, wrapped around the limb several times and knotted, with a stick between two knots [9]. A narrow cord is not what it describes (this entry's reading of [9]).
3. **Know the rules before you need them.** Place it at least 2 to 3 inches from the wound, between the wound and the heart, never on a joint [5][7]. It can go over clothing [5]. Pull the strap as tight as you can, twist the windlass rod until the bleeding stops or you cannot twist it further, and secure the rod [5][7]. Note the time [5]. Do not loosen or remove it; that is for a medical professional [7]. If bleeding continues, put a second one above the first, closer to the heart [7].
4. **Get trained.** Stop the Bleed courses teach tourniquet use, are held in person around the world, and an online course exists [6]. The Red Cross also teaches it [7]. The Red Cross bleeding page lists the tourniquet as "if trained" [8]. Reading this entry is not training.
5. **Keep it reachable.** Store it where you or someone else can grab it with either hand, not at the bottom of a pack (general practice). Tell the household where it is.

## Multi-Use

- **Triangular bandage:** sling, a bandage over a large wound, a way to tie a splint, and a head covering (general practice).
- **Cloth tape:** secures dressings and splints, and covers a blister (general practice).
- **Gauze pads and compress dressings:** cover a wound, or press on bleeding [1][13].
- **Emergency blanket:** warmth, and a ground sheet or wind barrier (general practice).
- **Gloves:** protect the helper, and keep the kit clean [1].
- **Tourniquet:** one job. Do not borrow it for lashing, repairs or restraint. It stays in the kit.

## Maintenance / Failure Modes

### Medicines

- **Personal medicines are your prescriber's call.** The Red Cross says to include personal items such as medications [1]. Ready.gov says to talk to your doctor or pharmacist about an emergency supply of medicines [3]. How much to keep, how to refill and how to store each one is for your prescriber and pharmacist. This entry gives no amounts, dosing or advice about holding back, splitting or stretching any prescription. See [chronic-med-continuity](../../06_first_aid/entries/chronic-med-continuity.md).
- **A prescribed device, such as an epinephrine auto-injector,** is used as its label and your prescriber direct, and you call 911 [06_first_aid.md](../../../06_first_aid.md#severe-allergic-reaction-anaphylaxis). See [anaphylaxis-and-allergic-reaction](../../06_first_aid/entries/anaphylaxis-and-allergic-reaction.md).
- **Over-the-counter medicines.** Ready.gov lists pain relievers, anti-diarrhea medicine, antacids and laxatives among home supplies [2]. Follow the label. They do not replace 911 care.
- **Aspirin.** The Red Cross list includes it [1]. Do not give it to someone because it is in the kit. Reye syndrome has occurred in children given aspirin when they had chickenpox or the flu, and children should not get aspirin unless their provider says so [12]. Ask a clinician, pharmacist or 911 dispatcher, and follow the label.

### Checking the kit

- The Red Cross says to check the kit regularly, check expiration dates, and replace used or out-of-date contents [1]. NPS says to check the expiration date on all items and replace them as needed [4]. Ready.gov says to replace expired items as needed [2].
- This guide's schedule is a kit check every 6 months, with a full review each year [11_essential_gear.md](../../../11_essential_gear.md#keeping-it-working). Restock right after any use.
- FDA says the expiration date is a critical part of deciding whether a medicine will work as intended, and that an expired medicine should not be used [11]. Store medicines in a cool, dry place such as a dresser drawer, not a humid bathroom cabinet [11].
- Look for worn, cracked or sticky parts on the tourniquet's strap, buckle or windlass, and replace it if any are damaged (general practice). Check that the pack seals are intact and the gloves are not brittle (general practice).

### What goes wrong

- **Nobody knows where the kit is, or it is buried.** Keep it in one place and tell the household (general practice).
- **A tourniquet no one has practiced with.** Take a course [6][7].
- **A cord or headwrap counted as the tourniquet.** It is not a kit item. Buy the real one.
- **Expired or heat-damaged contents.** Check dates every kit check [1][4][11].
- **Medicines in a hot car or a damp bathroom.** FDA says humid bathroom cabinets can reduce potency [11].
- **Used items never replaced.** The kit has gaps when you need it [1].
- **Counts sized for four, kit used by one.** Or the reverse. Match the kit to the household (general practice).
- **Trying to clean a major wound.** Control the bleeding, cover it, and get help [10].
- **Reaching for aspirin or another medicine just because it is there.** See above [12].
- **Waiting.** Call 911 for severe bleeding that will not stop [10].
