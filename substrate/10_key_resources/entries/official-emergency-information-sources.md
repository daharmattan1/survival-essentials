## Goal & When To Use

In an emergency you need two things: the warning, and the facts. Both come from officials, and both can fail if you wait until the day to set them up. This entry lists the official sources, what each one is for, how to sign up, and how to check a rumor.

Do the setup (Part A) on a calm day. Use Part C when the power or the internet is out. For lists by topic, see [`10_key_resources.md`](../../../10_key_resources.md#official-sources-by-topic). For judging a video, post or AI answer, see [`10_key_resources.md`](../../../10_key_resources.md#how-to-judge-a-source-in-a-hurry).

**Emergencies:** call 911. **Poisoning:** 1-800-222-1222 (America's Poison Centers), free, 24 hours a day [14]. Call 911 if the person has collapsed, had a seizure, has trouble breathing, or cannot be woken [14]. The poison help line, 211 and 511 are not substitutes for 911.

## What each source is

| Source | What it is | How you get it | Good for |
|--------|------------|----------------|----------|
| **Wireless Emergency Alerts (WEA)** | Short alerts that officials send to phones in a targeted area, with a sound and vibration [1] | Automatic. "There is no need to subscribe," and it is free [1] | Tornado, flash flood, AMBER and other urgent alerts |
| **Emergency Alert System (EAS)** | National public warning system. Delivers alerts over AM, FM and satellite radio, and broadcast, cable and satellite TV [2]. It can let the president address the nation within 10 minutes [1] | Turn on a radio or TV | Alerts when you are near a radio or TV |
| **NOAA Weather Radio All Hazards (NWR)** | Nationwide network of radio stations. Broadcasts official NWS warnings, watches, forecasts and other hazard information 24 hours a day, plus non-weather alerts [1][5] | Special receiver or scanner. Seven frequencies, 162.400 to 162.550 MHz [5] | Alerts that work with power or the internet out, if you have batteries [6] |
| **Local alert systems** | Your city or county's own text, email or phone alerts, sirens and school or workplace notices [3] | Opt in (see Part A) | Local detail: road closures, shelters, evacuation zones |
| **National Weather Service (weather.gov)** | Forecasts and the Active Alerts page. Enter a city or ZIP code for a local forecast [8] | Website, or the FEMA app | Weather detail |
| **FEMA app** | Real-time NWS weather and emergency alerts for up to five locations; helps find shelters [9][10] | Free download for iOS and Android [3][10] | Watching alerts for family in other places |
| **211** | Local help line: connects you to local resources and services. FCC-designated; 24/7; dial 211 [13] | Dial 211 | Non-emergency help: disaster recovery, housing, food [13] |
| **511** | Travel-condition phone number, run by state and local agencies. No federal requirement to provide it [15] | Dial 511, or your state DOT website | Road conditions and closures |
| **CDC** | Official health information for emergencies [16] | cdc.gov; CDC-INFO 800-232-4636 [16] | Health after floods, outbreaks, chemical or radiation events |
| **Poison Help** | 53 poison centers behind one number [14] | 1-800-222-1222, or the online tool at poisonhelp.org [14] | Swallowed, breathed or touched a poison |
| **DisasterAssistance.gov / FEMA helpline** | Federal disaster aid applications [17] | DisasterAssistance.gov or 1-800-621-3362 [17] | After a declared disaster |

**One thing to know about alerts.** WEA, EAS and NWR are sent through IPAWS, FEMA's national system for local alerting. Local officials write their own alerts, and FEMA does not review, edit or approve them [1]. The messages are authenticated [2]. So an alert that arrives this way is an official one.

## Steps

### Part A: Set up now (about an hour, once)

1. **Check your phone for WEA.** Look in your phone's settings and read the manual. Older phones may not support WEA, and some phones require you to turn it on. Some carriers label these messages "Government Alerts" or "Emergency Alert Messages." If alerts still do not arrive, ask your wireless provider [1].
2. **Do not turn alert categories off.** You can opt out of imminent-threat and AMBER alerts and public safety messages, but not national alerts [4]. If you opted out, you will not get them.
3. **Install the FEMA app** and set your alert locations: your main location plus up to four more, such as family or work [9]. You pick a 5-digit ZIP code or a city and state [9].
4. **Find your local alert system.** Search the web for your town, city or county name and the word "alerts," visit the website of your local emergency management or public safety office, or phone them [3]. Sign up for text or email alerts. Small charges may apply for text messages from your carrier [3].
5. **Ask about phone-call alerts** (Enhanced Telephone Notification, such as Reverse 911). Most systems include landline numbers. Mobile and internet phones may need an opt-in. Ask your local emergency management [3].
6. **Ask about sirens.** Outdoor sirens are meant for people outdoors and are not designed to be heard inside building walls. Some communities still have sirens that no longer work, so ask your local emergency management office [3].
7. **Get a NOAA Weather Radio receiver** with SAME and battery backup [6]. SAME (Specific Area Message Encoding) lets you pick your county, so it responds only to alerts for your area [6]. Battery backup matters because outages come with storms [6]. Look for the Public Alert or NWR logo; NWS does not endorse brands [6].
8. **Get your county's SAME code.** NWS lists the codes on its Weather Radio pages (by state, or by transmitter map), or you can call 1-888-NWR-SAME (1-888-697-7263) [7].
9. **Keep a battery-powered or hand-crank radio**, plus extra batteries, a phone charger and a backup battery in your kit [12].
10. **Share and list.** Ready.gov's alerts fact sheet suggests making a list of every alert system available to you and making sure everyone in the household receives them [3].
11. **Write the numbers on paper:** 911, poison help (1-800-222-1222), 211, 511 or your state DOT site, the number of your local emergency management office, your SAME code.

### Part B: In the emergency

1. **Danger now?** Call 911 (voice call if you can; see [`08_communication.md`](../../../08_communication.md#call-911-well)).
2. **An alert arrives** through your phone (WEA), NOAA Weather Radio, a radio or TV (EAS), a siren, or your local system. These are official. Read it to the end and do what it says (this entry's rule). Keep listening for updates.
3. **Keep a warning source on.** Do not use airplane mode while a warning is possible (general practice; see [`08_communication.md`](../../../08_communication.md#get-warnings-in)).
4. **Save the phone battery.** Ready.gov suggests low power mode or airplane mode to conserve energy [11]. Airplane mode blocks alerts, so use it only when no warning is possible (this entry's rule). Text instead of calling, because texts need less bandwidth [11].
5. **Look for detail** on weather.gov (Active Alerts), your local emergency management site, or 211 for local help [8][13].
6. **Driving or travelling?** Dial 511 or use your state DOT site for road conditions [15]. Do not read a phone while you drive (general practice).
7. **Health or poison question?** Poison Help for poisons. CDC for health after a disaster [14][16].

### Verify a rumor

After a disaster there are many rumors and scams. Use these steps before you act on a message, whether you read it, heard it, or were sent it [17].

1. **Where did it come from?** An alert through WEA, NWR, EAS, a siren or your local system is official [2]. A forwarded text, a social post, or a call from a stranger is not an alert.
2. **Find a second official source.** Check weather.gov for weather, your local emergency management for local orders, FEMA or DisasterAssistance.gov for federal aid, CDC for health, and poison help for poison [8][14][16][17]. Confirm before you evacuate, move or spend money because of a message (this entry's rule; see [`08_communication.md`](../../../08_communication.md#rumors-and-bad-information)).
3. **Share only verified information,** and discourage others from passing along unverified items [17].
4. **Be wary of scams.** FEMA says it never charges to apply for aid or for an inspection. Report suspected FEMA fraud to the FEMA Disaster Fraud Hotline, 866-720-5721 [17].
5. **Five questions** for any other source: [`10_key_resources.md`](../../../10_key_resources.md#how-to-judge-a-source-in-a-hurry).

### Part C: No internet

- **Radio.** AM and FM stations carry EAS alerts [2]. NOAA Weather Radio works without the internet. NWS also says to tune to your local radio and TV stations for forecasts, watches and warnings [7].
- **Weak NWR signal.** A full-power transmitter typically reaches about 40 miles over level ground. Range depends on terrain, antenna and receiver. Try moving the radio near a window facing the transmitter, upstairs, and away from other electronics. An external antenna can also help [7].
- **Phone.** WEA alerts go out through cell towers to phones in the affected area [3]. FEMA says phones can still receive alerts even when cellular networks are overloaded [2]. This still needs cell service, so a phone with no signal cannot receive them (general practice).
- **Calls fail; texts often work.** Mobile networks can become overwhelmed in emergencies [11].
- **Sirens** reach people outdoors, not indoors [3].
- **Paper.** Your written numbers and a printed map do not need power [12].
- **Internet delivery of NWS products cannot be guaranteed.** If you have no radio signal, NWS suggests the radio steps above first and weather.gov as an extra [7].

## Failure Modes & Fixes

| Failure | Cause | Fix |
|---------|-------|-----|
| No alert on your phone | Phone does not support WEA, alerts turned off, alert category opted out, airplane mode, phone off, or no cell service [1][4] | Check settings; ask your carrier [1]. Keep the phone on in a storm. |
| Weather radio goes off for every county | Receiver has no SAME [6] | Use SAME and set your county [6]. |
| Weather radio dead in the outage | No battery backup [6] | Battery backup, and check batteries on a schedule ([`10_key_resources.md`](../../../10_key_resources.md#training-to-take-before-you-need-it)). |
| No radio signal | Distance, terrain, indoors [7] | Window, upper floor, away from electronics, outside antenna [7]. Listen to local AM/FM [2][7]. |
| You hear no siren indoors | Sirens are for people outdoors; some do not work [3] | Do not rely on sirens. Use a phone and a radio. |
| FEMA app alerts look sparse for your town | The app shows NWS alerts [10] | Also sign up for your local system (Part A, steps 4 to 5). |
| 511 does not work in your state | No federal requirement to provide it [15] | Use your state DOT website. |
| You call 211 for an emergency | 211 is a referral service [13] | Call 911 for an emergency. |
| Believing a rumor | Unverified message [17] | Part B, Verify a rumor. |
| Local sign-up costs you money | Carrier text charges [3] | Check the terms; small charges can apply [3]. |
