## Goal & When To Use

Snares and traps work while you do other things. Snares exploit animals' use of established trails. FM 21-76 treats trapping as a way to gain food when other means fail, and warns that the animal you catch can hurt you [2].

**This is not a method to start with.** It is regulated almost everywhere, it carries disease and bite risks for you, and the legal position in an emergency is unclear (see below). Read the legal note and the handling warning before you set anything.

---

## ⚠ Legal Note — Know Your State (Not Legal Advice)

Trapping and snaring are regulated almost everywhere. Virginia is used here only as one worked example [1]:

- A trapping license is required in most cases, with some exemptions (for example, landowners on their own land).
- **No deadfall traps may be used.** The Virginia DWR page says so directly [1]. Do not read the deadfall sections below as permission.
- Land snares must have loops 12 inches or less in diameter, with the bottom of the loop no higher than 12 inches above the ground, and may be used only with the landowner's written permission [1].
- All traps must be visited **once each day** and every animal removed [1].
- Rabbits may be trapped only in season (Virginia: October 15 to January 31) and **only with box traps** [1].
- Opossum and raccoon on private land require the landowner's permission [1].

**Is there an emergency or survival exemption?** No source opened for this entry supports one. The Virginia DWR page contains no general emergency or survival exception; its only closed-season allowance is for landowners dealing with damage or a health threat [1]. Do not assume that being in an emergency makes snaring or deadfalls lawful (this guide's judgment). Know your regulations; contact your state wildlife agency. This is not legal advice.

## ⚠ Handling a Caught Animal — Tularemia and Bites

- **Tularemia.** Wear gloves when handling animals, especially rabbits and rodents [3]. Penn State says to wear plastic gloves with wild rabbits because the disease passes through cuts and abrasions in the skin [5].
- **Check the liver.** If a rabbit's liver has numerous white lesions about the size of a pinhead, discard the rabbit and do not eat it [4].
- **Flu-like illness after handling a rabbit:** see a doctor and say you handled one [4].
- **A live animal in a trap or snare can bite.** All mammals have teeth and nearly all will bite in self-defense. Even a squirrel can inflict a serious wound, and any bite risks infection. A mother can be extremely aggressive defending young, and any animal with no route of escape will fight when cornered [2].
- **A simple snare usually does not kill the animal** [2]. Plan for a live, struggling, biting animal when you approach. Do not reach for it bare-handed.
- Cook game thoroughly before eating [3]. Temperatures and the full dressing sequence are in `fish-and-game-processing-basics`.

## Steps

### Simple Snare (Wire or cordage loop)

Based on FM 21-76 [2]. A simple snare is a noose placed over a trail or den hole and attached to a firmly planted stake.

1. **Find where animals move.** Look for runs and trails, tracks, droppings and chewed vegetation. Animals have bedding, water and feeding areas with trails between them; a well-built snare placed haphazardly catches nothing [2].
2. **Make a noose large enough to pass freely over the animal's head.** Wire is the best choice, because cordage may loosen enough to slip off [2]. Where the law limits loop size, stay within it (Virginia example: 12 inches or less in diameter [1]).
3. **Place the noose across the trail**, with the other end tied to a firmly planted stake. If the noose is cordage, hold it upright with small twigs or grass blades [2].
4. **Funnel the trail** if you can: a barrier that narrows the path toward the snare, so the animal cannot turn aside [2].
5. **Set several snares rather than one** (general practice).
6. **Check at least once a day**, as the Virginia law requires [1], and sooner in warm weather. Wear gloves when you go to the animal (see the handling warning).

### Deadfall Traps — Figure-4 and Paiute (prohibited in Virginia; read the legal note)

**Deadfalls are prohibited where Virginia law applies [1], and this guide gives no construction steps.** FM 21-76 describes the figure-4 and the Paiute in a few sentences and warns that the trigger "requires close tolerances and precise angles" [2]. No source opened for this entry supports calling a deadfall humane, and a weight that does not kill leaves a hurt animal you must still approach as a hazard (this guide's judgment).

### Trap Line (several snares checked on a route)

1. **Scout where animals move** and place snares around bedding, water and feeding areas [2].
2. **Use one design along the line** for easy checking and repair (general practice).
3. **Walk the line at least once every day** and record which sites produce [1] (general practice for the record-keeping).
4. **Move a snare that catches nothing** to another trail (general practice).
5. **Mask your scent.** FM 21-76 says mammals depend on smell; coat hands and trap with mud, or smoke the parts, or let a trap weather a few days [2].

## Failure Modes & Fixes

| Problem | Cause | Fix |
|---------|-------|-----|
| Noose slips off animal's head | Cordage loosens; loop too large | Use wire where possible [2]; keep the loop only large enough to pass over the head [2] |
| Animal escapes | Weak stake, struggle loosens the noose | Plant the stake firmly [2]; a simple snare usually does not kill [2] |
| No catches | Wrong location, animals alarmed by scent or disturbance | Move to a trail near feeding or water [2]; mask scent and avoid disturbing vegetation [2]; use bait the animals know [2] |
| Catch taken by predators before you arrive | Delayed checking | Check at least daily [1]; sooner in warm weather (general practice) |
| Snare destroyed by weather | Wind, falling branches, flooding | Inspect after storms; rebuild (general practice) |
| You are bitten or scratched | Live, cornered animal | Wash the wound with soap and water and watch for infection; seek care if it worsens [2] (general practice for washing) |
| Fever or flu-like illness after handling a rabbit | Possible tularemia | See a doctor and mention the rabbit [4] |
