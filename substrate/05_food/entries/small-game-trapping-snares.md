## Goal & When To Use

Small game traps work passively over hours or days, turning predictable animal behavior into reliable protein. Snares exploit animals' tendency to follow established trails and enter narrow passages. Deadfall traps use gravity and a trigger mechanism to dispatch prey instantly, minimizing suffering. Trap lines — multiple traps checked daily along game trails — distribute catch risk.

Small game trapping is the **intermediate step** between foraging (lowest energy) and active hunting (highest energy cost). Rabbits, hares, squirrels, and ground birds are:
- Abundant in most temperate regions
- High-calorie protein (rabbit ~200 cal per 3 oz; hare ~160)
- Predictable in movement (follow trails, return to warrens)
- Capturable with minimal tools

**Legal note:** Trapping is **regulated or prohibited in most jurisdictions** outside genuine survival emergencies. Emergency framing (no food, no rescue prospect, survival-critical) is the legal and ethical boundary. Never use these methods for recreation or sport hunting.

## Steps

### Simple Snare (Wire or cordage loop)

1. **Select the location:** Find a narrow game trail with clearances on both sides (deer paths, rabbit runs between brush). Look for compressed vegetation, droppings, hairs on vegetation (all signs of animal traffic).
2. **Prepare the snare:** Form a loop 4–6 in diameter from wire, cordage, or plant stem. The loop should slip freely through a sliding knot or loop at one end.
3. **Set the height:** Position the loop at head height for the target animal (rabbit snares: 2–3 in above ground; hare: 3–4 in; squirrel: 6–8 ft high on branch).
4. **Anchor the snare:** Tie the fixed end of the snare to a sapling, stake, or branch that will provide leverage to lift the animal off the ground.
5. **Position the loop:** Suspend the loop above the trail using two small sticks as guides, narrowing the passage to funnel the animal's head into the loop.
6. **Bait (optional):** Suspend a food item (grain, vegetable scraps) just above the loop to draw the animal's head higher.
7. **Check regularly:** Visit every 6–12 hours. Animal struggle is brief; check frequently to prevent decomposition in warm weather.

### Figure-4 Deadfall Trap (Gravity trap with trigger)

1. **Gather materials:**
   - One flat baiting stick (~8 in long, ~1 in diameter)
   - One trigger stick (~6 in, carved with a distinctive notch or angle)
   - One vertical support stick (~3–4 in, sharpened at one end)
   - One heavy weight (flat rock or log, 20–50 lbs)
   - Bait (nuts, grain, berries)

2. **Assemble the trigger mechanism:**
   - Place the trigger stick at a precise angle to the support stick so they form an unstable notch.
   - The baiting stick lies horizontally beneath the trigger, held in place by the trigger's angle.
   - When an animal pulls the bait, the trigger releases and the weight drops.

3. **Position the trap:**
   - Set the deadfall in a location where small game feeds or shelters (near berry patches, warren entrances, log piles).
   - Level the ground beneath the weight so the trap drops vertically.
   - Position the bait so the animal must enter from the side and pull to dislodge it.

4. **Stability test:** Apply gentle pressure to the bait. The weight should fall immediately with minimal disturbance.

5. **Check daily:** Visit every 12–24 hours. A successful trap requires immediate processing to prevent waste.

### Paiute Deadfall (Alternative trigger design)

1. **Prepare sticks:**
   - One upright support stick (4–6 in, driven into the ground at an angle)
   - One horizontal baiting lever (10–12 in)
   - A release hook carved into the lever
   - One heavy flat weight (rock or log, 25–50 lbs)

2. **Assembly:** The baiting lever balances on the upright support; bait is attached to the end. When pulled, the lever rotates and a carved notch releases a pin holding the weight in place.

3. **This design is more sensitive** to light prey (squirrels, ground fowl) than figure-4 and works in very confined spaces.

### Trap Line (Multiple traps, systematic coverage)

1. **Scout the area:** Identify 5–10 active game trails within a 1-mile loop. Mark trails with subtle blazes (rock stacks, bent twigs, never permanent marks).
2. **Space traps evenly:** Place one snare or deadfall every 200–300 yards along the loop, favoring high-traffic areas (trail junctions, warren entrances, feeding sites).
3. **Maintain consistency:** Use the same trap type along a line for ease of checking and repair.
4. **Establish a daily circuit:** Walk the trap line at the same time each day (dawn or dusk preferred; more active game at low light). Record which traps are triggered, which locations yield catches, and which are inactive.
5. **Rotate locations:** After 3–5 days with no catch at a site, move that trap 50–100 yards upstream or to an adjacent trail.

## Failure Modes & Fixes

| Problem | Cause | Fix |
|---------|-------|-----|
| Snare slips off animal's head | Loop too large, anchor too weak | Reduce loop diameter to 4–5 in; reinforce anchor with heavier stake or additional guy-lines |
| Animal escapes snare | Slippage, cordage breaks | Use non-slip loop (wire preferred); test cordage before setting; choose fixed anchor capable of holding 20–30 lbs |
| Deadfall trigger is too sensitive | Slight touch releases it; grass/debris interferes | Adjust trigger angle; clear vegetation under the weight; test with gradual pressure before deployment |
| Deadfall misfires (weight doesn't drop) | Mud/debris in trigger notch, sticks warped | Clean trigger assembly; use fresh, dry sticks; position trap on level ground |
| No catches on trap line | Wrong location, animals avoiding traps, timing | Scout fresh trails; avoid areas with human scent; reset traps every 3–5 days; check at different times (dawn/dusk); bait more aggressively |
| Catch eaten by predators before retrieval | Delayed checking, coyotes/foxes raiding | Check every 12 hours maximum in warm weather; hang successful catches from branches out of reach; move trap line if predator activity is heavy |
| Snare or trap destroyed by weather | Wind, fallen branches, flooding | Inspect trap line after storms; rebuild damaged traps immediately; avoid low areas prone to flooding |

## ⚠ Legal & Ethical Note

**Snaring and small-game trapping are illegal or heavily regulated in most jurisdictions.** Regular use in non-emergency conditions violates state/provincial wildlife laws and carries fines. Emergency exception criteria (genuine survival threat, no realistic rescue prospect, starvation risk) are jurisdictionally variable and context-dependent. This entry describes the technique for genuine survival situations only. **Always know your local regulations and stay within legal bounds except in documented emergencies.** Not legal advice; consult your regional wildlife authority.
