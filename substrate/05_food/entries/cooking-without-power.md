## Goal & When To Use

Use this when the power is out at home, or you are cooking in camp, and the stove, oven or microwave will not run. The job has three parts: **do not poison anyone with carbon monoxide, do not make anyone sick with undercooked or dirty food, and do not start a fire.**

The easiest answer is to **eat food that needs no cooking.** Cook only what you must. Which food to keep and which to throw out is in [`09_urban_survival.md`](../../../09_urban_survival.md#food-what-to-keep-and-what-to-throw-out) and [food-safety-during-outages.md](../../09_urban_survival/entries/food-safety-during-outages.md). Planning how many days of food you have is in [calorie-planning-and-rationing.md](calorie-planning-and-rationing.md). Survey guide: [05_food.md](../../../05_food.md#cooking-and-eating-when-the-power-is-out).

This entry names no wild plant, mushroom or animal as edible. For "can I eat this?" go to [`regional/_index.md`](../../../regional/_index.md).

## ⚠ CARBON MONOXIDE: THE RULE FOR EVERY STOVE, GRILL AND FIRE

> ⚠ **Carbon monoxide (CO) has no smell or color. Burning anything indoors can kill the people sleeping or sitting in the room before they feel sick.**
>
> - **Outdoors only.** Camp stoves, charcoal grills and generators go outdoors. Ready.gov gives at least 20 feet from windows for all three, and from doors and attached garages for generators [2]; CDC gives more than 20 feet from windows, doors and vents for generators [3]. This repo uses 20 feet from windows, doors and vents for all three (this repo's rule). Keep a camp stove or grill away from your home's air intakes as well [6].
> - **Nothing burns inside a tent, vehicle, garage, snow shelter or home.** The only exception is a fireplace or stove built to vent and kept in working order. (A gas range already in the kitchen is covered in Step 4: cooking only, never heat.) Never burn charcoal indoors, and never use a portable camp stove or camping equipment inside a home, garage, vehicle or tent [3][4][5].
> - **A vent or a cracked window is not a fix.** It does not make a grill, camp stove, heater or generator acceptable indoors. CPSC says that even with doors and windows open, these spaces can trap CO and let it build to deadly levels [5]. The range hood advice in Step 4 is cooking practice for an installed gas range only.
> - **Never heat a home with a gas oven or range** [2][3][5].
> - **Symptoms:** headache, dizziness, weakness, upset stomach, vomiting, chest pain, confusion. They can feel like the flu. People who are asleep or drunk can die before any symptoms [3][5].
> - **If an alarm sounds or you think someone has CO poisoning:** everyone outside to fresh air now. Do not look for the source. Then call 911 or the fire department. Do not go back in until responders say so [5].
>
> **Verdict:** If you are not sure the place is outdoors and open, do not light it.

Alarm placement, generator rules and first aid in full: [generator-and-heater-co-prevention.md](../../09_urban_survival/entries/generator-and-heater-co-prevention.md) and [carbon-monoxide-poisoning.md](../../06_first_aid/entries/carbon-monoxide-poisoning.md).

## Steps

### 1. Start with food that needs no cooking

Ready.gov lists ready-to-eat canned meat, fruit and vegetables, protein or fruit bars, dry cereal, peanut butter, dried fruit and canned juice [1]. Commercially canned food can be eaten straight from the can without warming [1]. Keep a manual can opener [1]. Eat cold food first (see [`09_urban_survival.md`](../../../09_urban_survival.md#carbon-monoxide-and-fire-heat-light-and-cook-without-hurting-anyone)).

**Power-out clocks (from [`09_urban_survival.md`](../../../09_urban_survival.md#food-what-to-keep-and-what-to-throw-out)):** a closed fridge holds cold about 4 hours [2]; a full freezer about 48 hours [2]; a half-full freezer about 24 hours [20]. No thermometer and out longer than 4 hours: throw out perishables. At 40 F or higher for 2 hours or more: throw it out [2]. Thawed food that still has ice crystals, or is at 40 F or below, can be cooked or refrozen [16]. Cook thawing food first, then shelf-stable food.

### 2. Pick the cooking spot

1. **Outdoors, open air, flat and steady,** at least 20 feet from windows, doors, vents and an attached garage (this repo's rule; Ready.gov gives 20 feet from windows [2]).
2. **Away from things that burn.** NFPA gives 10 feet of clearance from the home, railings and overhanging branches for propane and charcoal grills [18]. The 20-foot rule above already covers this near your windows, doors and vents.
3. **Not in a tent, including the doorway** (this entry's rule). Cook in the open, then bring the food inside.
4. **Never leave a lit grill or stove alone, and keep children away** [18].
5. **Use the fuel the appliance is made for.** Charcoal lighter fluid only as its label says; never gasoline [18].
6. **Treat hot coals as a hazard after cooking** (general practice). Do not carry a live grill or hot coals indoors "for the rain."

### 3. Fireplace or wood stove (only if it vents and works)

- Cook in one only if it is built to vent and in working order [3][6]. Do not cook or burn anything on a stove or fireplace that is not vented [3].
- Open the damper before lighting and keep it open until the ashes are cool [6]. Never burn charcoal in a fireplace [6].
- A vented appliance works only if its vent needs no power [6].
- Candle warmers, chafing dishes and fondue pots burn fuel. Ready.gov lists them for emergency cooking [1]. **This repo's rule is that nothing burns inside except a vented fireplace or stove in working order, so leave them out** (this entry's rule).

### 4. Gas range

A gas range may light with a match if it has no electric igniter. Check its manual (general practice). A gas range is for cooking only; never use it or the oven to heat the home [2][3][5][22]. While you cook on it, run the range hood or open a window, as EPA advises for gas cooking [22][23], and keep a working CO alarm [3]. That is cooking practice, not a way to make burning fuel for heat indoors acceptable.

### 5. Canned food: warning signs

- **Throw out** cans that are bulging, open or damaged, and cans that spurt liquid or foam when opened [16]. Ready.gov says not to eat from cans that are swollen, dented or corroded, or any food that looks or smells abnormal [1].
- **Never taste food to test it.** You cannot see, smell or taste botulism toxin, and tasting a small amount can be deadly [17][16]. When in doubt, throw it out [16][17].
- **Home-canned jar of unknown origin: throw it out** [17].
- **To heat food in a can,** wash the can with hot soapy water [16], remove the label, and **open it first** before putting it on the heat [1][16]. Ready.gov also says to disinfect the can [1]; bleach amounts are in [`02_water.md`](../../../02_water.md). Never heat a sealed can.

### 6. Hands, water and surfaces

1. **Wash hands** with soap and clean running water for at least 20 seconds before, during and after preparing food [13]. No running water: pour from a jug (see [`09_urban_survival.md`](../../../09_urban_survival.md#toilets-and-hygiene-when-the-water-stops)). Use hand sanitizer of at least 60 percent alcohol only when soap and water are not available [13].
2. **Keep raw meat, poultry, seafood and eggs away from ready-to-eat food** [7].
3. **Use treated water for cooking and food prep.** Under a boil-water notice, or with any water of uncertain quality, use bottled water or water that was boiled or treated [14][15]. Boil rule: rolling boil for 1 minute; 3 minutes above 5,000 ft or if you are unsure of your elevation (CDC says 6,500 ft; this repo uses the lower number). Details and bleach doses: [boil-disinfection.md](../../02_water/entries/boil-disinfection.md) and [`02_water.md`](../../../02_water.md).
4. **Dishes:** wash, rinse and sanitize with clean water. Bleach amounts are in [`02_water.md`](../../../02_water.md) and [`09_urban_survival.md`](../../../09_urban_survival.md#toilets-and-hygiene-when-the-water-stops).

### 7. Cook to temperature (see MANDATORY PREPARATION), then serve

Put cooked food into clean dishes, not back on the plate that held it raw. Food held between 40 F and 140 F spoils fast. Do not leave perishable food out more than 2 hours, or 1 hour above 90 F [7]. Reheat leftovers to 165 F [7][8].

## ⚠ MANDATORY PREPARATION

### Cook to a thermometer reading. Color is not a test.

> ⚠ **You cannot tell if food is cooked through by its color and texture (except seafood). Use a food thermometer** [7][12].
>
> | Food | Cook to |
> |---|---|
> | Fish | 145 F [7][8] |
> | Ground meat and ground venison | 160 F [7][8] |
> | Venison, wild or farm-raised, all cuts | 160 F [8] |
> | Bear, wild pig, raccoon and other wild meat-eaters or omnivores, in any form including ground and jerky | 165 F [19] |
> | Rabbit and hare | 165 F [10] |
> | Poultry and game birds | 165 F [7][8] |
> | Soups, stews, casseroles, leftovers | 165 F [7][21] |
>
> Also on USDA's chart: beef, pork and lamb steaks, roasts and chops 145 F with a 3-minute rest [8].
>
> CDC's 2024 report says cooking wild game meat to at least 165 F is necessary to kill *Trichinella*, a parasite that can make people seriously ill, and that freezing might not be sufficient [19]. That is why this repo puts bear, wild pig and other wild meat-eaters or omnivores at 165 F in any form (this repo's rule). Venison stays at 160 F.
>
> **How to use a thermometer** [11][12]
> 1. Put the tip in the middle of the thickest part of the food, away from bone, fat and gristle [11].
> 2. Check irregular foods, such as a whole chicken or a roast, in several places [11][12]. Wait until the reading stops moving [12].
> 3. Clean the thermometer with soap and water between uses [11]. Wash it before it touches cooked food.
> 4. Test it in a glass of ice and water: it should read 32 F. If a digital one does not, replace it [12].
>
> **No thermometer:** cook until the meat is well done all the way through, then keep cooking. This is general practice and not reliable. Bear meat is very dark, so color cannot guide you [9]. The only real test is a thermometer.
>
> **Verdict:** No reading at the target temperature, no serving. Do not taste to check.

*Rabbit note:* USDA's chart lists 160 F for rabbit [8]; Missouri's wildlife agency sets 165 F [10]. This repo uses 165 F so both are met.

## Failure Modes & Fixes

| What goes wrong | Why | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Grill or camp stove moved into the garage or tent "just for the rain" | CO builds up fast; people die asleep [3][5] | Cook in the open air, 20 feet from openings; if it is raining, eat no-cook food |
| Gas oven used to warm the kitchen | CO and fire risk [2][3][5] | Dress in layers, go to a warm place (see [`09_urban_survival.md`](../../../09_urban_survival.md#warm-or-cool)) |
| Stove outside, but exhaust blowing toward a window or the air intake | CO can get in [6] | Move it so fumes go away from all openings |
| Fireplace used with the damper shut, or an unvented one | CO in the room [3][6] | Do not use it; cook outdoors |
| Meat judged by color | Color is not a reliable test [7] | Thermometer in the thickest part |
| Thermometer tip touches bone | False reading [11] | Move it to the thickest part, away from bone, fat and gristle [11] |
| Cooked food put back on the raw-meat plate | Germs move from raw to cooked food [7] | Clean plate and utensils |
| Bulging can eaten "because it smells fine" | Botulism toxin has no smell or taste [17] | Throw it out |
| Cooking with water from an unknown source | Germs in the food [15] | Bottled, boiled or treated water |
| Hands washed without soap or in dirty water | Germs spread [13] | Soap and clean water for 20 seconds; sanitizer only if no soap and water [13] |
