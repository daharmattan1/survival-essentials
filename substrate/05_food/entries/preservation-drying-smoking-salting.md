## Goal & When To Use

Use this entry to dry food at home with methods that extension services have tested: **jerky** (thin strips of lean meat), **dried fruit** and **dried vegetables**. Drying removes the water that germs need to grow [2]. For meat, drying alone is not enough. The meat must be heated to a kill temperature as well (see MANDATORY PREPARATION).

This entry does **not** teach cold smoking, improvised salt curing, brining for storage, sun-drying meat, or drying fish. See the warning below. Without a working dehydrator or oven, a thermometer and clean water, **cook the meat and eat it within days, or keep it cold**. Fresh meat and fish handling is in [fish-and-game-processing-basics.md](fish-and-game-processing-basics.md). Survey guide: [05_food.md](../../../05_food.md#keeping-food-drying-smoking-salting-canning). Outage food rules: [food-safety-during-outages.md](../../09_urban_survival/entries/food-safety-during-outages.md).

This entry names no wild plant, mushroom or animal as edible. Questions like "can I eat this?" go to [`regional/_index.md`](../../../regional/_index.md).

## ⚠ UNTESTED METHODS THIS ENTRY DOES NOT COVER

> ⚠ **Cold smoking, improvised salt curing and raw-meat air or sun drying can leave botulism toxin, Salmonella, Listeria or Trichinella in the food, and you cannot see, smell or taste any of them.**
>
> - **Botulism.** You cannot see, smell or taste the toxin, and tasting a small amount of contaminated food can be deadly [10]. CDC lists meats, poultry, fish and seafood among the low-acid foods linked to botulism [10]. In cold-smoked fish the heat is not enough to weaken botulism spores, so safety depends on a tightly controlled process [14]. Commercial smoked-fish rules set time, temperature and measured salt in the flesh [14]. A campfire and a handful of salt give none of those controls.
> - **Trichinella** (a parasitic worm in muscle). CDC says curing (salting), drying, smoking or microwaving meat alone does not consistently kill the worms. Freezing wild game may not kill them either, because some wild-game worms resist freezing [11].
> - **Salmonella and E. coli O157:H7.** Extension services link illnesses to improperly made homemade jerky. Drying alone does not kill them [2].
> - **Listeria.** Pregnant women, people 65 and older, newborns and people with weak immune systems are told to avoid refrigerated smoked fish [12].
> - **Sun or air drying meat.** NCHFP says meats are high in protein, ideal for germ growth when heat and humidity cannot be controlled, and should be dried indoors in an oven or dehydrator [7].
> - **Drying fish.** This entry has no tested method for fish. Do not dry, smoke or salt it.
>
> **Verdict:** If it is not in the Steps below, do not rely on it to keep food. Cook it, keep it cold, or leave it. If you cannot account for how smoked, salted, dried or canned food was made, throw it out. CDC says not to eat food if you do not know how it was canned, preserved or fermented [10] (this entry's rule extends that to smoked, salted and dried food).

For tested curing or smoking instructions, NCHFP maintains pages for pork (bacon, ham, sausage) that point to extension publications [15]. They assume a kitchen with measured ingredients and refrigeration, so this entry does not reproduce them.

## Steps

### A. Jerky from meat strips

1. **Choose lean meat.** NCHFP names beef, pork, venison and smoked turkey breast. Raw poultry is generally not recommended for jerky because of texture and flavor [1][2]. If you do dry poultry or game birds, heat them to 165 F first (see MANDATORY PREPARATION). Bear, wild pig, raccoon and other wild meat-eaters or omnivores also need 165 F in any form, including jerky strips [16] (this repo's rule, from CDC's 2024 finding on Trichinella). Venison strips stay at 160 F.
2. **Keep it clean and cold.** Wash hands with soap and running water for at least 20 seconds before and after handling raw meat. Use clean tools. Keep meat at 40 F or below; use red meat within 3 to 5 days, ground meat and poultry within 2 [1][2].
3. **Wild game.** If gut contents touched the meat or your hands, do not make jerky from it; cook it thoroughly instead [1]. Chill deer carcasses fast [1]. Wild game can carry *Trichinella*. Heating to temperature is the protection, not freezing [11][16]. CDC's 2024 report says cooking wild game meat to at least 165 F is necessary to kill *Trichinella* and that freezing might not be sufficient [16].
4. **Slice no thicker than 1/4 inch,** and trim and discard all fat, which turns rancid [1][2]. Partly freezing the meat first eases slicing [1].
5. **Marinate in the refrigerator** (1 to 2 hours, or overnight). Do not save and reuse marinade [1][2]. Marinade is for flavor and tenderness [1]; its salt is not the safety step (this entry's rule).
6. **Do the heat step** (see MANDATORY PREPARATION). The heat-first method is in step 1 there.
7. **Dry.** Lay strips on racks close together, not touching [1]. Put them in a dehydrator or oven preheated to 140 F [1][3]. USDA's Meat and Poultry Hotline says to hold a steady 130 to 140 F and not to raise the heat to rush drying, because higher heat causes "case hardening" that can trap moisture inside [2]. Meat that was not heated in marinade takes about 10 to 24 hours; heated strips dry faster, so start checking after 3 hours [1].
8. **Test.** Dry until a test piece cracks but does not break when bent [1][3]. Cut it open: there should be no moist or underdone spots [3].
9. **Cool, pat off oil, and pack airtight** in glass jars, heavy plastic bags or vacuum bags [1][2].
10. **Store it.** NCHFP: properly dried jerky keeps at room temperature for two weeks in a sealed container; refrigerate or freeze for longer [1]. Penn State says to eat it within one to three weeks [3], and that jerky keeps one to two months refrigerated below 40 F [4]. This entry's rule: **room temperature up to 2 weeks; refrigerator up to 1 to 2 months; freezer for longer.** KSU says homemade jerky is best used within 1 to 2 months [2].

### B. Fruit

1. **Pretreat light-colored fruits** such as apples, pears, peaches and apricots, usually in an acidic solution (ascorbic acid or citric acid), so they do not darken [9].
2. **Dry.** In a dehydrator, Penn State starts at 145 F while there is surface moisture, then drops to 135 to 140 F after an hour [9]. In an oven, 140 to 150 F with the door open 2 to 3 inches so moisture escapes [9]. NCHFP says dehydrators are designed to dry food fast at 140 F [8].
3. **Test.** Fruit is dry when it is pliable and no beads of moisture form when you press a piece between your fingers [9]. Home-dried fruit should hold about 20 percent moisture [6].
4. **Condition it.** Cool the fruit, pack it loosely in jars, seal, and let it stand 7 to 10 days. Shake daily. If condensation forms inside a jar, the fruit needs more drying [6]. This evens out the moisture and lowers mold risk [6].

### C. Vegetables

1. **Blanch first** (a brief scald). Penn State recommends a solution with 1/4 teaspoon of citric acid per quart of water for most vegetables; steam blanching is also an option. Onions, garlic, peppers and herbs do not need blanching [9]. Penn State gives no blanching times, and this entry gives none: find tested times from an extension service before you blanch.
2. **Dry** with the same temperatures as fruit (Part B, step 2) [9]. Dry until tough, brittle or crisp. Dry vegetables hold about 10 percent moisture and need no conditioning [6][9]. This entry gives only that moisture test, not drying times.

### D. Sun drying (fruit only)

NCHFP says fruits are high in sugar and acid, so only they can be dried outdoors, and only when conditions are right: hot, dry, breezy days, at least 85 F, with humidity below 60 percent. Bring the food in at night. Vegetables and meats are not recommended for outdoor drying [7]. Avoid "hardware cloth" screens (galvanized, coated with cadmium or zinc), and avoid copper and aluminum screens [7]. Penn State calls sun drying impractical in humid Pennsylvania [9].

### E. Storing dried food

Cool the food completely first; warm food sweats and the moisture feeds mold [6]. Pack in clean, dry, insect-proof containers in amounts you will use at once, and store in a cool, dry, dark place [6]. NCHFP gives 4 months to 1 year as the range. Most dried fruit keeps about 1 year at 60 F and 6 months at 80 F; vegetables about half as long [6]. Check often [6]. Food that got damp but is not spoiled: use it right away or re-dry it [6]. **Moldy food is discarded** [6].

## ⚠ MANDATORY PREPARATION

### The jerky heat step (non-omissible)

> ⚠ **Drying alone does not kill the germs in raw meat. Heat the meat first, then dry it. Measure the heat with a food thermometer.**
>
> 1. **Heat meat strips to 160 F before drying [1][2][5], and poultry or game birds to 165 F** [2][5]. **Bear, wild pig, raccoon and other wild meat-eaters or omnivores: 165 F** in every form, jerky strips included. CDC's 2024 report says cooking wild game meat to at least 165 F is necessary to kill *Trichinella* [16] (this repo's rule). Venison stays at 160 F. Use a metal stem-type food thermometer and check several strips. Color does not tell you [1][13].
> 2. **How:** bring the strips and marinade to a boil and boil for 5 minutes, then drain and dry. Strips thicker than 1/4 inch may need longer [1][2]. USDA also names steaming or roasting to temperature before dehydrating [5].
> 3. **Why first:** a dehydrator may not reach 160 F or 165 F, and most instructions omit this step [2]. Evaporating water keeps the meat cool, and surviving germs get harder to kill [5].
> 4. **If you skipped the heat step:** NCHFP and KSU allow heating the strips after drying as an added safety step: place strips close together on a baking sheet in an oven preheated to 275 F for 10 minutes, if they were cut 1/4 inch thick or less (thicker strips take longer to reach 160 F) [1][2]. This entry's rule: use that only for beef, pork and venison strips, never poultry, game birds, bear, wild pig or other wild meat-eaters or omnivores, and check the temperature.
> 5. **Dry at 140 F** (steady 130 to 140 F in a dehydrator) until a test piece cracks without breaking [1][2][3].
> 6. **Do not rely on freezing, salt or smoke** to protect you. CDC says some wild-game worms survive freezing, and that curing, drying and smoking alone do not consistently kill *Trichinella* [11][16].
>
> **Verdict:** No thermometer reading of 160 F (165 F for poultry, game birds, bear, wild pig and other wild meat-eaters or omnivores), no jerky. If you cannot measure the temperature, cook the meat and eat it fresh.

## Failure Modes & Fixes

| What goes wrong | Why | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Strips dried without the heat step | Dehydrators often never reach 160 F or 165 F; germs survive [2] | Heat first as above. If already dried (meat strips only), oven heat step 10 minutes at 275 F, or do not eat [1] |
| Strips too thick | Thicker strips heat slowly and unevenly [1] | Slice no thicker than 1/4 inch; give thicker strips longer and check with the thermometer |
| Heat turned up to hurry the drying | "Case hardening" traps moisture inside [2] | Keep a steady 130 to 140 F [2] |
| Jerky bends and is moist inside | Not dry enough | Dry longer until a test piece cracks without breaking [1][3] |
| Mold on jerky, fruit or vegetables | Not dry enough, warm packing, or moisture reabsorbed [6] | Throw out moldy food [6]. Cool fully, pack dry, store cool |
| Condensation in the jar | Fruit still holds too much moisture [6] | Return it to the dehydrator, then condition again [6] |
| Jerky kept too long on the shelf | Room-temperature life is 2 weeks [1] | Refrigerate or freeze; use within the times above |
| Gut contents touched the meat | Fecal bacteria on the meat [1] | Do not make jerky from it; cook it thoroughly and eat it fresh [1] |
| Smoked, salted, dried or canned food of unknown origin, or that smells or looks off | Possible botulism, which has no warning smell, look or taste [10] | Do not taste it. When in doubt, throw it out [10] |
| "Just a small taste to check" | A small taste of botulism-contaminated food can be deadly [10] | Never taste to test |

**Spoilage signs** (dried and stored food): visible mold [6], condensation in the container [6], a sour or rancid smell or a slimy or sticky surface (general practice). A clean look and smell prove nothing, because botulism toxin gives no sign [10]. If the food was not heat-treated and dried by the steps above, throw it out.
