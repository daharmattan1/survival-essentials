## Goal & When To Use

This entry is the universal *technique* for cleaning, cooling and cooking fish and game: how to remove organs, scale, skin and portion, and what you cannot do without tested equipment (smoking, salt-curing, canning). The *biology* (which species, which diseases, which seasons) is region-specific and lives in the regional game-animal entries.

Wild fish and game carry parasites (*Trichinella*, tapeworms), bacteria (*Salmonella*, *Campylobacter*, *E. coli*), tularemia (rabbits) and, in deer and elk, chronic wasting disease (a prion disease). Cooking to temperature controls the parasites and bacteria. Nothing in this entry controls the prion disease; see MANDATORY PREPARATION.

Speed matters, but this guide has no opened source for exact time clocks. Dress the animal as soon as possible after the kill [6], cool it quickly to 35–40 F [5][6], and cook it or get it cold soon after. Food held between 40 F and 140 F spoils fast [1]. Never leave perishable food out more than 2 hours (1 hour above 90 F) [1].

## Steps

### Fish (Scaling, Gutting, Filleting)

Wear disposable gloves if you have them [6]. Cool fish quickly to 35–40 F and keep the flesh out of direct sunlight [6]. The cutting steps below are general practice.

#### 1. Scaling (if keeping skin)

1. Place the fish on a flat surface with head pointing away from you.
2. Using a knife back, rock scraper, or smooth stick, scrape against the scale direction (tail toward head) in long strokes.
3. Scrape both sides and the belly until all scales are removed.
4. Rinse in cold water.

#### 2. Gutting (removing internal organs)

1. Make a shallow cut from the anus (near the tail) to the gills, just below the belly surface. **Do not cut into the digestive tract.**
2. Spread the belly open. The intestines and organs will be exposed.
3. Grasp the gills and pull toward the tail, drawing out the organs in one motion.
4. Use your finger or a stick to scrape dark material (blood, bile) from along the backbone. Rinse with cold water.
5. Remove the gills (optional, but they spoil quickly). Cut the gills where they attach at the jaw and head.

#### 3. Filleting (optional; for faster cooking)

1. Place the fish on its side. Make a cut behind the gills down to the backbone.
2. Using the backbone as a guide, slice from tail toward the head, keeping the knife parallel to the backbone.
3. Repeat on the other side.
4. If filleting with skin on: rinse and trim any remaining scales or dark lateral line meat.
5. If skinning: place the fillet skin-down and run the knife between flesh and skin, angling the blade slightly downward.

#### 4. Cooking and keeping

- **Cook to 145 F internal, or until the flesh is opaque and separates easily with a fork** [1][2]. Check with a thermometer in the thickest part.
- **Keep cold.** Hold fish at 35–40 F until you cook it [6].
- **Do not dry, smoke or salt-cure fish in the field.** This guide gives no tested field method. Commercial smoked fish depends on measured salt in the flesh, controlled temperature and set times. For example, hot-smoked fish is held at 145 F or higher for at least 30 minutes, with water-phase salt of 2.5 to 3.5 percent in the finished flesh [12]. Cold smoking does not heat the fish enough to weaken botulism spores, so its safety depends on controlling the process [12]. You cannot see, smell or taste botulism toxin [10]. If in doubt, throw it out [10].

### Small Game: Rabbits, Hares, Squirrels, Ground Birds

#### 1. Field Dressing (as soon as possible after the kill)

1. **Put on gloves before you touch the animal.** Wear gloves with any rabbit, hare or rodent because of tularemia, which can enter through cuts or scrapes [17][18][6].
2. Place the animal on its back. Make a shallow cut from the sternum to the anus, avoiding the digestive tract.
3. Reach into the abdomen and pull out the organs.
4. **Inspect the liver.** If a rabbit's liver has numerous white lesions about the size of a pinhead, discard the rabbit and do not eat it [18]. For any other animal, a spotted or discolored liver is a reason to discard the animal (this guide's rule).
5. Discard the organs after inspecting the liver (this guide's rule; this guide does not recommend eating wild-game organ meats).
6. Wipe out the cavity with a clean cloth or paper towel. **Do not use grass or snow** [4].
7. Cool the carcass quickly. Do not pile warm carcasses together, because that slows cooling [6]. Above 40 F, resealable bags of ice or snow in the cavity speed cooling [6].
8. Wash your hands with soap and water after handling (general practice).

#### 2. Skinning

1. **For rabbits/hares:** Make a shallow cut around the hind legs just above the feet. Pull the hind legs apart sharply; the skin often tears loose from the legs.
2. Grasp the skin at the rear and pull it forward and up toward the head. Separate the head and front legs from the skin by twisting.
3. **For squirrels:** Make a small cut on the belly and along the legs. Work the skin off the same way, using the tail as a handle.
4. **For ground birds:** Pluck feathers by hand (preserves skin) or remove skin and feathers together by cutting the wings at the shoulders and peeling.

#### 3. Final Cleaning (if not done in the field)

1. If the abdomen was not opened in the field, make a shallow cut from breastbone to anus.
2. Remove organs (inspect the liver as above) and discard them.
3. Rinse the cavity with cold, clean water, then dry it thoroughly (excess moisture encourages bacterial growth) [6].

#### 4. Butchering Into Portions

1. Separate the hind legs (pull back and twist or cut between hip and body).
2. Separate the front legs (cut at the shoulder joint).
3. Remove the spine and rib cage in one piece.
4. Trim away tough fascia, hair/feather remnants, and any bruised or discolored meat.
5. Portion into meal-sized pieces.

#### 5. Cooking and keeping

- **Rabbit and hare:** USDA's chart says 160 F internal [2][3]. The Missouri Department of Conservation says 165 F [18]. The two sources differ; this guide's rule is 165 F, which covers both.
- **Squirrel and other wild small mammals:** 160 F is the CDC's figure for wild game [15]. This guide's rule is 165 F, to match rabbit and birds.
- **Game birds and all poultry:** 165 F internal, with juices running clear [1][4][6].
- **Broth, stews, soups, leftovers:** 165 F [5].
- **Jerky from small game:** heat the strips to 160 F (165 F for birds) before drying, then dry at 140 F until a test strip cracks but does not break [7][8][9]. See Preserving under Medium Game, below.
- **Refrigerator:** use game birds within 3 days at 40 F or lower [4]; other game meat within 2 to 3 days [5].

### Medium Game: Deer, Wild Boar (abbreviated; see regional entries for the full field-dress sequence)

#### 1. Field Dressing (as soon as possible after the kill)

1. **Wear gloves** (rubber or latex) throughout field dressing and butchering [16][5]. Do not use household knives or utensils for field dressing [16].
2. Position the carcass on its back with hind legs elevated.
3. Make a careful cut from the breastbone to the pelvis, just deep enough to open the abdomen without puncturing organs.
4. Reach in and separate the windpipe at the throat; grasp all organs and pull downward and out of the body cavity.
5. **Do NOT cut into or touch the contents of the stomach or intestines** (contamination).
6. **Where chronic wasting disease (CWD) is present, avoid handling internal organs, especially the brain or spine** [14]. Minimize contact with the brain, spinal cord, spleen and lymph nodes as you work [16].
7. Inspect the liver for spots or discoloration; if present, do not eat the animal (this guide's rule).
8. Wipe the cavity with paper towels or clean cloths, not grass or snow [5][4]. Do not rinse the deer in a pond or stream [6]. If you rinse with clean water, dry the cavity thoroughly [6].
9. Cool the carcass to 35–40 F quickly [5]. If air temperature is above 40 F, put resealable bags of ice or snow in the cavity [6]. Prop the cavity open for air.

#### 2. Skinning & Butchering

1. Hang the carcass from the hind legs if possible (gravity assists).
2. Make cuts around all four legs just above the hooves.
3. Cut along the belly from sternum to tail.
4. Pull the hide away from the carcass, working from legs toward the spine. Use the knife only where needed.
5. Remove the head (cut between the skull and first vertebra) if desired.
6. Butcher into major muscle groups: hind legs, front legs (shoulders), backstrap (along the spine), ribs.
7. Trim all white fascia, shot-damaged meat and hair.
8. Where CWD is present, bone out the meat and remove fat and the weblike membranes, which removes lymph nodes [16]. Boning out helps you avoid high-risk tissues; it does not tell you whether the animal is infected. Testing does [14].

#### 3. Preserving

- **Hold the carcass or meat at 40 F or below for two to three days at most** if you are processing it yourself [5]. Outdoor temperatures swing, so this guide gives no longer hanging time.
- **Cook venison (all cuts: steaks, roasts, ground) to 160 F internal** [2][3]. Penn State lists 145 F for whole cuts [5]; USDA's chart lists 160 F. Use 160 F. Stews and casseroles: 165 F [5].
- **Refrigerator:** 2 to 3 days [5]. **Freezer:** properly wrapped game meat keeps 9 to 12 months [5]. Do not refreeze thawed product [5].
- **Jerky:** slice thin, heat the strips to 160 F first (in marinade, in an oven, or simmered), then dry at 140 F until a test strip cracks but does not break when bent [7][8][9]. Store jerky airtight in a cool, dark place; Penn State says eat it within one to three weeks, or freeze it [7]. Penn State's venison page says dried jerky keeps one to two months refrigerated below 40 F [5].
- **Unheated hanging, sun-drying or smoke-drying of raw strips is not a method this guide gives.** CDC says curing (salting), drying, smoking or microwaving meat alone does not consistently kill *Trichinella* worms [11].
- **Salt-curing, brining, cold smoking or hot smoking for storage:** no tested field procedure. Do not rely on them to keep meat. FM 21-76 describes smoking meat to keep for weeks and eating it without further cooking [19]; this guide does not repeat that, because it conflicts with the CDC statement above.
- **Canning:** pressure canning is the only recommended method for low-acid foods such as meat [10]. Use a tested pressure-canning recipe from an extension service for your altitude. This guide gives no times or pressures.

## ⚠ MANDATORY PREPARATION

### COOK TO TEMPERATURE — ALWAYS

Cook wild fish and game to these internal temperatures, measured with a food thermometer in the thickest part away from bone. Color is not a reliable guide [1].

- **Fish:** 145 F, or until the flesh is opaque and separates easily with a fork [1][2].
- **Rabbit and hare:** 160 F on USDA's chart [2][3]; 165 F per the Missouri Department of Conservation [18]. This guide's rule: 165 F.
- **Venison (all cuts):** 160 F [2][3]. **Ground game meat:** 160 F [6].
- **Game birds and poultry:** 165 F [1][4][6].
- **Wild boar, bear and other wild meat-eaters:** 160 F (CDC advice for wild game) [15]. Bear meat is very dark, so use a thermometer, not color [15].
- **Stews, casseroles, leftovers:** 165 F [5].
- **Jerky:** heat strips to 160 F (165 F for poultry and game birds) before drying, then dry at 140 F [7][8][9].

Raw or undercooked wild game can transmit *Trichinella*, bacteria and tularemia. Freezing does not reliably kill wild-game *Trichinella*, because some strains resist freezing [11][15]. Curing, drying and smoking do not either [11]. Cooking to temperature is the protection [11].

### NEVER EAT (CROSS-LINK REGIONAL GAME-ANIMAL DISEASE BLOCKS)

- **Brain, spinal cord, eyes, spleen, tonsils and lymph nodes of deer and elk** [13][16]. CWD is a prion disease, and cooking temperatures do not destroy prions [13]. The CDC names the brain and spine [14]; Alberta Health names brain, spinal cord, spleen and lymph nodes [13]; Indiana DNR adds eyes and tonsils [16].
- **CWD facts to keep straight:** the CDC says CWD infections in people have never been reported and it is not known whether people can be infected [14]. Where CWD is present, strongly consider having the animal tested; if it tests positive, do not eat it [14]. A negative test does not guarantee the animal is uninfected [13].
- **A rabbit whose liver has numerous pinhead-size white lesions** [18].
- **Meat from a visibly sick animal** (staggering, drooling, confusion, emaciation), and any carcass whose liver is spotted or discolored (this guide's rule).
- **Meat contaminated by gut contents.** Trim the exposed surface and cook thoroughly, or discard it if the gut wall ruptured badly (general practice).
- **Any smoked, salted, dried or canned game or fish you cannot account for.** Botulism toxin cannot be seen, smelled or tasted; if in doubt, throw it out [10].

**Regional cross-link:** The active region's game-animal disease index (`../../regional/<biome>/game-animals/_index.md`) carries species-specific CWD, *Trichinella* and tularemia blocks. Read it before preparing any wild game.

## Failure Modes & Fixes

| Problem | Cause | Fix |
|---------|-------|-----|
| Meat tastes strong or off | Slow dressing, slow cooling, gut contents on the meat | Dress promptly, cool to 35–40 F, keep meat clean and dry [5][6]. If it smells sour or spoiled, do not taste it; discard it (general practice) |
| Meat is warming before you can cook it | Warm weather, no cooling | Ice or snow in resealable bags in the cavity above 40 F [6]. Cook or refrigerate soon; never leave perishable food out more than 2 hours (1 hour above 90 F) [1] |
| Worry about parasites | Wild game can carry *Trichinella* | Cook to the temperatures above [11][15]. Salting, drying, smoking and freezing are not reliable controls [11][15] |
| Jerky molds | Not dried enough, wet storage | Heat to 160 F first, dry at 140 F until strips crack but do not break, store airtight and cool [7][8]. Discard moldy jerky (general practice) |
| Smoked, salted or home-canned meat smells off, looks off, or you are unsure how it was made | Spoilage or botulism | Do not taste it. Throw it out [10] |
| Skin or feathers won't come off | Carcass has not cooled; tight adhesion | Cool the carcass first; for birds, brief scalding in hot water before plucking (general practice) |
