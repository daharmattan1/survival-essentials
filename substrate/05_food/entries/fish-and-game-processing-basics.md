## Goal & When To Use

Fish and game are edible only when processed correctly. Improper handling introduces parasites (*Trichinella*, tapeworms), bacterial pathogens (*Salmonella*, *Campylobacter*), and prion disease (chronic wasting disease in deer/elk). This entry describes the universal *technique* (how to remove organs, scale, skin, portion) that applies to any fish or game animal. The *biology* (what species, what diseases, what seasons) is region-specific and cross-links the regional game-animal disease entries.

Processing is **immediate and non-deferrable**: field dress within 1–2 hours of kill, remove organs within 4 hours, and begin cooking or preservation within 8 hours (less in warm weather). Delay = spoilage + toxin accumulation.

## Steps

### Fish (Scaling, Gutting, Filleting)

#### 1. Scaling (if keeping skin)

1. Place the fish on a flat surface with head pointing away from you.
2. Using a knife back, rock scraper, or smooth stick, scrape against the scale direction (tail toward head) in long strokes.
3. Scrape both sides and the belly until all scales are removed (they should flake off easily).
4. Rinse thoroughly in cold water.

#### 2. Gutting (removing internal organs)

1. Make a shallow cut from the anus (near the tail) to the gills, just below the belly surface. **Do not cut into the digestive tract.**
2. Spread the belly open. The intestines and organs (heart, liver) will be exposed.
3. Grasp the gills and pull toward the tail, drawing out the organs in one motion. Most internal parts will come free together.
4. Use your finger or a stick to scrape any dark material (blood, bile) from the backbone. Rinse thoroughly with cold water.
5. Remove the gills (optional, but they spoil quickly). Cut the gills where they attach at the jaw and head.

#### 3. Filleting (optional; for faster cooking)

1. Place the fish on its side. Make a cut behind the gills down to the backbone.
2. Using the backbone as a guide, slice from tail toward the head, keeping the knife angled parallel to the backbone.
3. Repeat on the other side.
4. If filleting with skin on: rinse and trim any remaining scales or dark lateral line meat (it spoils fastest).
5. If skinning: place fillet skin-down and run the knife between flesh and skin, angling the blade slightly downward as you move along the fillet.

#### 4. Cooking or preservation

- **Immediate cooking** (preferred): Boil, bake, or fry gutted whole fish or fillets at 140–160°F internal temperature.
- **Drying/smoking**: Split the fish lengthwise, remove the backbone if large, and hang in smoke or above a low fire for 24–48 hours until jerky-dry.
- **Salting**: Gut and fillet, then layer with salt (1 part salt to 3 parts fish by weight) in a sealed container. Fish will cure in 7–10 days and last months if kept cool/dry.

### Small Game: Rabbits, Hares, Squirrels, Ground Birds

#### 1. Field Dressing (immediate, within 2 hours of kill)

1. Place the animal on its back. Make a shallow cut from the sternum to the anus, avoiding the digestive tract.
2. Reach into the abdomen and grasp the organs (heart, liver, lungs). Pull them out carefully.
3. **Inspect the liver:** If it has spots, discoloration, or unusual texture, discard the animal (sign of disease).
4. Separate edible organs (liver, heart, kidneys) and set aside in a clean cloth. Discard the rest.
5. Wipe the cavity with clean cloth or grass to remove blood and debris.
6. Cool the carcass quickly: if possible, hang in shade or bury briefly in cool soil while you continue work.

#### 2. Skinning

1. **For rabbits/hares:** Make a shallow cut around the hind legs just above the feet. Pull the hind legs apart sharply; the skin often tears loose from the legs.
2. Grasp the skin at the rear and pull it forward and up toward the head like removing a sweater. The head and front legs will remain in the skin; separate them by twisting.
3. **For squirrels:** Make a small cut on the belly and along the legs. Work the skin off the same way, using the tail as a handle.
4. **For ground birds:** Pluck feathers by hand (harder but preserves skin) or remove the skin and feathers together by cutting the wings at the shoulders and peeling.

#### 3. Evisceration & Final Cleaning (if not done in field)

1. If the abdomen was not opened in the field, make a shallow cut from breastbone to anus.
2. Remove organs and discard (except edible liver/heart/kidneys, saved separately).
3. Rinse the cavity with cold water. Remove any blood clots or debris.

#### 4. Butchering Into Portions

1. Separate the hind legs (pull back and twist or cut between hip and body).
2. Separate the front legs (cut at the shoulder joint).
3. Remove the spine and rib cage in one piece (optional: crack ribs for marrow).
4. Trim away tough fascia, hair/feather remnants, and any bruised or discolored meat.
5. Portion into meal-sized pieces (2–4 oz for stew, larger for whole roasting).

#### 5. Cooking or Preservation

- **Immediate cooking** (preferred): Stew, roast, or boil whole pieces until an internal thermometer reads 160–165°F (rabbit/hare) or 165°F (game birds).
- **Drying/Jerky**: Cut meat into strips (~¼ in thick), hang in smoke or above a low fire for 12–24 hours until brittle.
- **Broth**: Simmer bones, joints, and trimmings for 2–4 hours to extract all nutrition, then cool and preserve by smoking or salting.

### Medium Game: Deer, Wild Boar (abbreviated; see regional entries for full field-dress sequence)

#### 1. Field Dressing (within 2 hours of kill; critical in warm weather)

1. Position the carcass on its back with hind legs elevated.
2. Make a careful cut from the breastbone to the pelvis, just deep enough to open the abdomen without puncturing organs.
3. Reach in and carefully separate the trachea (windpipe) at the throat; grasp all organs and pull downward and out of the body cavity.
4. **Do NOT touch the contents of the stomach or intestines** (contamination risk).
5. Inspect the liver for spots or discoloration; if present, do not eat (disease indicator).
6. Separate edible organs (liver, heart, kidneys, tongue) into a clean cloth.
7. Cool the carcass immediately: prop open the body cavity to allow air circulation; if possible, move to shade or hang from a tree.

#### 2. Skinning & Butchering

1. Hang the carcass from hind legs if possible (allows gravity to assist).
2. Make cuts around all four legs just above the hooves.
3. Cut along the belly from sternum to tail.
4. Pull the hide away from the carcass, working from legs toward the spine. Use the knife to separate fascia only where necessary.
5. Remove the head (cut between the skull and first vertebra) if desired.
6. Butcher into major muscle groups: hind legs (hindquarters), front legs (shoulders), backstrap (along the spine), ribs.
7. Trim all white fascia, shot-damaged meat, and hair.

#### 3. Preservation (critical for large game)

- **Immediate consumption:** Stew, roast, or grind for patties. Cook to 160°F internal temp.
- **Jerky/drying:** Slice with the grain into strips ¼–½ in thick; hang in smoke for 24–48 hours.
- **Brining/salting:** Layer with salt and hang in a cool/dry location for 7–14 days. Requires cool storage (below 50°F) to prevent spoilage.
- **Smoking:** Cold-smoke at <90°F for 24–72 hours, or hot-smoke at 160–180°F for 6–12 hours for shelf stability.

## ⚠ MANDATORY PREPARATION

### COOK THOROUGHLY — ALWAYS

**Every wild fish and game animal MUST be cooked to eliminate parasites and bacteria:**

- **Fish:** Internal temperature ≥140°F (63°C), held for at least 15 seconds.
- **Small game (rabbits, hares, squirrels, birds):** Internal temperature ≥160°F (71°C).
- **Medium/large game (deer, boar, elk):** Internal temperature ≥160°F (71°C).
- **Check temperature in the thickest part of the meat**, away from bone.

Raw or undercooked wild game transmits *Trichinella*, tapeworms, and bacterial pathogens that cause severe illness or death.

### NEVER EAT (CROSS-LINK REGIONAL GAME-ANIMAL DISEASE BLOCKS)

**These parts harbor prions, parasites, and pathogens that cannot be cooked out:**

- **Brain, spinal cord, spinal fluid** — Prion disease (chronic wasting disease in deer/elk; Creutzfeldt-Jakob-like syndrome in humans). Incurable. NEVER consume.
- **Lymph nodes, bone marrow** — Elevated prion concentration in CWD-affected animals. Avoid.
- **Organs from visibly sick or injured animals** — If the liver is spotted, discolored, or enlarged; if the animal showed signs of disease (stumbling, excessive drooling, confusion); discard the entire carcass.
- **Digestive tract contents** — Bacteria from the stomach and intestines can contaminate the meat if the gut wall ruptures. Handle carefully; cook all exposed surfaces thoroughly.

**Regional cross-link:** The active region's game-animal disease index (in `../../regional/<biome>/game-animals/_index.md`) carries species-specific prion/parasite warnings (CWD in deer; trichinella in pigs; tularemia in rabbits). Read it before consuming any wild game.

## Failure Modes & Fixes

| Problem | Cause | Fix |
|---------|-------|-----|
| Meat tastes gamey/strong | Improper field dressing, organs not removed quickly | Must be done in field; remove organs within 1–2 hours; cool carcass rapidly after dressing |
| Meat spoils before cooking | Warm weather, delayed dressing, inadequate cooling | Process immediately after kill; hang in shade; bury in cool soil if necessary; cook or preserve within 8 hours (4 in summer) |
| Parasites visible in meat | Undercooked or inadequately processed | Cook thoroughly to recommended internal temp; avoid consuming organs unless from inspected animals; salt-cure or smoke-dry for long-term preservation |
| Jerky or dried meat molds | Moisture trapped; inadequate smoke/salt | Hang in direct smoke flow; ensure wood smoke (not just heat); increase salt concentration; store in dry, cool location |
| Preserved meat (smoked/salted) turns rancid | Fat oxidation; inadequate salt; warm storage | Use salt cure (≥1:3 ratio); smoke heavily; store cool (<50°F if possible); rewrap periodically to keep flies/insects off |
| Difficulty removing skin/feathers | Insufficient cooling after kill, adhesion too tight | Cool the carcass first; work on fresh kills; consider scalding in hot water (briefly) for birds before plucking |
