## Goal & When To Use

Use this when the store is out of reach or the power is out and you want to know how long the food in the house will last. You will count people, count calories, divide, and decide what to eat in what order. Rationing (cutting portions) is the last step, and only if the count says you must.

**Water comes first, and water is never rationed.** Ready.gov says to store at least one gallon of water per person per day, for drinking and sanitation, and "never ration drinking water unless ordered to do so by authorities" [4]. If supplies run low, drink the amount you need today and try to find more for tomorrow [4][5]. If officials set a water limit, follow it. Water sources and treatment: [`02_water.md`](../../../02_water.md) and [water-sourcing-and-storage.md](../../02_water/entries/water-sourcing-and-storage.md).

This entry is arithmetic and portions only. It never says a wild plant, mushroom or animal is edible. For "can I eat this?" go to [`regional/_index.md`](../../../regional/_index.md), then your region's food hub. Survey guide: [05_food.md](../../../05_food.md#plan-your-calories).

This is general information, not medical or dietitian advice. It gives no medicine advice. Questions about medicine and meals go to a pharmacist or clinician.

## Steps

### 1. List the people

Write down each person with age, and note anyone who is a baby, pregnant, breastfeeding, sick, elderly, or on a special diet. Ready.gov says to remember special dietary needs [3]. FEMA's brochure says people with special diets and allergies, babies, toddlers and elderly people need particular attention [5].

### 2. Pick a daily calorie figure for each person

- A general planning figure for an adult is about 2,000 calories a day. FDA says needs may be higher or lower depending on age, sex, height, weight and activity level [2].
- USDA's Dietary Guidelines table (2020-2025 edition) gives ranges. Each range runs from "sedentary" (only the activity of independent living) to "active" (walking more than 3 miles a day at 3 to 4 miles per hour on top of daily life) [1].

| Person | Calories per day, sedentary to active [1] (this entry's range across the Guidelines' age rows) |
|---|---|
| Girl or boy, age 2 to 3 | about 1,000 to 1,400 |
| Child age 4 to 8 | about 1,200 to 2,000 |
| Child age 9 to 13 | about 1,400 to 2,600 |
| Teen age 14 to 18 | about 1,800 to 3,200 |
| Adult woman, 19 and over | about 1,600 to 2,400 |
| Adult man, 19 and over | about 2,000 to 3,000 |

Where a range is wide, the low end is for a small, inactive person and the high end for a large, very active one. Hard work and cold push a person toward the high end (general practice).

Pregnancy and breastfeeding add calories. For women with a healthy prepregnancy weight the Guidelines list about +340 a day in the second trimester, +452 in the third, and +330 for the first six months of breastfeeding (+400 after that) [1]. Pregnant or breastfeeding women should ask their clinician about their own needs (general practice).

A newer edition exists: Dietary Guidelines for Americans, 2025-2030, released January 2026. Its text says calorie needs depend on age, sex, height, weight and activity level and uses a 2,000-calorie pattern as its example, but it has no age, sex and activity calorie table, so the table above stays from the 2020-2025 edition [10].

Babies are not in this table. A baby's food is breast milk or formula (see Step 7).

### 3. Count the calories you have

1. On each package, find "calories per serving" and "servings per container" [2].
2. Multiply the two numbers. A package may hold several servings, so eating two servings means roughly double the listed calories [2].
3. Add up all the packages. Leave out anything that needs a fridge or freezer you no longer have working.

### 4. Divide

Total calories, divided by the household's daily calories, equals days of food. Example (this entry's arithmetic): three people at 2,000 each eat 6,000 a day. A cupboard holding 36,000 calories lasts 6 days at full portions.

### 5. Eat in the right order

FEMA's order when the power goes off: first perishable and fridge food, then freezer food, then non-perishable pantry food [5]. Keep fridge and freezer doors shut while you plan. The keep-or-throw-out rules are in [`09_urban_survival.md`](../../../09_urban_survival.md#food-what-to-keep-and-what-to-throw-out) and [food-safety-during-outages.md](../../09_urban_survival/entries/food-safety-during-outages.md).

Choose foods that need no cooking, no water and no special preparation where you can [5]. Commercially canned food can be eaten out of the can without warming, so keep a manual can opener and utensils [3][5]. Do not eat from a can that is bulging, open or damaged, or that spurts liquid or foam when opened [9]. When in doubt, throw it out [9]. Never taste food to test it [9].

### 6. Ration only if the count says you must

What the sources say:

- FEMA's older brochure says food, unlike water, can be rationed, except for children and pregnant women. The brochure and the Southern Nevada Health District add that this holds if activity is reduced: healthy people can survive on half their usual food intake for an extended period [5][6].
- These are older statements. The brochure is an older FEMA, American Red Cross and USDA document, and it is the source this entry found for rationing [5]. No source opened for this entry gives a number of days or a percentage beyond that statement, so this entry gives none.

This entry's rules, which are more cautious than the minimum the sources allow:

1. **Do not cut portions for children, pregnant women, anyone who is sick, or anyone with diabetes** (this entry's rule; ask a clinician).
2. **Do not cut portions if you are cold or doing hard work.** Hard work and cold raise calorie needs (general practice).
3. **Cut only as much as the count requires, and only for resting healthy adults.** Example (this entry's arithmetic): two adults and a child, 24,000 calories left, 6,000 a day at full portions. The child eats a full portion and the resting adults cut about a quarter, so the household eats about 5,000 a day and the food lasts a little under 5 days (24,000 / 5,000 = 4.8).
4. **If you take medicine tied to meals, such as for diabetes, ask your clinician before cutting food, and do not change doses on your own.**
5. **If water is limited,** FEMA's brochure says to avoid salty foods and foods high in fat and protein, and to choose salt-free crackers, whole-grain cereals and canned foods with a lot of liquid [5].

### 7. Babies

- **Breast milk or ready-to-feed formula first.** CDC says ready-to-feed liquid formula is sterile, and powder is not, so ready-to-feed is the best choice for babies who are not getting breast milk. If a baby gets both, breastfeed first, then give formula [8]. Ready.gov also says to use ready-to-feed formula [3].
- **If you must mix powder: follow the label exactly.** Use the exact amount of water and formula listed on the container [7][8]. Never dilute formula and never stretch it (this entry's rule). CDC says adding too much or too little formula can make a baby sick [7]. Do not make or feed homemade formula [7].
- **Water for mixing:** CDC says use bottled water until local officials say tap water can be used; if there is no bottled water, treat the water by boiling or disinfecting [7]. Ready.gov says to use bottled water, with boiled water as a last resort [3]. Boil times and chemical doses are in [boil-disinfection.md](../../02_water/entries/boil-disinfection.md) and [`02_water.md`](../../../02_water.md).
- **Throw out leftover formula** after a feeding; do not save it [7].
- **Wash your hands with soap and water first** [7]. If bottles cannot be cleaned because clean water is short, CDC suggests feeding from a cup [7].

## Failure Modes & Fixes

| What goes wrong | Why it matters | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| No manual can opener | A shelf of cans you cannot open | Keep one with the food [3][5] |
| Pantry food eaten while fridge food spoils | Wasted calories and avoidable illness | Perishables first, then freezer, then pantry [5] |
| A child's or pregnant woman's portion is cut | The sources exempt them from rationing | Full portions for them; cut elsewhere or find more food [5][6] |
| Portions cut during cold or hard work | Calorie needs go up, not down | Keep portions up and rest more (general practice) |
| Formula watered down "to make it last" | Too little formula can make a baby sick [7] | Follow the label; use ready-to-feed if you can; ask a clinician or local officials where to get more |
| Counting food that was in a dead fridge | Spoiled food is not calories you can use | Count only food that passes the keep-or-throw-out rules (see [food-safety-during-outages.md](../../09_urban_survival/entries/food-safety-during-outages.md)) |
| Water rationed to save "for later" | Dehydration comes faster than hunger | Drink what you need today; find more tomorrow [4][5] |
| Salty, high-protein food with little water | Raises thirst [5] | Choose foods with liquid in them [5] |
