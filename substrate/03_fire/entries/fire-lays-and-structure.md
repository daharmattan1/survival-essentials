## Goal & When To Use

A fire lay is the **structure** that holds fuel and controls airflow. The lay you choose determines whether a flame bridges to full burn or dies mid-tinder. Use a **teepee lay** for speed in open conditions; a **log-cabin lay** for sustained, controlled burn with larger fuel; a **lean-to lay** to shelter from wind; and a **star lay** to conserve fuel over long hours. Choose before ignition — a lay built mid-burn collapses and wastes tinder.

---

## Steps

### 1. Prepare the Platform

- Clear a **3-foot diameter** down to bare soil or sand (or on rock in wet conditions).
- Remove leaves, moss, pine needles, and organic debris — they hold moisture and slow heat transfer.
- If the ground is wet, **lay green (live) branches crosswise** to elevate the fire ~4 inches, or build on dry rock/gravel.
- In sandy/ashy soil, scrape a shallow depression (2–3 inches) to stabilize the lay and contain embers.

### 2. Teepee Lay (Speed & Single-Handed Ignition)

**When to use:** calm to moderate wind, quick ignition, boiling water, emergency signals. Best for beginners.

- Gather tinder into a **fist-sized bundle**, fluffed so air passes through.
- Lay kindling (pencil-to-finger-thick twigs) in a **teepee cone** around the tinder: the leanest twigs on the bottom, leaning inward at ~45°.
- Leave a **wind-facing gap** (the opening into which you blow or face the lighter) large enough for your fist to reach the tinder core.
- Add heavier kindling (finger-to-wrist-thick) in concentric circles, maintaining the cone slope.
- Lay arm-thick fuel logs vertically around the outside, resting on the kindling cone. Leave the gap open.
- The burn pattern: flame rises → spreads horizontally through kindling → ignites fuel logs from inside.

**Failure mode:** if the cone collapses when you add fuel, you've stacked kindling too loosely. Rebuild with stiffer twigs and tighter stacking angles.

### 3. Log-Cabin Lay (Sustained Burn & Larger Fuel)

**When to use:** cold nights, unattended cooking, fuel is plentiful and large, you need 4+ hours of burn.

- Place two arm-to-leg-thick logs **parallel**, running East-West, ~6 inches apart.
- Lay two logs perpendicular (North-South) across them, creating a square frame.
- Repeat: two more logs East-West, two more North-South, building a cribbed-log wall 3–4 layers high. Logs should sit naturally in the groove of the layer below (not tied).
- At the center-bottom, leave a **hollow chamber** 4–6 inches tall — this is where tinder and kindling go.
- Build an inverted cone of kindling in the hollow, leaving gaps for airflow upward.
- Place tinder in the center, leaving a vent hole toward the prevailing wind.
- Light the tinder; flame rises through kindling into the log layers, which burn from inside-out.

**Advantage:** the log frame supports fuel weight; airflow comes up through the center, not across the top. Coals drop into the cavity and sustain the burn for hours.

**Failure mode:** if logs are stacked too tightly or tinder is wet, flames choke on lack of oxygen. Leave at least ½-inch gaps between logs; pre-dry tinder in your clothing.

### 4. Lean-To Lay (Wind Protection & Long-Duration Cooking)

**When to use:** strong wind, you need sustained flames for cooking or signaling, fuel is stick-sized.

- Drive a **green log** (or sturdy branch) 2–3 feet into the ground at a 45° angle, leaning away from the wind. Secure it firmly.
- Lay arm-thick logs **parallel to each other** against the angled pole, creating a lean-to shelter. Leave a gap at the bottom (6–8 inches) for airflow.
- Pack kindling tightly against the underside of the logs, forming an angled wall.
- Place tinder in a depression at the base of the kindling, facing the windward gap.
- Light the tinder; wind flows up through the bottom gap, across the tinder and kindling, and up through the fuel logs. The angled log roof reflects heat down.

**Advantage:** shields the flame from wind; reflected heat sustains the burn even in gusts.

**Failure mode:** if the angled pole is too steep or kindling is too dense, the air tunnel blocks and the fire smolders. Keep the lean angle at ~45° and leave gaps in kindling.

### 5. Star Lay (Fuel Economy & Night Watch)

**When to use:** fuel is scarce, you'll be awake tending the fire for 6+ hours, you want minimal waste.

- Arrange 4–6 arm-to-leg-thick logs like **wheel spokes**, radiating from a central point.
- Build a small teepee cone of kindling and tinder at the hub, where the logs meet.
- As the inner ends burn down, **slide each log inward** like a clock mechanism, keeping the burn zone at the center.
- No heavy setup, minimal fuel pile needed.

**Advantage:** you control burn rate by how fast you advance the logs. One person can tend it all night.

**Failure mode:** if logs are too thick and poorly dried, the center burns slowly and you spend all night pushing. Use well-seasoned wood; test by snapping a piece — it should break clean.

## Failure Modes & Fixes

| Problem | Likely Cause | Fix |
|---------|-----------|-----|
| Flame dies after 10 seconds | Tinder is wet; kindling has no airflow | Pre-dry tinder in your shirt. Rebuild: loosen kindling spacing; ensure a wind-facing gap. |
| Smoke but no flame in the tinder | Wind is quenching the ignition site | Shield the tinder with your body; use a lean-to lay; move to wind-protected location. |
| Kindling catches but doesn't ignite fuel | Gap between kindling and fuel logs is too large; fuel is damp | Reduce the gap to ½ inch; select dried fuel wood; lay smaller fuel first, graduate to larger logs. |
| Coals form but flames die when you step back | Airflow is blocked (too-dense tinder or wet kindling choking the draft) | Add small sticks between tinder and kindling to create ventilation tunnels; do not pack materials. |
| Logs roll off the lay when heavy fuel settles | Lay is unstable (especially lean-to or teepee) | For teepee: re-tighten the cone angle. For lean-to: ensure the angled pole is deeply seated and the logs rest securely. Wedge with rocks if needed. |
| Fire spreads unexpectedly in high wind | Embers escape and ignite nearby debris | Pre-clear a 4-foot diameter. Build in a natural depression (low ground) when possible. Never leave the fire unattended in wind. |

## ⚠ MANDATORY PREPARATION: Fire Safety Boundary

**Clearance (before lighting):**
- Clear a **4-foot radius** minimum around the lay down to bare soil. In high wind or very dry conditions, increase to 6 feet.
- Remove overhanging branches (at least 12 feet above the fire).
- Note the prevailing wind direction; position the fire so embers blow away from shelter and stored fuel.

**Ventilation (indoors, if applicable):**
- **NEVER use a fire for heat or cooking inside a closed tent, cave, or shelter without a chimney or vent.** Carbon monoxide accumulates invisibly; even a small fire in an enclosed space can kill in minutes.
- If you must have fire in a structure: cut or leave a vent hole at the top; position the fire away from the entrance to avoid drafts that reduce draw.
- **Monitor for dizziness, nausea, or headache** — signs of CO poisoning. Move into fresh air immediately.

**Unattended fire (the rule):**
- **Never leave a fire burning unattended**, even to "go get more wood" for 5 minutes.
- If you must step away, **extinguish it completely:** pour water on coals, stir the ashes, and ensure no heat remains (test with your hand 6 inches above).
- Embers can reignite hours later if unattended.

**Wildfire risk (dry seasons):**
- If there are burn bans or high-fire-danger warnings in your area, build only if it is genuinely a survival emergency (not convenience).
- Know local regulations before lighting any fire outdoors.
