## Goal & When To Use

Use this every time you light a fire, and again every time you leave it. Nearly 85 percent of US wildland fires are human-caused, and campfires left unattended are one cause [17]. A fire that was your rescue becomes a second emergency the moment it escapes.

This is general information, not legal advice or medical advice. Fire rules differ by place and week. Check the land manager.

> ⚠ **Fire goes OUTSIDE.** Never burn anything in a tent, vehicle, garage or house. Opening a flap or window does not fix this. See [the CO rule](../../../03_fire.md#fire-indoors-in-tents-and-in-vehicles-the-carbon-monoxide-rule).

## Steps

### 1. Is a fire allowed here?

Check before every outdoor fire, in the place you are standing. Rules change by land manager and by week [3][7][10]. Some bans cover stoves too, and penalties can run to thousands of dollars [9][10]. The examples below show what to look for.

- **Who to check with:** the national forest, national park, Bureau of Land Management (BLM) field office, or state forestry office that manages the land. Each forest and park posts alerts or restrictions on its own page [7][9][10].
- **What restrictions look like.** Forest Service "stages" vary. On the Tonto National Forest, Stage 1 bans building or using a fire or stove fire except in developed sites with provided rings, and allows devices fueled only by pressurized liquid petroleum or LPG that can be turned on and off, in cleared areas. Stage 2 bans fires even in developed sites. Its one exception is a stove or grill fueled only by pressurized liquid petroleum or LPG [9].
- **A current order, as an example:** the Tahoe National Forest order effective Oct 9, 2026 through Nov 30, 2026 allows campfires only in a charcoal grill or fire ring provided at listed recreation sites. A violation is punishable by a fine of not more than $5,000 for an individual, or up to 6 months in prison, or both. The only named exemptions are officers and organized rescue or firefighting forces on duty, and holders of one Forest Service permit. California Campfire Permit holders are not exempt. The order lists no survival or emergency exemption for the public [10].
- **A park example:** Voyageurs National Park bans campfires "including within designated fire rings" and charcoal fires, allows gas and propane camp stoves, and the order stays until rescinded [11].
- **BLM:** public lands in California are under year-round fire restrictions, and a campfire permit is needed outside a developed campground [12]. Its page lists restrictions by field office [12].
- **State law example:** Virginia bans open-air burning before 4 p.m. within 300 feet of woods or flammable vegetation from Feb 15 through Apr 30. A fully enclosed ring under a fine metal screen, attended, may be acceptable. The fine is up to $500. Even with precautions and permits, the person who started a fire that escapes may have to pay suppression costs [13]. In California, an escaped campfire is evidence the person was negligent, and a person whose negligence lets a fire escape is financially responsible for firefighting costs [14].
- **If a ban is on,** check whether it covers stoves too. Some orders exempt only pressurized-fuel stoves [9][10][11].

Lighting a fire to survive is your call. Do not assume the law will protect you (this entry's rule). More in the [burn-ban section of the guide](../../../03_fire.md#burn-bans-and-what-a-fire-can-cost-you).

### 2. Pick and prepare the site

- Flat, open overhead, no gusty winds, not an open field. At least 15 feet from tents, vegetation and low-hanging branches. Vertical clearance at least three times the flame height. A cleared area at least 10 feet across [2][1]. Sequoia's Campfire Tips gives a 5-foot cleared circle [6]; this entry uses the larger figure.
- Away from overhanging branches, steep slopes, rotten stumps, logs, dry grass and leaves [5][8].
- Not at the base of a hill. An escaped fire travels uphill fast. No fire on a windy day [6]. Strong winds and heavy fuel can turn a small campfire into a catastrophic wildfire [5].
- Dig to bare dirt. Duff (the layer of decomposing material under pine needles) burns, and lets even a small ember smolder for days, mostly underground and unnoticed [6].
- Rocks: the Forest Service says not to line the pit with rocks because they can explode when hot [6]. The National Park Service says to check under rocks around a fire for burning embers [4]. Smokey Bear says to circle the pit with rocks if you have no metal fire ring [2]. This entry follows the cautious choice: a ring of bare dirt, with rock only if dry and not porous (this entry's rule).
- Keep the fire small. Pile spare wood away from the fire [2][5].
- Keep water, a bucket or large container, and a shovel at the fire [2][4]. Put them there before you light it [2][7].
- Fire goes only where the manager allows. Use an existing fire ring rather than building a new one [7].

### 3. While it burns

- **Never leave a campfire unattended, even for a moment** [4]. The Forest Service adds: not "even for a few minutes or if you take a nap," and never leave children or young adults alone in charge [6].
- A responsible adult supervises it until it is completely out, or hands it to another [14]. Keep an eye on children and pets [1].
- Do not burn trash or dangerous things: aerosols, pressurized containers, batteries, glass or aluminum [2]. Never pour gasoline or any flammable liquid on a fire [20].

### 4. Put it out: drown, stir, drown, feel

1. **Drown.** Pour lots of water until the hissing stops, wetting all the embers. Dirt or sand alone will not put it out [1].
2. **Stir.** Use the shovel to mix water into the embers and dirt. Scrape sticks and logs until no embers are exposed or smoldering, not just the red ones [1].
3. **Drown again.** Keep adding water, dirt and sand until everything is cool [1].
4. **Feel.** Hover the back of your hand over the ashes. If you sense any heat, keep drowning and stirring [1][6]. Do not touch them until they are cool (this entry's rule). Make sure the ground under where the fire was is cool to the touch before you leave [20].
5. **Check under the rocks** around the ring [4], and look within 50 feet of the fire for sparks or embers that escaped [6].
6. "If your campfire is too hot to touch, it's too hot to leave" [2]. Whitish or gray coals can hold heat for hours and flare up if the wind gusts [3].
7. **Charcoal briquettes:** drown them in a pail of water, mix, then put them in the pit [6]. Never take charcoal or hot coals into a tent, vehicle or house for warmth: the CDC found a charcoal grill inside a tent in a camping death, and CPSC says never use charcoal for heat in tents, campers, vans, cars, trucks or homes [18][19]. See [`carbon-monoxide-poisoning`](../../06_first_aid/entries/carbon-monoxide-poisoning.md).

**With no water:** mix plenty of soil or sand into the embers and keep stirring until everything is cool to the touch [5]. That means stirring dirt in, not covering the fire with it.

**Never bury a fire.** "Do not bury your coals. They can smolder underground and possibly re-ignite" [5]. A buried fire "can smolder for hours and then escape" [6]. Do not just kick sand or dirt on it; that can bank the coals and keep them hot longer [3].

### 5. If the fire escapes

1. **If it has left the pit** (jumped to surrounding vegetation, or embers are landing outside the ring), **call 911 immediately** [2]. Say your location. If your fire gets out of control, note your location and call 911. If there is no cell service, tell the nearest park ranger or campground host [3].
2. **Get people away from the fire first** (general practice). A small flare-up you can reach with water or by throwing dirt on it is what the shovel is for [7][8]. Do not stay to fight a fire that is growing (general practice).
3. **Move away from the fire, not uphill from it** (this entry's rule, from the Forest Service fact that escaped fires run uphill fast [6]).
4. **Leave when told.** "Evacuate immediately if authorities tell you to do so." If trapped, call 911 and give your location, knowing a response can be delayed or impossible [16].
5. **Do not hold back from calling because of liability.** You may be liable for suppression costs and damages [6][13][14]. A life is worth the bill (this entry's rule).

### 6. If your clothing is on fire

1. **Stop.** Do not run [20].
2. **Drop** to the ground and **cover your face with your hands** [15].
3. **Roll** over and over, or back and forth, until the fire is out [15].
4. If you cannot drop and roll, or you are helping someone, smother the flames with a blanket or towel [21].
5. **Then treat the burn.** Call 911 for the triggers in the [guide's burns section](../../../03_fire.md#fire-and-your-body-burns-first-aid) and see [`burns`](../../06_first_aid/entries/burns.md). The guide covers what not to put on a burn (no ice, butter or oil).

## ⚠ MANDATORY PREPARATION

**These steps are never skipped:**

1. Check for a ban or restriction in the place you are, before lighting.
2. Water and a shovel next to the fire before you light it.
3. Site cleared to bare dirt, flat, open overhead, 15 feet from tents, vegetation and low branches.
4. Someone watches the fire the whole time it burns.
5. **Drown. Stir. Drown. Feel.** Do not leave until the ashes and the ground under them are cool.
6. **Never bury a fire.**
7. Fire outside only. Nothing burns in a tent, vehicle, garage or house.

## Failure Modes & Fixes

| What goes wrong | Why | Fix |
|-----------------|-----|-----|
| Fire restarts hours later | Embers left under rocks or in duff, or fire was buried | Check under rocks, dig to dirt next time, never bury [4][5][6] |
| Gray ashes still hot | Coals hold heat for hours | Keep drowning and stirring until the back of your hand feels no heat [3][1] |
| Kicked dirt "put it out" | It banks the coals | Spread the coals and stir dirt or water in until cool [3] |
| No water nearby | Not planned | Mix plenty of soil or sand into the embers and stir until cool [5]. Next time, bring water to the fire first |
| Wind picks up | Gust or change in weather | Put the fire out if you cannot keep it contained (this entry's rule, from the wind warnings in [5][6]) |
| Ban found out afterward | Rules not checked | Put the fire out. Use layers and shelter. Check stoves too |
| Embers land in grass or on a tent | Too close, too big | Keep 15 feet from tents; call 911 if it leaves the pit [1][2] |
| Wet or porous rock bursts | Trapped water | Do not use it. Check [`wet-weather-fire`](wet-weather-fire.md) |
| Someone's sleeve catches | Reaching over the fire, loose or synthetic clothing | Stop, drop, cover face, roll; then burn care [15] |
