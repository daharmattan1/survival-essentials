## Goal & When To Use

Use this when you need a fire (cold, wet, night coming, no heat at home) and everything around you is damp. Rain does not end the need for shelter and dry clothes first. Do those, then fire ([guide: warming a cold person](../../../03_fire.md#warming-a-cold-person), [Scenario A](../../../03_fire.md#worked-scenario-a-cold-rain-wet-wood-dusk)).

> ⚠ **Fire goes OUTSIDE. Never inside a tent, vehicle, garage, snow shelter or house.** Rain is when people are most tempted. Carbon monoxide (CO) cannot be seen or smelled. It kills sleepers and drinkers before they feel symptoms [6]. In Georgia in 1999, six people died in two zipped tents, one with a propane stove running and one with a charcoal grill brought in for warmth. The CDC wrote that opening tent flaps, doors or windows is not enough to stop CO building up [5]. The one exception is a stove or fireplace built to vent and kept in working order. See [the CO rule](../../../03_fire.md#fire-indoors-in-tents-and-in-vehicles-the-carbon-monoxide-rule).

The steps below add to [`tinder-and-fuel-progression`](tinder-and-fuel-progression.md), [`fire-lays-and-structure`](fire-lays-and-structure.md) and [`ignition-methods`](ignition-methods.md). They do not repeat them.

## Steps

### 1. Check first: is a fire allowed?

A ban stays in force until the land manager lifts it; one National Park Service order, for example, "will remain in effect until rescinded" [11]. Do not assume rain ends a ban; check the park, forest or land office. See [`extinguishing-and-fire-safety`](extinguishing-and-fire-safety.md) and the [guide on burn bans](../../../03_fire.md#burn-bans-and-what-a-fire-can-cost-you).

### 2. Find dry wood

- **Standing or leaning dead wood** is usually dry even in rainy climates, because its upright position sheds most rain. **Dead branches that snap** when broken are dry. Rotten wood lying on the ground smolders, is very hard to light, and is poor fuel [1].
- **Dead lower branches of conifers.** Small branches near the ground on the underside of conifers, dead from lack of sun, often hold all three kindling sizes [1]. Dead conifer needles also work as kindling [1].
- **Split to the dry core.** The inner wood of dead branches and small trees is the driest because weather does not reach it. Wood split to show its inner grain burns faster and hotter than wood still covered by bark [1]. The National Park Service says the same: bark does not burn as well as the rest of the wood, and thinner split pieces light faster [3].
- **How to split small pieces.** The Army method, for pieces under 3 inches thick and 1 to 2 feet long: set the piece upright on a sturdy base, rest a fixed-blade knife on top, and drive it down the grain by striking the knife tip with a forearm-sized branch. Pick wood with few knots [1]. Keep both hands clear of the blade's path (general practice).
- **Feather sticks.** Shave a dry stick into thin curls that stay attached [1].
- **Natural tinder the Army lists:** birch bark, shredded inner bark of some trees (cedar, chestnut, red elm), dead dry grass, ferns, moss, lichen, seed down (milkweed, cattail, thistle), and pitch wood scrapings [1]. Take bark from dead wood only (general practice).
- **Damp tinder:** store it between layers of clothing so body heat dries it before use [1].
- **Green wood** is a last resort: finely split and mixed evenly with dry, dead wood [1].
- **Never burn poison ivy or poison oak wood.** Burning and breathing the smoke can cause respiratory distress [1].
- **Collecting rules.** Wood may be collected from the area only "if the rules permit", and that is not allowed in many national parks [3]. Leave No Trace guidance is to use only sticks from the ground that can be broken by hand [9]. The Army advice above (standing dead wood, splitting) is survival technique. In a protected area, check the rule before you cut or break standing wood.

Keep what you gather under cover (a pack, a jacket, a tarp) until you light. The Army manual's advice is "keep the firewood dry" [2] (general practice for how).

### 3. Build a platform

- **Base and brace.** A base is a flat piece of wood or non-porous rock. It keeps moisture-prone tinder and kindling off wet ground and gives a clear spot to light. The brace, a forearm-sized stick, lies across the base to control air [1].
- **No wet or porous rock.** Porous rock holds water and can explode when heated [1][2]. This entry's rule: a bare dirt ring, rock only if dry and not porous ([site guide](../../../03_fire.md#pick-and-prepare-the-site)).
- **In deep snow:** lay green logs side by side, then one or two more layers, the top layer at right angles to the one below, wide enough for the whole fire [1][2].
- **Bare ground still matters.** Clear to bare dirt, a circle at least 10 feet across [4]; 3 feet of bare soil is the Army's minimum in an emergency [1]. That keeps a wet-looking duff layer from smoldering under you ([site guide](../../../03_fire.md#pick-and-prepare-the-site)).

### 4. Shield the fire without roofing it

- Choose a spot protected from the wind [1].
- Build a **fire wall (reflector)** if time allows: non-porous rock, or a stack of green logs, behind the fire. It directs heat, cuts wind and reduces flying sparks. Build it before the fire is hot [1][2].
- Do not roof the fire. The Forest Service wants a site open overhead, at least 15 feet from tents and low branches, with vertical clearance of at least three times the flame height [4]. A tarp or rain cover near a fire can melt or burn (general practice). If you hang rain cover, keep it well above and clear of the flame and sparks (this entry's rule, from that clearance).

### 5. Lay and light for wet conditions

- **Teepee lay.** Tinder and a few kindling sticks in a cone. As it burns, outer sticks fall inward and feed the fire. It "burns well even with wet wood" [1][2].
- **Light from the upwind side** [1][2]. Add kindling by the handful, with gaps for air. Laid on like a blanket, it smothers the flame [1].
- **Metal match.** Not affected by wet or dry conditions or cold. Good sparks burn hot and fast and can make up for some moisture in tinder [1].
- **Waterproof matches** in a waterproof container with a striker pad. Hold the head down and angled so the flame climbs the stick [1]. A butane lighter resists wind better than a liquid-fuel one. In extreme cold, keep it warm against your body [1].

### 6. Feed it

- Fuel does not have to be fully dry if the fire is well enough established to dry it. Wetter fuel makes more white smoke as the moisture burns off [1].
- Do not put wet or green fuel on young tinder or kindling. Wait for a strong flame and coals (see [`tinder-and-fuel-progression`](tinder-and-fuel-progression.md)).
- Spare wood goes away from the flames. The National Park Service suggests upwind of the fire [3]; Big Thicket says pile it away from the fire [8]. Damp pieces can dry near, not in, the fire [2] (the distance is general practice: a few feet, never touching the ring).
- **Drying clothes:** hold the back of your hand where the clothes would hang. If it stays comfortable for three seconds or more, the fabric is not too close. Polypropylene has a low melting point [1].
- Use only wood and natural fuel. Never pour gasoline or any flammable liquid on a fire [1].

### 7. Carry fire starters that survive rain

- **Two methods at least.** Every lighter and match is a limited supply; a metal match can start thousands of fires [1].
- **Matches:** waterproof, in a waterproof case with a striker. Dipping heads in melted wax helps [1].
- **Tinder:** cotton balls coated with a petroleum-based paste, lint, paper, solid fuel tabs [1]. The National Park Service names paraffin-soaked cotton balls and shredded paper [3]. Pack it in a waterproof pouch (general practice).
- **Char cloth:** cotton heated until black but not burned, kept in an airtight container [2]. Cool the tin before opening it, away from flame (see [`tinder-and-fuel-progression`](tinder-and-fuel-progression.md)).

## ⚠ MANDATORY PREPARATION

1. **Outside only.** No fire, stove, lantern, charcoal or heater inside a tent, vehicle, garage, snow shelter or house, and a flap, vent or cracked window does not fix it [5][6][7]. CPSC: do not use fuel-burning portable heaters or lanterns while sleeping in tents, campers or vehicles, and the risk is higher at altitude [7].
2. **Know the rule.** Check for a ban or restriction before lighting.
3. **Clear the site.** 10 feet of bare dirt, 15 feet from tents, brush and low branches, open overhead [4]. Water and a shovel next to the fire before you light it [4][10].
4. **Watch the cold person.** Shivering that will not stop, fumbling or confusion: sleepiness or confusion means call 911 (this entry's rule). See [warming a cold person](../../../03_fire.md#warming-a-cold-person).
5. **CO symptoms:** headache, dizziness, weakness, upset stomach, vomiting, chest pain, confusion [6]. Get everyone into fresh air first, then call 911 (see [`carbon-monoxide-poisoning`](../../06_first_aid/entries/carbon-monoxide-poisoning.md)).

## Failure Modes & Fixes

| What goes wrong | Why | Fix |
|-----------------|-----|-----|
| Tinder flares and dies | Damp or packed tight | Dry it between layers of clothing, fluff it, use at least a cupped handful [1] |
| Smoke, no flame | Kindling smothers the fire | Open gaps; add kindling by the handful [1] |
| Lit, then dead once you add wood | Fuel too wet or too big a step in size | Split to the dry core, add smaller pieces first, wait for coals [1] |
| Tinder soaks up water from the ground | No base | Put it on a flat piece of wood or non-porous rock [1] |
| Rock cracks or pops | Wet or porous rock | Stop. Use bare dirt or wood. Never use wet or porous rock [1][2] |
| Fire sags into the snow | No log base | Two layers of green logs, top layer crosswise [1] |
| Wet gathered wood everywhere | Standing dead wood not tried | Look for standing or leaning dead wood and dead low conifer branches [1] |
| Clothes scorch or melt | Too close | Three-second hand test; watch polypropylene [1] |
| You are tempted to bring the fire under the tent | Rain, cold, dark | Do not. Add layers and ground insulation instead; see [`emergency-shelter-types`](../../04_shelter/entries/emergency-shelter-types.md) |

**Reader's note.** Fire does not replace shelter and dry layers. If you cannot get a fire going in a few tries, stop wasting starters. Go back to [`insulation-and-ground-barrier`](../../04_shelter/entries/insulation-and-ground-barrier.md).
