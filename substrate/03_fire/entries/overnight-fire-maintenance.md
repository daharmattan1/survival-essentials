## Goal & When To Use

**The default is to put the fire out before you sleep or leave.** The Forest Service says to fully extinguish a campfire "before leaving or going to sleep" [7]. The National Park Service says if it is too hot to touch, do not leave or go to sleep [5]. Forest Service tips add "even for a few minutes or if you take a nap" [6].

Use this entry only when the choice is a night fire or freezing. That is this entry's rule, not an agency rule: the agencies above say to put the fire out before you sleep [5][7]. See also the [overnight section of the guide](../../../03_fire.md#keep-it-going-including-overnight). You take on a risk and may be responsible if the fire escapes (see [`extinguishing-and-fire-safety`](extinguishing-and-fire-safety.md)).

> ⚠ **Two rules that do not bend.**
>
> 1. **Nobody sleeps in an enclosed space (tent, vehicle, snow shelter, house) with any fire, stove, lantern, heater or charcoal burning in it.** Carbon monoxide (CO) cannot be seen or smelled, and people who are sleeping can die before they feel symptoms [10]. Opening tent flaps, doors or windows is not enough to stop CO building up [11]. CPSC says never use fuel-burning portable heaters or lanterns while sleeping in tents, campers or vehicles, and the risk is higher at altitude [12].
> 2. **A fire is never left alone.** Someone stays awake with it, in shifts.

Read the [guide's CO rule](../../../03_fire.md#fire-indoors-in-tents-and-in-vehicles-the-carbon-monoxide-rule) and [`carbon-monoxide-poisoning`](../../06_first_aid/entries/carbon-monoxide-poisoning.md).

## Steps

### 1. First try to stay warm without burning anything

The CDC's camping-death report recommends enough bedding and clothing to stay warm without fuel-burning devices, and eating and drinking more to cut hypothermia risk [11]. Use a better shelter, ground insulation, dry layers ([`insulation-and-ground-barrier`](../../04_shelter/entries/insulation-and-ground-barrier.md), [`emergency-shelter-types`](../../04_shelter/entries/emergency-shelter-types.md)). Build a reflector wall for any fire you do keep (step 3).

### 2. Check the rules and the weather

- Is a fire allowed here? Check bans and restrictions ([`extinguishing-and-fire-safety`](extinguishing-and-fire-safety.md)).
- No fire on a windy day [6]. Whitish or gray coals can retain heat for hours and flare up if the wind starts gusting [5]. In mountains, winds often reverse in the evening and morning, which can blow sparks and smoke toward a shelter [1]. If wind rises and you cannot keep the fire contained, put it out (this entry's rule, from the wind warnings in [6][8]).

### 3. Set up the site before dark

- Flat, open overhead, 10 feet cleared, at least 15 feet from tents, vegetation and low branches, vertical clearance at least three times the flame height [3][4]. Open overhead means nothing above the fire: no branches, no tarp edge, no rock ledge (this entry's reading of "open overhead").
- Keep the fire at least 15 feet from tent or tarp fabric. The Army manual puts a fire about 3 feet from a snow shelter [1]; this entry follows the Forest Service distance of 15 feet from tents [3][4].
- Dig to bare dirt, clear duff, no wet or porous rock [1][2]. See [`fire-lays-and-structure`](fire-lays-and-structure.md).
- **Reflector (fire wall).** A wall of non-porous rock or stacked logs, green wood being most effective, behind the fire on the side facing you. It directs heat in the direction you want and shields it from wind [1]. It also reduces flying sparks [2]. Build it before the fire is hot [1]. Never use wet or porous rock [2].
- Water and a shovel next to the fire [3][6][7].
- The Army's advice for a snow shelter is to set its opening 90 degrees to the prevailing wind, so a wind reversal is less likely to blow sparks and smoke in [1]. Use the same idea for any shelter (general practice).

### 4. Pick a lay that suits a night watch

- **Star lay:** logs like wheel spokes, inner ends in the fire. As the ends burn, pull each log closer. It conserves fuel, lets you burn big pieces, and "must be constantly tended." Hardwood works best [1]. See [`fire-lays-and-structure`](fire-lays-and-structure.md).
- **Pyramid lay:** layers of logs, each smaller than the last, with a starter fire on top, so it burns downward. The Army says it needs no attention during the night [1][2]. Forest Service and National Park Service rules say never leave a campfire unattended [6][7]. This entry follows the stricter rule: a pyramid saves effort, not the watch.
- Keep the fire small [3]. Add fuel only after the flame is steady.

### 5. Choose and stack fuel

- Gather before dark (general practice). Gather more than you think you need (general practice).
- Fuel should be at least forearm thick. Dry, split hardwood (oak, hickory, ash) gives more heat with less smoke than softwood (pine, spruce, fir). Softwood burns faster and smokes more [1].
- Stack split wood so the watcher can reach it without leaving the fire, but away from the flames. Pile extra firewood away from the fire [8]; the National Park Service suggests upwind [5]. The exact distance is general practice: far enough that a spark or flare-up cannot reach it.
- Damp pieces can dry near the fire [2]. Not in it, and not against the ring.

### 6. Set the watch

- One awake person at the fire at all times. A responsible adult supervises the fire until it is out, or hands it to another [9]. Never leave children or young adults alone in charge [6].
- Swap in short shifts so nobody nods off (general practice). The watcher stays at the fire, not in the shelter.
- At handover, say where the water, shovel and spare wood are, and what the plan is if the fire spreads (general practice).
- The watcher checks: sparks landing on fabric or gear, wind shift, logs rolling out, coals flaring, clothes too close. Hold the back of your hand where clothes would hang; if it stays comfortable for three seconds, the fabric is not too close. Polypropylene has a low melting point [1].
- If there is nobody to watch it, put it out [6][7].

### 7. Do not "bank" it under soil

The old Army manual lists "Bank the fire to keep the coals alive overnight" among its hints [2]. The agencies say the opposite for burying: "Do not bury your coals. They can smolder underground and possibly re-ignite" [8], and a buried fire "can smolder for hours and then escape" [6]. Kicking sand or dirt on a fire can bank the coals and keep them hot longer [5]. This entry follows the agencies: do not cover a fire with soil to keep it alive. Keep it small and tended.

### 8. At dawn, put it out

Drown, stir, drown, feel. Check within 50 feet for escaped embers [6]. Do not leave until the ashes and the ground under them are cool ([`extinguishing-and-fire-safety`](extinguishing-and-fire-safety.md); [guide](../../../03_fire.md#put-it-out)).

## ⚠ MANDATORY PREPARATION

1. **No flame, stove, lantern, heater or charcoal in any enclosed shelter, ever,** and no hot coals or charcoal grill carried in for warmth [10][11][12][13]. The CDC found a charcoal grill and a propane stove inside the tents in the Georgia deaths [11]. Army sources note that candles, fuel gel and small oil lamps also make CO [1].
2. **Fire site cleared, 15 feet from shelter fabric, open overhead, water and shovel at the fire.**
3. **A named watcher at all times.** No watcher, no fire.
4. **Dead out before anyone leaves.**
5. **CO symptoms on waking** (headache, dizziness, weakness, upset stomach, vomiting, chest pain, confusion [10]): get everyone into fresh air first, then call 911 (this entry's rule; symptoms from [10]). Symptoms are easy to mistake for a cold or flu [12].

## Failure Modes & Fixes

| What goes wrong | Why | Fix |
|-----------------|-----|-----|
| Watcher falls asleep | Long shift, warmth, tiredness | Short shifts. If no one can stay awake, put the fire out [6][7] |
| Sparks land on the tent or tarp | Too close, wind shift | 15 feet minimum, orient the opening, move or put the fire out [4][1] |
| Fire flares at 3 a.m. | Gray coals hold heat, then gust | Keep water and shovel at hand, stir and wet if it grows [5] |
| Logs roll out of the pit | Poor lay | Star lay, logs pushed inward [1]. Clear bare dirt around the fire, 10 feet across [3] |
| Fuel runs out | Too little gathered | Gather before dark. If you run out, let it burn down and put it out (drown, stir, feel) [4] |
| Damp wood stalls the fire | White smoke, little heat | Dry pieces near, not in, the fire; use drier dead wood [1][2] |
| Heat is tempting you to move the fire or a stove under cover | Cold | Do not. Add layers and insulation. Nothing burns under a tarp or in a shelter [10][11] |
| Someone wakes with a headache | Possible CO | Fresh air for everyone, then 911 |
| Embers found the next morning | Fire was banked under soil | Do not bank; drown, stir, feel [6][8] |
