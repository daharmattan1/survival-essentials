## Goal & When To Use

Some water cannot be fixed at home. This entry helps you spot it, stop, and switch to bottled or stored water. It also covers a private well that a flood has reached. Use it when:

- Water has come from a flood, a sewage overflow, or a spill.
- Water smells of fuel or chemicals, has an oily sheen, or looks like pea soup or green paint.
- Officials have issued a "do not drink" or "do not use" notice.
- You own a well and floodwater has reached it or come close.

Survey guide: [02_water.md](../../../02_water.md#never-drink). General sourcing and storage: [water-sourcing-and-storage.md](water-sourcing-and-storage.md). If someone has already swallowed it and is ill, call Poison Control at 1-800-222-1222 or 911 if they are in distress; see [dehydration-and-diarrhea.md](../../06_first_aid/entries/dehydration-and-diarrhea.md) for vomiting and diarrhea.

## ⚠ CONTAMINATION — what this method does and does NOT handle

> ⚠ **Floodwater, fuel, toxic chemicals, sewage and algal toxins cannot be made drinkable at home. Boiling, bleach, filters and UV do NOT fix chemical contamination. Do not drink it. Do not wash food in it. Use bottled or stored water.**
>
> - **Boil:** rolling boil 1 minute; 3 minutes above 5,000 ft or if you are not sure of your elevation (EPA 5,000 ft; CDC 6,500 ft). Kills bacteria, viruses and protozoa including *Cryptosporidium* and *Giardia*. CDC says you cannot make water containing fuel, toxic chemicals or radioactive materials usable by boiling or disinfecting it [2]. Boiling water that holds algal toxins "does not remove toxins and can concentrate the toxin" [8].
> - **Filter:** an absolute 1 micron or smaller filter removes parasites such as *Cryptosporidium* and *Giardia*; an absolute 0.3 micron or smaller filter removes bacteria and parasites. Filters do not remove viruses, and they do not remove chemicals or toxins.
> - **Chemical (bleach, iodine):** kills bacteria and viruses; does NOT reliably kill *Cryptosporidium*; does not fix chemicals or fuel. EPA: boiling or disinfection will not destroy contaminants "such as heavy metals, salts, and most other chemicals" [7].
> - **UV pen or lamp:** treats germs in clear water. It does not remove chemicals (see [uv-treatment.md](uv-treatment.md)).
> - **The boundary:** these methods work on germs. Floodwater and chemical spills carry chemicals and germs together. Nothing in this entry's home toolkit removes the chemicals. The answer is a different source.

## What is in floodwater and spills

CDC lists what floodwater can hold [1]:

- Human and livestock waste (sewage).
- Household, medical and industrial hazardous waste, including chemical, biological and radiological.
- Coal ash waste that can contain arsenic, chromium and mercury.
- Downed power lines, sharp debris and other germs.

CDC adds that eating or drinking anything contaminated by floodwater can cause diarrheal disease [1]. Contamination from fuel and toxic chemicals is a separate problem: CDC says water contaminated by fuel or toxic chemicals cannot be made usable by treatment, and that you should not drink or bathe in it [3].

Clear-looking floodwater is no better. You cannot tell by looking whether floodwater holds sewage or chemicals, so treat all of it as unusable (this entry's rule) [1].

## Steps

### A. Recognize it, then stop

1. **Floodwater, a chemical or fuel smell, an oily sheen, a "do not drink" notice, or a bloom like pea soup or green paint: stop.** Do not drink it. Boiling will not fix it [2][3][4][9].
2. **Do not use floodwater for drinking, or for washing or preparing food.** FDA says to use water from a trusted source for drinking and for washing or preparing food, and prefers bottled water that was not exposed to floodwater [10]. Dishes and teeth follow the same rule (this entry's rule); CDC's one-page water sheet lists washing dishes and brushing teeth among the uses to avoid when tap water may not be usable [12].
3. **Do not bathe in water that may hold sewage or toxic chemicals** [1]. Wash clothes that touched floodwater or sewage in hot water and detergent before reuse [1].
4. **Throw out food that touched floodwater,** including food in cardboard boxes, home-canned food, and food in damaged cans or packages that are not waterproof [10][11]. Undamaged all-metal cans are the one exception: see the "Stored water or cans sat in floodwater" row under Failure Modes & Fixes.
5. **Do not move propane tanks you find.** Call the fire department, police or your state fire marshal [1].

### B. Switch water

1. **Use sealed bottled water or water you stored before the event.** CDC says to use commercially bottled water during a "do not use" advisory [4].
2. **During an advisory, skip dispenser ice.** CDC says not to use water or ice from refrigerator or freezer dispensers during any drinking water advisory [4]. Under a "do not use" advisory, do not drink or use tap water for any purpose while it is in effect [4].
3. **Follow local officials.** CDC: listen for local information on how to use water to drink, cook or clean [1][4]. Wait until they say the notice is over [12]; do not decide on your own that it has ended (this entry's rule).
4. **If bottled or stored water runs short,** call your health department or local emergency management and ask where water is being supplied. Do not fall back on the flood source (this entry's rule).

### C. Algal bloom (pea soup or green paint)

1. **Do not drink from a water body with a suspected or posted bloom** [9].
2. **Backcountry filters and treatment devices do not destroy or remove the toxins** [9].
3. **Boiling can raise toxin levels** [8][9]. CDC: toxins may be present even when you cannot see a bloom [8]. Follow advisories and use bottled water for drinking and cooking when officials report toxins in your supply [8].

### D. A private well that flooding reached

A private well is one you own, not a city supply. After a flood, treat it as contaminated until a lab says otherwise.

1. **Stay away from the pump while the area is flooded,** because of the shock risk [5].
2. **Do not drink or wash with water from the flooded well** [5][6]. FDA says the well water should be tested and disinfected after the floodwater recedes [10].
3. **Get a well or pump contractor** to clean and disinfect the well before the pump is turned on [5]. If floodwater covered or entered the well, MDH says to have a licensed contractor inspect it first, and that running the pump to clear debris can ruin the pump [6].
4. **Disinfect and flush as the health department directs.** MDH describes disinfection with a chlorine solution but gives no amounts on its page and sends readers to a separate guide [6]. This entry gives no dose numbers: **call your state or local health department and use their method for your well.** EPA also tells well owners to disinfect and test after a flood, and to call the state or local health department [7].
5. **Test it.** After disinfecting and flushing out the chlorine, send a sample to a certified lab, found through your health department. MDH names coliform bacteria as the test [6]. Disinfection and testing may need to be repeated until the well tests clean [6].
6. **Water that runs clear is not proof.** EPA says to pump the well until the water runs clear after the pump is back on, and to ask the county or state health department or extension service if it does not [5]. Clear water can still hold germs or chemicals. Do not drink from the well until a lab result says so [6].
7. **Ask what else to test for.** The health department can say whether chemicals should be checked as well as bacteria. If a spill, farm field or fuel tank was near the well, say so (general practice).
8. **Until then, use bottled or stored water.** Boil only if your health department says to. Boiling does not remove chemicals [7].

## ⚠ MANDATORY PREPARATION

**Plan before a flood: you cannot fix this water after it arrives.**

- **Store water ahead:** at least 1 gallon per person per day for 3 days [14].
- **If you have a private well:** know your health department's number and a contractor you can call. MDH advises storing clean water before shutting off a well that may flood [6].
- **After a flood:** use bottled or stored water only, until local officials say the notice is over [4][12] or, for a well, a certified lab clears it [6].
- **Never treat chemical, fuel or floodwater as a boil-and-drink problem.** Boiling, bleach, filters and UV do not remove chemicals [2][7].

## Failure Modes & Fixes

| Failure | Cause | Recovery |
|---------|-------|----------|
| You boil water that smells of fuel | Boiling does not remove fuel or chemicals [2] | Do not drink it. Use bottled or stored water. |
| Floodwater looks clear | Looks do not show chemicals or germs | Do not use it. Treat all floodwater as unusable [1][10]. |
| Floodwater used to wash produce, dishes or a baby bottle | Floodwater on food and utensils [1][10] | Discard food that touched floodwater; baby nipples, pacifiers and wooden boards that touched it should be thrown out [11]. |
| Well water runs clear, so it gets used | Clear is not tested [5][6] | Wait for a clean lab result from a certified lab [6]. |
| You guess at a well chlorine dose | No dose is given on the pages opened for this entry | Call the health department for the amount and method for your well. |
| Algal bloom water boiled to "make it drinkable" | Boiling can raise toxin levels [8][9] | Use bottled water. |
| Stored water or cans sat in floodwater | Contact with floodwater | Water in a screw-cap, snap-lid or pull-top container that may have touched floodwater: throw it out and use other water. FDA says to discard any food or beverage not in a waterproof container if there is any chance of contact, and lists screw-caps and snap lids as not waterproof [10]. Floodwater can hold chemicals [1], and treatment does not purify water contaminated with fuel or toxic chemicals [13]. If your bottled water has an odor, do not drink or use it [10]. Damaged cans: throw out [10][11]. Undamaged all-metal cans can be rinsed and sanitized as FDA describes [10]. Empty containers that touched contaminants: clean and re-sanitize before reuse [13]; see [02_water.md](../../../02_water.md#storing-water). |
| You decide on your own that a notice is over ("the water looks fine") | Looks do not tell you; only the notice does (this entry's rule) | Go back to bottled or stored water. Wait until local officials say the notice is over [4][12]. |
