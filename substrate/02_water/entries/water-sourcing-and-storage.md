## Goal & When To Use

Effective water sourcing and storage prevents contamination before treatment and preserves treated water for later use. Use this method when:

- You must evaluate whether a water source is safe enough to treat (vs. abandon it).
- You need to store water in advance of emergency or for backup during treatment/boiling delays.
- Water source is turbid and requires pre-settling or pre-filtering before chemical/boiling treatment.
- You need to prevent recontamination after treatment.

---

## ⚠ CONTAMINATION — source hierarchy and pre-treatment requirements

> ⚠ **SOURCING HIERARCHY — Best to Worst**
>
> Water quality depends on source. Treat accordingly:
>
> 1. **Spring or groundwater (well, hand pump):** Lowest contamination risk. Direct treatment usually sufficient (single boil or chemical). Avoid if livestock visible or runoff flows near.
> 2. **Moving surface water (fast stream, river rapids):** Medium risk. Pre-filter or pre-settle before treatment. Avoid slow water or backflows.
> 3. **Still surface water (pond, lake, cistern):** Higher risk — livestock, wildlife, runoff concentrate here. Always pre-settle and pre-filter. Protozoan risk high.
> 4. **Stagnant water (swamp, puddle, ditches):** Highest risk. Pre-settle and pre-filter mandatory. May contain chemical/heavy-metal runoff; if it smells like fuel/chemicals, find a different source.
> 5. **Treated municipal water (after disruption, if available):** Use first. Boil if supply line integrity unknown.
>
> **Rule:** The dirtier the source, the more pre-treatment is required before chemical or boiling disinfection. Visible sediment or color MUST be removed before chemical or UV (SODIS) treatment.

## Steps

### Step 1: Source Evaluation

**Assess water quality before collection:**

1. **Look at the location:** Is it upstream of livestock, roads, waste, or dead animals? If yes, this is a poor source — find another if possible.

2. **Check for visible contamination:**
   - Clear water with no sediment → can treat directly (boil/chemical/filter).
   - Cloudy, brown, or reddish water → pre-settle or pre-filter required.
   - Water with visible algae blooms, foam, or slick on surface → avoid (may contain toxins).
   - Water that smells like fuel, chemicals, or sewage → avoid entirely; find a different source.

3. **Test for flow (if stream/river):** Fast water is safer than still water. Avoid backflows or pools in streams.

4. **Spring sources:** Often cleanest. Collect from outflow (not the pool below where animals drink). Springs rarely require pre-settling.

### Step 2: Pre-Filtering or Pre-Settling (if water is cloudy)

**Purpose:** Remove sediment and visible particulates before chemical/UV treatment (chemicals and UV fail on turbid water; filters strain debris to extend filter life).

**Method 1 — Pre-settling:**
- Pour water into a large clean container (pot, bucket, bag).
- Let it sit undisturbed for 30–60 minutes.
- Sediment will settle to the bottom.
- Carefully pour or siphon the clear water on top into your treatment container. Discard sediment.

**Method 2 — Pre-filtering:**
- Strain water through cloth, fine mesh, or a sand/charcoal multilayer filter.
- Pour cloudy water through the cloth into a collection container.
- Repeat 2–3 times if very muddy.
- Then treat the now-clearer water with boiling, chemical, or fine filter.

**Method 3 — Combined:**
- Pre-settle for 30 min, then pre-filter the clear top portion.
- This removes both large sediment and fine particles.

### Step 3: Collection

1. **Use a clean container.** Use a pot, bag, bucket, or bottle that has not held chemicals, fuel, or pesticides. If uncertain about a container, rinse with boiled water first.

2. **Collect from the cleanest point.** If a stream, collect from moving water, not stagnant pools. If a pond, collect from the center (farther from shore runoff) if possible.

3. **Collect only what you can treat/store.** Large volumes are heavy and hard to treat quickly. Collect 1–10 liters at a time depending on your treatment method.

4. **Cover during transport.** Use a cloth or bag to keep dust and debris out during the walk back.

### Step 4: Storage (before or after treatment)

**Storage containers (pre-treatment or treated water):**
- **Food-grade plastic buckets or bottles:** safest for water storage. Buy new or use only containers that held food/water.
- **Glass:** excellent; does not degrade or leach. Heavy but durable.
- **Metal (stainless steel, enamel-lined):** acceptable; does not leach. Avoid bare steel or cast iron (rust).
- **Do NOT use:** containers that held pesticides, fuel, paint, or chemicals — even if rinsed.

**Storage conditions:**
- **Cool and dark:** Store out of direct sunlight (sunlight degrades chlorine residual and promotes algae growth in untreated water).
- **Covered:** Use a lid or cloth to keep dust and insects out.
- **Labeled (optional but helpful):** Write the date treated water was prepared. Treated water stored without disinfectant residue should be consumed within 24–48 hours. Chlorine-treated water (with slight chlorine smell) lasts longer (1–2 weeks in cool, covered storage).

**Rotation (if stockpiling long-term):**
- Empty, rinse, and refill stored water every 6 months.
- Older water is not necessarily unsafe if stored covered in cool dark place, but rotation keeps supply fresh.
- If stored water develops an odor or color, discard and refill.

### Step 5: Prevention of Recontamination

**After treatment:**
- Cap bottles tightly or cover containers.
- Do not drink directly from a shared container (use cups to avoid cross-contamination).
- Do not place treated water on unwashed ground or surfaces.
- If treating a large batch, transfer portions to sealed containers for personal use.

## Failure Modes & Fixes

| Failure | Cause | Recovery |
|---------|-------|----------|
| No water source visible (desert, cave) | Geography or season | Collect morning dew (cloth on grass, wring into container); look for underground seeps; melt ice/snow if available; seek lower elevation. Prioritize sourcing over treatment—no water to treat is fatal. |
| Source is contaminated but only option | Limited options (surrounded area) | Pre-settle + pre-filter, then boil (most reliable single method). Avoid chemical treatment alone (unreliable for Crypto/turbidity). Boiling kills all pathogens even if water is not perfectly clear. |
| Water stays cloudy after pre-settling (30+ min) | Sediment is very fine (silt/clay) | Use pre-filter (cloth, sand filter) instead. Or extend settling to 2+ hours, then siphon *very* carefully. Fine sediment requires mechanical filtration to fully remove. |
| No clean container available | Only have contaminated or unknown containers | Rinse container with boiled water or fill with treated water first, slosh to rinse, discard, refill. If very suspicious, skip storage — treat and drink immediately. |
| Stored water develops smell or color | Algae growth (sunlit storage) or contamination | Discard. Retrieve fresh water and store in covered, cool, dark location. If water must be stored >1 week, ensure it is chlorinated (slight chlorine smell after treatment indicates residual protection). |
| No time to pre-settle cloudy water | Emergency situation, must drink immediately | Boil (works on cloudy water; boiling does not require clarity). If boiling impossible, pre-filter through cloth, then use chemical with doubled dose and 4-hour wait, then drink sediment-free top portion. Filter alone on very cloudy water is risky. |
| Treated water tastes like chlorine or iodine | Normal residual from chemical treatment | Taste indicates disinfectant is present (protecting from recontamination). Acceptable. To reduce taste: let water sit uncovered for 30 min to allow gas to escape, or add lemon juice/vitamin C. |
