## Goal & When To Use

Use this entry to decide where emergency water can come from, what you must never drink, and how to store water so it is not recontaminated. Use it when:

- Tap water is off or under a notice and you need to find water.
- You are outdoors and must pick the least-bad source to treat.
- You are stockpiling water ahead of time.
- Water is cloudy and needs clearing before treatment.

**Every outdoor source needs treatment.** Treat the water with one of the treatment entries before drinking. A source that smells of fuel or chemicals cannot be treated.

---

## ⚠ CONTAMINATION — what sourcing does and does NOT handle

> ⚠ **Choosing a better source lowers risk. It does not make water drinkable. Treat all outdoor water. Never drink floodwater, pool or spa water, or anything with fuel or chemicals in it. Boiling and chemicals do not fix chemical contamination.**
>
> - **Boil:** rolling boil 1 minute (CDC: 3 minutes above 6,500 ft / 2,000 m; EPA: 3 minutes above 5,000 ft / about 1,500 m; if unsure, 3 minutes). Kills bacteria, viruses and protozoa including *Cryptosporidium* and *Giardia*. Does NOT remove chemicals.
> - **Filter:** absolute 1 µm or smaller removes protozoa; absolute 0.3 µm or smaller removes bacteria and protozoa. Most portable filters do NOT remove viruses, so add a disinfectant after filtering.
> - **Chemical (bleach, iodine):** kills bacteria and viruses in clear water; does NOT reliably kill *Cryptosporidium*. Longer wait in cold water. Iodine: not in pregnancy, with thyroid problems, or for more than a few weeks.
> - **The boundary:** no single method covers chemical, bacterial, viral and protozoan threats at once. If chemicals are possible, pick a different source.

### Outdoor sources, best first (CDC; EPA)

1. **A spring, at the point where it comes out of the ground.**
2. **Moving water,** such as a stream. EPA: it is generally better to use flowing water than still, stagnant water.
3. **Still water** such as a pond or lake. Avoid scum, and anything near livestock, camps, roads or sewage.

**Treat all of it.** CDC: rivers, streams and lakes "might be contaminated" with livestock waste, sewage and chemicals. Rain can be collected, then treated. During floods, well water might also be contaminated (CDC); a flooded private well needs disinfection and testing before use (EPA). **Do not use water with floating material, a questionable odor, or a dark color** (EPA).

**Rule:** the dirtier the source, the more clearing it needs before treatment. Particles block UV and sunlight, soak up chlorine and clog filters.

### At-home sources, best first (CDC; Ready.gov; Regional Water Providers Consortium)

- **Sealed commercial bottled water,** stored in its original container. CDC calls unopened bottled water the most reliable source.
- **Water heater tank** (the tank on the faucet side, not the home heating system). See "Draining a water heater" below.
- **Water in the pipes.** Shut the main valve first so contaminants cannot flow in. Open the highest faucet to let air in, then draw from the lowest faucet.
- **Melted ice cubes,** if the ice was made from uncontaminated water.
- **Liquid from canned fruit and vegetables.** Do not use cans or bottles that sat in floodwater.
- **Toilet tank, last resort only.** Never the bowl. Only if the tank water is clear and has no colored or chemical toilet cleaner in it, and treat it before drinking (CDC; OSU Extension). Some agencies advise against drinking it at all (Regional Water Providers Consortium lists toilet water as not for drinking).

### Never drink (CDC)

- **Floodwater.** It can hold sewage, livestock waste, hazardous chemicals and coal ash. Eating or drinking anything contaminated by floodwater can cause diarrheal disease. Do not drink it even if treated (Regional Water Providers Consortium).
- **Pool or spa water.** CDC: use it for hygiene and cleaning only, not drinking.
- **Anything with fuel or toxic chemicals in it.** CDC: it cannot be made drinkable.
- **Water from the home heating system** (radiator, boiler).
- Water that smells of fuel or chemicals, has an oily sheen, or has a scum or algae bloom.

## Steps

### Step 1: Source evaluation

1. **Look at the location.** Is it near livestock, roads, waste, camps or dead animals? If so, find a better one.
2. **Check what you can see and smell.**
   - Fuel or chemical smell, oily sheen, bloom, foam or scum: do not use it (CDC; EPA).
   - Floating material or a dark color: do not use it (EPA).
   - Cloudy water: clear it first (Step 2).
3. **Prefer moving water to still water** (EPA). Collect from a spring at the point where it emerges.
4. **Assume it needs treatment.** Looks and smell do not show whether water has germs.

### Step 2: Clear cloudy water

1. Let it sit still about an hour, until sediment forms on the bottom.
2. Pour or siphon off the clear top.
3. Strain that through a clean cloth, paper towel or coffee filter (CDC; EPA; WMS 2024).

This removes particles only. It does not treat the water. Homemade sand, gravel, cloth and charcoal filters clear particles and cut some germs, but the result still needs treatment.

### Step 3: Collect

1. **Use a clean container.** Never use one that held bleach, pesticides, fuel or other toxic chemicals (CDC).
2. **Collect from the cleanest point,** not shoreline mud or stagnant pools.
3. **Cover it** during transport.

### Step 4: Store

- **Containers.** Food-grade water containers are best; they do not transfer toxic substances into the water (CDC). If you cannot get one, choose a container with a tight lid made of durable, **unbreakable material (not glass)**, ideally with a narrow neck (CDC). Do not reuse containers that held bleach, pesticides or other toxic chemicals (CDC).
- **Sanitize before filling.** Wash with soap and rinse completely. Then add a solution of 1 teaspoon of unscented household bleach per quart of water, cover and shake so it touches all inside surfaces, wait at least 30 seconds, pour it out, and air-dry (CDC; Ready.gov).
- **Label and place.** Write "drinking water" and the date on each container. Store at 50 to 70 °F (10 to 21 °C), out of direct sunlight, away from gasoline and pesticides (CDC).
- **Quantity.** At least 1 gallon (about 4 liters) per person per day for at least 3 days, and 2 weeks if possible. Store more for pregnancy, illness, pets and hot weather (CDC; Ready.gov).
- **Replace water you filled yourself every 6 months** (CDC; Ready.gov). Replace commercially bottled water by its expiration date (CDC).
- **If stored water smells, looks off, or touched floodwater,** do not drink it as is. Treat it again or replace it.

### Step 5: Prevent recontamination

- Keep stored and treated water in clean, covered containers (EPA; CDC).
- Pour into a cup. Do not drink from a shared container.
- Do not set treated water on dirty ground.
- Do not add vitamin C or other taste removers to water you plan to store, because they remove the disinfectant that protects it (WMS 2024).

## Draining a water heater (Regional Water Providers Consortium; OSU Extension)

A tank holds roughly 30 to 80 gallons. A tankless heater holds none. Use only the tank that feeds your faucets, never the home heating system.

1. **Turn off the power first.** Electric: switch off the breaker. Gas: turn the knob to the **pilot** setting; do not turn it completely off.
2. **Close the cold-water supply valve** (usually on top of the tank) so mains water cannot flow in.
3. **Let air in.** Open the relief valve on the side of the tank, or open a hot tap in the house.
4. **Open the drain valve at the bottom** and fill a container. A garden hose helps. **The water may be very hot.** Wear gloves and safety glasses.
5. **Never turn the power or gas back on while the tank is empty** (OSU Extension). Wait until the tank has refilled.
6. **Treat the water** if the mains may have been contaminated. Use heater water as a short-term source only. Metals and other contaminants can dissolve into water at heater temperatures (WMS 2024).

## Failure Modes & Fixes

| Failure | Cause | Recovery |
|---------|-------|----------|
| No source in sight | Dry area or season | Collect rain and treat it (CDC). Look for a spring. |
| Only source is questionable surface water | Limited options | Pick the best available (spring, then moving water, then still water). Clear it, then boil. Add a filter or disinfectant if you cannot boil. Do not drink it if it smells of fuel or chemicals. |
| Water stays cloudy after settling | Very fine silt or clay | Strain it through a coffee filter or tightly woven cloth. If it is still tea-colored, count it as cloudy and double the bleach dose (see the chemical entry). |
| No clean container | Only unknown or contaminated containers | Do not use a container that held chemicals. Wash and sanitize one as in Step 4. |
| Stored water smells or looks off | Contamination or growth | Do not drink it as is. Treat it again or replace it. |
| Treated water tastes of chlorine or iodine | Normal after chemical treatment | Pour back and forth between clean containers and let it stand a few hours (EPA). Or add a pinch of vitamin C just before drinking, never to stored water (WMS 2024). |
| Source may have chemicals or floodwater in it | Fuel, chemical or flood contamination | Do not drink it. Boiling and chemicals will not fix it. Use bottled water or another source and contact your health department (CDC). |
