## Goal & When To Use

Decide whether to cross moving water on foot, and cross with the least risk if you truly must. **Turning back is the default.** The Park Service says your best option may be turning back [1], and Great Smoky Mountains National Park says it is better to turn back or wait for flooding streams to recede than risk your life [4].

If you are lost, none of this applies yet. Stay put [9]. The Forest Service calls following a stream downhill "a very last resort" [9], and Olympic National Park calls rivers and creeks often the most dangerous routes there [3]. Do not enter a river to find your way out. The survey guide covers this: [`07_navigation.md`](../../../07_navigation.md#rivers-and-streams-following-and-crossing).

General information, not medical advice. If someone is swept away, hurt, or shows signs of dangerous cold, call 911 if you can reach it.

## ⚠ WHEN NOT TO CROSS

> ⚠ **MOVING WATER KILLS FAST. Do not cross if ANY of these is true.**
>
> - The water would come **above your knees** [1].
> - You are **not comfortable** crossing, for any reason [2].
> - You are **above rapids or falls** [2].
> - The stream or river is **rising**, **muddy with floating debris**, or **it has rained hard upstream**, even if it is dry where you stand. Rain far upstream can cause flooding where no rain falls [4][8]. (Rising water is the sign; the debris and muddy-water cues are this entry's rule.)
> - It is **a flood**. The Forest Service says not to attempt to cross flowing streams in a flood [8]. The Weather Service says it is never safe to drive or walk into floodwaters [6].
> - It is the **afternoon or evening** and the stream is fed by snowmelt or a glacier. Levels rise then [2]. Olympic National Park advises crossing in the morning, before snowmelt swells the water [3].
> - You are **cold, tired, or carrying a heavy load** and have a safe way not to cross (this entry's rule).
>
> **Knee depth is the most, not a green light.** The Weather Service says 6 inches of fast-moving flood water can knock an adult off their feet [5][6]. Yukon-Charley says the depth and the speed of the water carry the greatest risk of injury or death [2]. If it is shallow but fast, it can still knock you down.
>
> **Verdict:** turn back, wait, or find another route. The Park Service says to be ready to spend an extra hour or even a day finding a safe crossing [1].

## Why it kills

- **Force, not just depth.** Per the Weather Service, 6 inches of fast-moving water can knock you off your feet [5][6]. It also says 12 inches of water can float a car or small SUV [5], and 12 inches of rushing water can carry away most cars [6]. People underestimate the power of water [6].
- **Cold shock.** Sudden immersion in cold water can cause involuntary gasping and rapid breathing in the first 2 to 3 minutes or more. It can be as severe at 50 to 60 F as at colder temperatures. Muscle control can fail, which makes self-rescue or staying afloat without help almost impossible. Hypothermia (a dangerously low body temperature) begins at a core temperature of 95 F [7]. Great Smoky Mountains National Park says exposure to cold water can lead to hypothermia in minutes [4].
- **Traps.** Most drownings in that park come from a leg or ankle caught in an underwater rock ledge or between boulders [4]. A **strainer** is a downed tree or other debris that lets water through but traps people, and the Park Service lists strainers among the most dangerous river features [1].
- **Rising water.** Levels can change by as much as a foot from morning to late afternoon [1]. Snow and ice melt as the day warms, so water is usually higher in the afternoon and evening and lower overnight and early morning [2].

## Steps

### Part A: Before you decide

1. **Stop and look.** Do not step in because the far side is close.
2. **Check what is upstream.** Look at the sky and listen. Thunderstorms and lightning upstream can raise the water quickly [8]. Watch for rising water [8].
3. **Check a gauge if you can.** The USGS runs about 7,000 streamgages, and its WaterAlert service can notify you when flow is too high or too low to fish or kayak safely [10]. This only helps if you have signal and the stream has a gauge (general practice).
4. **Judge the speed and depth.** Yukon-Charley says the depth and the speed of the water pose the greatest risk [2]. If the water is fast, or logs and debris are moving in it, do not cross (this entry's rule).
5. **Decide: turn back, wait, or look for a better spot.** Turning back, or waiting until the water drops, is always an option [1][4]. Stop here unless something threatens your life right now, such as fire or a rising flood behind you.

### Part B: Scout for the best place

6. **Look downstream first.** The Park Service says to scout downstream for log jams, waterfalls and other hazards that could trap you, and to pick an exit point where you can get out if you fall in [1].
7. **Pick the widest, shallowest part.** Look for where the river braids (splits into several channels) and cross at the widest part [1][3]. Choose the gentlest section [2]. Never cross above rapids or falls [2].
8. **Time it.** Cross early in the morning, when levels are typically lowest [1][3].
9. **Protect your warmth.** Before you cross, make sure your dry fire-starting gear and warm clothing stay dry [2].

### Part C: Prepare yourself

10. **Unbuckle the pack's hip belt [1][3] and chest (sternum) strap [2]** so you can drop the pack fast. A heavy pack does not float and can snag on river obstacles [2].
11. **Keep boots or shoes on** to protect your feet [2].
12. **Take a pole or stout stick.** Keep two points of contact with the ground at all times [1]. Trekking poles or a staff add stability [2].
13. **Do not tie yourself to anything.** Olympic National Park says never to tie yourself in while you cross [3]. No opened source teaches a rope crossing, so do not improvise one (this entry's rule).

### Part D: Cross

14. **Face upstream** and look forward as much as you can [1]. Angle your travel across the current, as Olympic National Park puts it [3].
15. **Take short, solid steps** [2]. Test each foothold before you put weight on it (general practice). Be careful of eddies and of deep drop-offs or holes the current has cut [2].
16. **If the bottom drops away,** turn back the way you came [2].
17. **In a group,** stand in a line with your hands on the shoulders of the person ahead, with the strongest person on the upstream side. Stay out of the fall line of others, so a fall does not take them with you [2]. Link arms or use shoulders, not packs: the packs must be free to drop (this entry's rule).

### Part E: If you fall or are swept off your feet

18. **Do not try to stand up** in fast water [4]. Float on your back with your feet pointing downstream [4].
19. **Drop the pack** if it is dragging you.
20. **Head for your exit point** (Step 6) or the nearest slow edge (general practice).

### Part F: After you cross

21. **You are wet.** Get out of the wind, add dry layers, and go to the cold-injury steps: [`04_shelter.md`](../../../04_shelter.md#stop-heat-loss-ground-wet-and-wind) and [`04_shelter.md`](../../../04_shelter.md#someone-is-too-cold-hypothermia-and-frostbite). Shivering, confusion or drowsiness: call 911 if you can.

## Floodwater: turn around, don't drown

- **Do not walk or drive into floodwater.** The Weather Service's message: "It is NEVER safe to drive or walk into flood waters" [6].
- **Do not drive around a barrier** blocking a flooded road. The road may have collapsed under the water [6].
- Over half of flood-related drownings happen when a vehicle is driven into floodwater, and the next largest share involves walking into or near it [6].
- **Camping low?** Get to higher ground immediately [5]. Do not camp or park beside streams or rivers in threatening weather [8]. See [`site-selection-and-hazards.md`](../../04_shelter/entries/site-selection-and-hazards.md).
- **Trapped by moving water:** move to the highest possible point and call 911 if possible [5].
- Depth figures differ a little between Weather Service pages: one says 12 inches can float a car or small SUV and 18 inches can carry away large vehicles [5]; another says 12 inches of rushing water can carry away most cars and 2 feet can carry away SUVs and trucks [6]. Either way, a foot of moving water is enough to lose a car.

## Failure Modes & Fixes

| Failure | Why it happens | Fix |
|---------|----------------|-----|
| "It is only knee deep, so it is fine" | Speed matters as much as depth [2][5] | Judge the current. If you cannot stand firm, do not go. |
| You start crossing late in the day | Snowmelt and glacier flow rise in the afternoon and evening [2][3] | Camp, cross early next morning. |
| Pack straps still buckled, you fall | A heavy pack does not float and can snag [2] | Unbuckle hip belt and sternum strap before the first step [1][2]. |
| You are tied to a rope or partner and are pulled under | Rope in a current can trap you; sources say never tie in [3] | Do not tie in. Link arms or shoulders only. |
| Foot wedged between rocks | Leg or ankle caught underwater [4] | Move your feet deliberately. If you fall, do not try to stand; float on your back, feet downstream [4]. |
| The bottom suddenly drops | Hole or deep drop-off [2] | Go back the way you came [2]. |
| Cold hits you after you cross | Wet clothes and cold water [7] | Dry layers, shelter, warmth. See the shelter guide links in Part F. |
| You cross a stream you could have avoided | You were lost and wanted to follow water out | Stay put [9]. Do not follow a stream unless it is a very last resort [9]. |
| Water rose while you were on the far bank | Rain upstream, snowmelt [4][8] | Wait for it to fall. You are not stuck; plan for a wait with shelter and food. |
