## Goal & When To Use

Terrain reading means **relating yourself to the land using contours, slopes, and visible landmarks.** Use this to:

- Confirm your position before moving.
- Identify "handrails" (terrain features you can follow to stay on course).
- Spot "catching features" (unmissable landmarks you cannot overshoot).
- Orient a map without a compass.
- Estimate distance by counting terrain folds (contours).

This is the second layer of the navigation standard rule: *know where you are before you move; pick a bearing to a feature you can't overshoot.*

---

## Steps

### Step 1: Read the Contour Lines

A topographic map uses contour lines to show elevation. Each line connects points at the same elevation.

1. **Contour interval (CI):** The vertical distance between consecutive lines. Usually printed on the map (e.g., CI = 40 ft, or 20 m).
2. **Line spacing:** Closely spaced lines = steep ground. Widely spaced lines = gentle slope.
3. **Index contours:** Every 5th line is thicker and labeled with elevation. Count up or down from an index line to find unknown elevations.
4. **Counting contours to estimate distance:** On a 1:24,000 map (1 inch = 2,000 feet), each contour represents ~40 vertical feet (CI 40). A mile of horizontal distance on gentle terrain (30° slope) involves roughly 25–30 contours. **Do NOT count contours for absolute distance; use them to assess climbing effort.**

### Step 2: Identify Terrain Features and Landforms

Learn to spot these shapes on a map; they correspond to real features on the ground.

| Feature | Map Pattern | Real World | Navigation Use |
|---------|---|---|---|
| **Ridge** | Contours form a "V" pointing downhill | High ground, elevated line of terrain | Follow ridgelines to avoid valleys; good visibility |
| **Valley / Drainage** | Contours form a "U" or "V" pointing uphill | Creeks, ravines, draws | Follow drainages downhill (they lead to water); contours funnel walkers here |
| **Spur** | Contours protrude outward from a ridge | Small ridge jutting downhill | Spurs block sidhill movement; go around them |
| **Saddle / Pass** | Contours form a narrowed hourglass (two ridges near each other) | Low point between two hills | Natural crossing point; easier than contouring the ridges |
| **Steep slope** | Tightly bunched contours | Cliffs, talus, very steep ground | Avoid for foot travel unless necessary; assess before committing |
| **Gentle slope** | Widely spaced contours | Open meadow, gradual hillside | Easier movement; better visibility for map-to-terrain matching |

### Step 3: Use Handrails and Catching Features

**Handrails** guide you along a straight line without you having to navigate.

1. **Find a linear feature:** A ridgeline, creek, power line, road, or ridge that runs near your route.
2. **Follow it.** You do not have to measure bearing or distance constantly; the feature keeps you on course.
3. **Handrails are most powerful when they run perpendicular to your intended direction of travel.** A creek running north-south is a handrail if you're traveling east; if it runs east-west, it's not helpful.

**Catching features** are unmissable terrain landmarks that mark the boundary of your objective.

1. **Define a large feature** you cannot miss (a mountain peak, a major river, the crest of a ridge, a road).
2. **If you wander left or right, you hit the catching feature** and know to correct your bearing.
3. **Example:** Targeting a small village in a valley. The ridge above the valley is a catching feature — if you climb to the ridge, you went too far uphill and can descend toward the village.

### Step 4: Terrain Association (Map-to-Land Matching)

Relate the contours you see on the map to the terrain in front of you.

1. **Pick a known feature** on the map (a peak, saddle, ridge junction).
2. **Locate it on the ground** visually.
3. **Orient the map** (rotate it so contour lines and ridges align with the terrain you see).
4. **Estimate your location** by triangulation: sight from two known features, find where lines from those sightings intersect on the map — that's you.

**Steps for simple terrain association:**
- Stand on a ridge or high ground where you can see distant features.
- Find three distant, identifiable features (peaks, buildings, distinctive ridges).
- Lay the map flat. Rotate it until the contour lines of known terrain align with the real terrain in front of you.
- Draw a line on the map from each feature toward you. Where the lines intersect is your position.

### Step 5: Aspect (Direction a Slope Faces)

A slope's aspect is the compass direction it faces (north-facing, south-facing, etc.). Aspect matters for:

- **Snow stability:** North-facing slopes in the Northern Hemisphere hold snow longer; south-facing slopes melt faster.
- **Navigation in fog:** Aspect helps you stay on course when visibility is zero. Count contours to gain/lose elevation, then check aspect to confirm direction.
- **Plant growth:** South-facing slopes are drier and more open; north-facing slopes are lusher and have thicker brush.

**How to read aspect from a map:**
- A slope faces the direction the contour lines "point" (their concave side).
- If contours form a "U" opening to the north, the slope is north-facing.

### Step 6: Distance Estimation Using Terrain

No GPS; no precise rangefinder. Estimate distance by terrain and time.

1. **Measure on the map:** Use your hand span or a stick to estimate straight-line distance.
2. **Adjust for terrain:** Steep uphill is slower (2 mph); gentle downhill is faster (4 mph); level terrain is medium (3 mph). Offset by obstacles (brush, rock, water).
3. **Estimate time:** Distance ÷ speed = travel time. A 6-mile flat walk at 3 mph takes 2 hours.
4. **Reality-check:** If the terrain is steep or brushy, your actual pace is half the estimate.

## Failure Modes & Fixes

| Failure | Why It Happens | Fix |
|---------|---|---|
| Map doesn't match the terrain; you're turned around | Map was not oriented correctly; you rotated it mentally instead of physically. | Stop. Locate ONE identifiable feature (peak, pass, saddle). Point it out on the map. Rotate the map so that feature is in the same direction on paper as it is in front of you. Recheck terrain association. |
| You followed a ridge that has no contour line on the map | Misread the scale; the ridge is smaller than the map's resolution (1:50,000 maps show features ~300 ft apart; small drainages don't appear). | Cross-reference with the terrain. If the ground feature is real and the map doesn't show it, ignore it for navigation. Use visible peaks/ridges that ARE on the map. |
| Handrail (creek, ridge) ends or splits, and you continue blindly | You became overconfident in the handrail and stopped checking the map. | Mark your last known position on the map. Use terrain association again to locate yourself before moving. |
| Catching feature is too far away to verify | Feature is beyond visual range; clouds or haze obscure it. | Drop back to shorter-range features (passes, ridges 2–3 miles away). Wait for visibility to improve, or navigate by contour counting + dead reckoning. |
| You're on the wrong side of a saddle (hiking uphill when you should be level or downhill) | Contours were too tightly bunched to read; saddle location was misidentified on the map. | Stop. Count contours to locate saddle on the map. Adjust bearing. |
