## Goal & When To Use

Terrain reading means relating yourself to the land using contours, slopes, and visible landmarks. The Army calls navigating by terrain association more forgiving of mistakes than dead reckoning, because you compare what the map says you should see with what you see, and errors show up as a mismatch (ATP 3-50.21 para 7-61). Use it to:

- Confirm your position before you move.
- Choose a handrail (a feature that runs roughly along your direction of travel).
- Choose a catching feature (a prominent feature that tells you that you have gone too far).
- Orient a map with or without a compass.

**This is a planning and position-checking method, not a reason to leave a place you should stay.** If you are lost and not very, very confident of the route, the Forest Service says it is better to stay put (USFS, If You Get Lost). See `01_core_principles.md` and `07_navigation.md`.

## Steps

### Step 1: Read the contour lines

A contour line connects points at the same elevation (TC 3-25.26 para 9-8).

1. **Contour interval (CI):** the vertical distance between adjacent lines, printed in the map margin with the unit (feet, meters or yards) (TC 9-9). Always read it first.
2. **Spacing shows steepness.** Evenly spaced, widely spaced lines mean a gentle slope. Evenly spaced, close lines mean a steep slope; the closer the lines, the steeper (TC 9-16, 9-17).
3. **Index contours.** Every fifth line is heavier and numbered with its elevation (TC 9-8). Count up or down from the nearest numbered line to find other elevations (TC 9-9).
4. **Estimate climb.** Count the contour lines between you and the goal and multiply by the interval. Five lines at a 40-foot interval is about 200 feet of climb or descent. This gives climbing effort, not distance on the ground (this guide's arithmetic).

### Step 2: Recognize landforms

The Army describes these in ATP 3-50.21 paras 7-74 and 7-75.

| Feature | Map pattern | On the ground | Use |
|---------|-------------|---------------|-----|
| **Ridge** | U or V contours whose closed end points away from high ground | Sloping line of high ground; steep to cross at right angles, may be a gentle incline along its length | Can serve as a handrail in good weather. Avoid ridge tops in thunderstorms (NWS). |
| **Valley** | U or V contours whose closed end points upstream, toward high ground | Stretched-out groove, usually with running water; high ground on three sides | Locate it on the map to match your position. **It is not a route out.** See the warning below. |
| **Draw** | Smaller U or V contours pointing toward high ground | Small stream course, little level ground | Shows where water collects. There is essentially no level ground and little room to move in it (ATP 7-75). |
| **Spur** | U or V contours with the closed end pointing away from high ground | Short sloping line jutting from the side of a ridge | A fixed point to match to the map. |
| **Saddle** | Hourglass shape | Dip or low point between two areas of higher ground | A good landmark to match to the map. |
| **Hill** | Concentric closed circles | High ground, slopes down in all directions | Innermost circle is the top. |
| **Cliff** | Contours very close together, sometimes touching | Vertical or near-vertical drop | Never assume you can cross. Treat bunched lines as a hazard. |
| **Steep slope** | Close, evenly spaced contours | Hard going, risk of a fall | Avoid for foot travel unless you must (TC 9-17). |
| **Gentle slope** | Wide, evenly spaced contours | Open, gradual ground | Easier movement (TC 9-16). |

**Do not follow a stream or drainage downhill as a plan.** The Forest Service says that "as a very last resort" you may follow a drainage or stream downhill, and calls it often a difficult path that could lead to a trail or road (USFS, If You Get Lost). Olympic National Park warns that rivers and creeks are often the most dangerous routes there (Olympic NP, Wilderness Safety). Staying put is the default. A ridge line is not a highway either: the NWS says to avoid open fields, hilltops and ridge tops in a lightning storm (NWS, Lightning Safety Outdoors).

### Step 3: Handrails, catching features, aiming off

**Handrail.** A linear feature that runs **roughly parallel to your direction of travel**, such as a road, railroad, power line, ridge line or stream. You can rough-compass along it without precision work or steering marks for as long as it stays on one side of you (ATP 3-50.21 para 7-65).

1. Pick a feature that runs the way you are going. A road that runs north-south is a handrail if you are traveling north or south. It is not a handrail if you are traveling east; it crosses your path (this guide's example).
2. Keep it on your right or left and glance at the map often.
3. Know where it ends or turns away from your route.

**Catching feature.** A prominent feature near the point where your route or the handrail changes direction. It warns you that it is time to part ways, and it tells you when you have gone too far (ATP 7-66). A long feature that crosses your path ahead, such as a road or river, is a good catching feature: if you reach it, you have gone too far.

**Aim off (deliberate offset).** TC 3-25.26 para 8-15 and ATP 3-50.21 para 7-58 support this when the destination is on or near a large linear feature that crosses your direction of travel (road, railroad, power line, ridge, stream). Steer about 10 degrees to one side of your planned azimuth. When you reach the feature you know which way to turn. Each degree moves you about 18 m per 1,000 m (TC 8-15), so 10 degrees over 1,000 m puts you about 180 m to one side.

*Example:* a cabin stands on a road that crosses your route. Aim 10 degrees to the left of the bearing to the cabin. When you reach the road, turn right.

**Attack point.** The last easily identified point from which you take a precise bearing for a counted distance. About 500 m or less is most desirable (ATP 7-67).

### Step 4: Terrain association and finding your position

1. **Orient the map.** Hold it flat with its north and south matching north and south on the ground (ATP 7-69). With a compass, line the baseplate edge along a north-south grid line and correct for declination (ATP 7-70; see `dead-reckoning-and-pace.md`). Keep metal away from the compass (ATP 7-70 note).
2. **Without a compass,** find linear features common to the map and the ground, such as roads, railroads or power lines, and rotate the map to match. Check with a second feature, because one linear feature can leave the map reversed (ATP 7-72).
3. **Match what you see.** Pick identifiable features on the map (peak, saddle, stream junction) and find them on the ground. Compare the contours with the ground around you.
4. **Resection** (locating yourself from known points) with a map and compass (TC 3-25.26 paras 6-18, 6-19):
   - Orient the map. Identify two or three known distant features on the ground and mark them on the map.
   - Take a compass (magnetic) azimuth to one feature. Convert it to a map azimuth with the declination rule (compass to map; see `dead-reckoning-and-pace.md`).
   - Convert it to a back azimuth: add 180 if the azimuth is 180 or less, subtract 180 if it is more (TC 6-3). Draw that line on the map **from the known feature back toward you**.
   - Repeat for the second and third features. Where the lines cross is your position. Three or more features give greater accuracy (TC 6-18).
5. **Modified resection** if you are on a linear feature such as a road, canal or stream: find one distant identifiable point, take its azimuth, convert it, make it a back azimuth, and draw it from the point. You are where the line crosses the linear feature (TC 6-21).

### Step 5: Aspect (the direction a slope faces)

Aspect is the compass direction a slope faces looking downhill. To read it from the map, find downhill: it is across the contour lines, toward the lower numbers. If downhill points north, the slope is north-facing (general practice).

Aspect helps with weather effects (ATP 3-50.21 para 7-46). In the Northern Hemisphere, north-facing slopes get less sun and are cooler and damper than south-facing ones. In summer they hold patches of snow. In winter, south-facing slopes and the south sides of boulders lose snow first. All of this is reversed in the Southern Hemisphere (ATP 7-46). Treat these as hints about terrain, not as a way to find north.

### Step 6: Estimate distance on the map

1. Use the graphic (bar) scale printed on the map. Lay the edge of a strip of paper along your route, mark the points, and read the distance on the bar scale (ATP 3-50.21 paras 7-78, 7-79).
2. A straight line is the shortest distance on the map. Ground distance is longer around obstacles. Climbing, brush and poor footing slow you down (general practice).
3. For time, use your own measured pace or time over a known stretch of ground like the route (see `dead-reckoning-and-pace.md`). The sources give no standard walking speeds, and this guide does not either.

## Failure Modes & Fixes

| Failure | Why it happens | Fix |
|---------|----------------|-----|
| Map does not match the terrain; you feel turned around | Map was not oriented to the ground, or you rotated it only in your head | Stop. Find one feature you can identify on both. Rotate the map physically until that feature is in the same direction on paper as in front of you. Check with a second feature (ATP 7-72). |
| A feature is on the ground but not on the map | It is smaller than the map shows, or the map is old | Ignore it for navigation. Use features that are on the map. |
| Handrail ends or splits and you keep walking | Overconfidence in the feature; stopped checking the map | Stop. Mark your last known position. Use terrain association to relocate before moving on. |
| You arrive at a long feature and do not know which way to turn | No deliberate offset | Next time aim off to a chosen side (Step 3). Now: use the map and the last confirmed feature to decide, or stay put if you cannot. |
| Catching feature cannot be seen | Haze, cloud, or too far away | Use closer, more visible features, wait for visibility, or move more slowly with dead reckoning (`dead-reckoning-and-pace.md`). |
| You are climbing when the map says level | Misread the contour interval or misidentified the saddle | Stop. Recount the contour lines between you and the saddle on the map and recheck the interval. |
| You want to follow a creek downhill because it must lead to people | Natural but dangerous instinct | Do not, unless it is a very last resort (USFS). Olympic NP calls rivers and creeks often the most dangerous routes. Stay put and signal. |
