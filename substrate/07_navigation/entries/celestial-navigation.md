## Goal & When To Use

These methods give **rough** direction when you have no compass. Use them to hold a straight line, to orient a map, or to cross-check a compass. They do not justify leaving a place you should stay (see `07_navigation.md`). If a compass and one of these methods disagree, the compass is the instrument to trust once you have corrected for declination (see `dead-reckoning-and-pace.md`).

- **Sun visible:** shadow-tip method, equal-shadow method, or the watch method.
- **Clear night in the Northern Hemisphere:** Polaris.
- **Clear night in the Southern Hemisphere:** the Southern Cross.
- **Moon visible:** a rough east-west reference only.

Cross-check with a second method before you commit to a long leg (this guide's judgment).

## Steps

### What the sun does (read this first)

The sun rises in the east and sets in the west, but rarely due east or due west, and the point changes with the season. Shadows move in the opposite direction from the sun (ATP 3-50.21 para 7-21).

- In the Northern Hemisphere, shadows move from west to east during the day and point **north at local noon** (ATP 7-21).
- That "due south at noon" is true only **north of 23.4 degrees north**. South of 23.4 degrees south, the sun is due north at noon and the shadow points south. In the tropics the noon sun can be north or south depending on the date and place (ATP 7-22).
- So a shadow points north only around local noon. In the morning, a Northern Hemisphere shadow falls toward the west side; in the afternoon, toward the east side (this guide's inference from ATP 7-21). Do not use "shadow equals north" at other times of day.

### Method 1: Shadow-tip (sun only)

This works anywhere on Earth except beyond 66.5 degrees latitude north or south (ATP 7-22). The moon is not covered by the Army method; use the sun.

1. Push a stick into the ground at a spot where it casts a clear shadow. The ground can slope, and an inclined stick works; a flat spot the size of your hand is enough (ATP 7-23). Mark the tip of the shadow with a stone. **This first mark is always west.**
2. Wait 10 to 15 minutes, until the tip has moved a few inches (ATP 7-22). Mark the new tip. This second mark is east.
3. Draw a straight line through the two marks. This is an approximate east-west line.
4. Stand with the first mark (west) on your left. North is in front of you, east on your right, south behind you (ATP 7-22).

A line at right angles to the east-west line is the approximate north-south line (ATP 7-22).

### Method 2: Equal-shadow (sun, more accurate)

Use it at latitudes below 66 degrees, any time of year (ATP 7-25).

1. Start 5 to 10 minutes before local noon. Put a stick upright at a level spot where it casts a shadow at least 12 inches long. Mark the tip.
2. Draw an arc around the stick using the shadow length as the radius.
3. The shadow shortens toward noon, then lengthens. Mark the spot where the tip touches the arc the second time.
4. A straight line through the two marks is east-west.

### Method 3: Watch (analog watch, approximate)

The Army says this method can be inaccurate, especially in the lower latitudes, and may cause circling (ATP 7-28; TC 3-25.26 para 8-21). Use it for a first guess only.

- **Northern temperate zone:** hold the watch flat and point the hour hand at the sun. South is midway between the hour hand and 12 o'clock (ATP 7-26). **On daylight saving time, use 1 o'clock instead of 12.**
- **Southern temperate zone:** point the 12 at the sun. North is midway between the 12 and the hour hand. **On daylight saving time, use 1 o'clock instead of 12** (ATP 7-27).
- If you are unsure which end of the line is north, the sun is in the east before noon and in the west after noon (ATP 7-26).
- Digital watch: draw a clock face on paper with the correct time and use that (ATP 7-29).
- To limit circling, make a shadow clock (ATP 7-24), set the watch to the time it shows, and take another shadow-clock reading after an hour of travel (ATP 7-28).

### Method 4: Polaris (Northern Hemisphere, night)

1. Find the Big Dipper. The outer two stars of its bowl are the **pointer stars**. Draw a line from the outer bottom star to the outer top star and extend it about five times the distance between them. Polaris is on that line (ATP 7-34). NASA gives the same check.
2. Or find Cassiopeia, the "W" of five stars. Polaris lies between Cassiopeia and the Big Dipper, and the two groups sit on opposite sides of it (ATP 7-33, 7-36).
3. Face Polaris. You are facing true north, not magnetic north (ATP 7-36; NASA).

Facts about Polaris:

- It is not the brightest star (NASA).
- It stays in nearly the same place all night: it moves only about 1.08 degrees around the pole (ATP 7-33). **It does not rise or set.** The Big Dipper and Cassiopeia rotate around it counterclockwise (ATP 7-33).
- Its height above the horizon is about equal to your latitude. At 35 degrees north it is about 35 degrees up (ATP 7-35). Near the equator it sits close to the horizon and can be hard to see behind trees or haze. Far north it is high in the sky (TC 3-25.26 para 8-25).
- It cannot be seen in the Southern Hemisphere (TC 8-25).

### Method 5: Southern Cross (Southern Hemisphere, night)

1. Find the Southern Cross (Crux). Its four brightest stars form a cross; the two stars of its long axis are the guide (ATP 7-37).
2. Imagine a distance of 4.5 to 5 times the distance between those two stars, extended along the long axis. Look down to the horizon from that point; that is south (ATP 7-38).
3. Check with the two pointer stars to the left of the Cross, which also point toward south and help tell the true Cross from the False Cross. The area between them is dark and nearly empty of stars (ATP 7-38).

### Moon (rough east-west reference only)

The moon gives no usable bearing. ATP 3-50.21 para 7-31 gives one rule: if the moon rises before the sun has set, the lit side is the west; if it rises after midnight, the lit side is the east. That is a rough east-west reference during the night. Do not use the moon for time or bearing.

### Other signs (weak hints)

Moss cannot be relied on to show north because it grows all the way around some trees. Growth is lusher on the equator-facing side of trees, and tree rings are more widely spaced there (ATP 7-44). In the Northern Hemisphere, north-facing slopes are cooler and damper than south-facing ones (ATP 7-46). Treat all of these as tiebreakers, not a way to set a course (this guide's judgment).

## Failure Modes & Fixes

| Failure | Why it happens | Fix |
|---------|----------------|-----|
| Shadow marks are almost on top of each other | Waited too short a time | Wait the full 10 to 15 minutes and mark again (ATP 7-22). |
| You used "shadow points north" in the morning or evening | The shadow points north only around local noon in the Northern Hemisphere, and only north of 23.4 degrees north | Use the shadow-tip method, which works from the two tip positions at any time of day, not the direction of one shadow. |
| Shadow-tip line looks wrong | A mark was disturbed or the stick was moved between marks, or you are beyond 66.5 degrees latitude | Mark again with the stick left in place. Beyond 66.5 degrees the method is ineffective (ATP 7-22). |
| Watch method gives a direction that does not fit the terrain | The method is approximate and can be off, especially at lower latitudes; daylight saving time not applied | Use 1 o'clock for daylight saving time; confirm with another method; avoid long legs on it alone (ATP 7-26 to 7-28). You may circle. |
| Cannot find Polaris | Big Dipper low or hidden, trees or haze near the horizon | Scan the sky at about the angle above the horizon that equals your latitude (ATP 7-35). Use Cassiopeia on the other side of Polaris. Polaris stays put; the Dipper and Cassiopeia rotate around it. |
| Big Dipper and Polaris are confusing | The Little Dipper's dim stars are hard to see near towns, and Polaris is the last star in its handle | Use the Big Dipper and Cassiopeia together (ATP 7-33). |
| Southern Cross looks wrong | Confused with the False Cross | The pointer stars to the left help tell the true Cross; the dark coal sac between them marks it (ATP 7-38). |
| Star direction drifts as you walk | The star moves in the sky | Check the azimuth again. A star near the north horizon serves about half an hour; when moving south, check about every 15 minutes (ATP 7-39). |
