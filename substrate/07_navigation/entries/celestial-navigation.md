## Goal & When To Use

Celestial navigation gives you true direction (north or south) using the sun and stars — no compass, GPS, or instruments required. Use this when:

- **Sun is visible:** shadow-stick method or sun arc (east-west) works any time of day.
- **Night sky is clear:** Polaris (Northern Hemisphere) or Southern Cross (Southern Hemisphere) give absolute north/south.
- **Moon is visible:** moon position can confirm time and rough bearing.
- **You have no compass or GPS.**

This is the foundation of the navigation standard rule: *know where you are before you move.*

## Steps

### Method 1: Shadow-Stick (Day or Night)

**Purpose:** Find true north/south using the sun (day) or moon (night). Works anytime the shadow falls.

1. Push a straight stick (3–4 ft tall) vertically into level ground, perpendicular to the earth.
2. Mark the tip of the shadow on the ground with a stone or mark in sand.
3. **Wait 10–15 minutes** (do not wait 1 minute — the shadow moves slowly in the early morning and late afternoon; morning/late afternoon is acceptable; midday accelerates).
4. Mark the new shadow tip position.
5. **Draw or trace a line connecting the two marks.** This line runs **east-west** (first mark = west, second mark = east).
6. Stand perpendicular to this line. **If the line runs left-right in front of you, you are facing north** (or south if facing the opposite direction).

**Why it works:** The sun moves in an arc from east to west. Any two shadow positions give you the east-west line; perpendicular to that is north-south.

**Northern Hemisphere refinement:** In the Northern Hemisphere, the sun's arc is always south of your head (unless you're past the Arctic Circle). So if the line runs east-west, the sun's side of it points south.

### Method 2: Sun Arc (Fast, Day Only)

**Purpose:** Confirm east-west when you have limited time.

1. **Morning to midday:** The sun is moving from east to south; your shadow points roughly north.
2. **Midday:** Your shadow points north (Northern Hemisphere) — this is the only time shadows point true north because the sun is due south.
3. **Midday to evening:** Your shadow points north again as the sun swings from south to west.

**Practical use:** Extend your arm toward the sun at midday (or any time if you accept ±15° error). Your shadow falls away from north; your arm points south.

### Method 3: Polaris (Northern Hemisphere, Night)

**Purpose:** Find true north using a star that sits directly above Earth's north pole.

1. **Locate the Big Dipper** (part of Ursa Major, the Great Bear). It looks like a pot with a handle. The two stars on the "cup" end are the **pointer stars.**
2. **Trace a line from the two pointer stars outward** (away from the handle). Continue about 5 times the distance between those two stars. You'll reach a bright star: **Polaris** (the North Star).
3. **Polaris marks true north.** Face it; north is directly in front of you.

**Alternate: Cassiopeia.** If the Big Dipper is below the horizon or obscured:
- Locate Cassiopeia (looks like a "W" or "M" depending on season).
- Polaris lies approximately midway between the Big Dipper and Cassiopeia (on the arc between them).
- Trace from Cassiopeia's center toward the Big Dipper; Polaris is near the midpoint.

**Polaris altitude:** Polaris sits at an angle above the horizon equal to your latitude. At 40°N (mid-US), Polaris is 40° above the horizon.

### Method 4: Southern Cross (Southern Hemisphere, Night)

**Purpose:** Find true south using a small, bright constellation visible only south of the equator.

1. **Locate the Southern Cross** (Crux). It is the smallest of the 88 constellations, but bright. It looks like a Greek cross (four stars in a + shape) tilted 5–45° from vertical depending on the season.
2. **Identify the long axis** — the two stars forming the longest line through the cross.
3. **Extend this line downward** (away from the top of the cross) roughly 4–5 times the cross's length. Where this line hits the horizon is **true south.**
4. **Two pointer stars** nearby (outside the cross, to the left) help confirm; they bracket the southern direction.

**Practical:** Draw an imaginary line from the top of the cross through its center, and extend it down to the horizon.

### Method 5: Moon Position (Night, Rough)

**Purpose:** Confirm time and rough direction when the moon is visible; less precise than Polaris.

- **New moon (invisible):** Moon is near the sun; no help.
- **Waxing crescent to first quarter:** Moon is in the western sky in the evening; sets near sunset.
- **Full moon:** Rises roughly east at sunset, is due south near midnight (Northern Hemisphere), sets roughly west before dawn.
- **Waning crescent to last quarter:** Moon is in the eastern sky before dawn; rises near sunrise.

The moon's position confirms time of night and that you're oriented correctly, but is not precise enough for a bearing without a compass.

## Failure Modes & Fixes

| Failure | Why It Happens | Fix |
|---------|---|---|
| Shadow-stick gives different direction 10 minutes later | Stick is not perpendicular to ground; ground is sloped. | Re-plant on level ground; use a level object (plumb the stick with your eye). Recheck after 15 minutes. |
| Can't find Polaris; Big Dipper is below horizon | Horizon too low, trees/terrain block it; wrong season. | Climb higher if safe; wait for Dipper to rotate above horizon (it circles Polaris all night). In summer at mid-latitudes, Dipper may be high; in winter, lower. |
| Two shadow marks are same position | Waited less than 10 min; sun is nearly overhead (near noon in tropics). | Wait at least 15 min. Shadow motion is fastest near sunrise/sunset. |
| Southern Cross looks wrong; many stars nearby | Confusion with False Cross (nearby stars forming a looser X). | True Cross is smaller, brighter, and more compact. Pointer stars are to the left; they narrow the field. |
| Polaris appears to move (rises/sets) | You are moving; or you misidentified a star near the horizon. | Polaris does not move. If it appears to move, you moved. Recheck your body position. At latitudes above ~50°N, Polaris is high and stationary. At lower latitudes, it's lower and can appear to move near the horizon. |
| Sun arc method gives different direction each time | You are using different body parts (arm vs. shadow). Use one consistent method. | Stick with shadow-stick (most reliable) or noon-shadow (midday only, true north). Sun-arc estimates are ±15°. |
