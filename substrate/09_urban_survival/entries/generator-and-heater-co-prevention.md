# Generator and Heater Carbon Monoxide Prevention

**This is general safety information from government pages. It is not medical advice. If anyone has symptoms or an alarm sounds, get outside first, then call 911.**

Carbon monoxide (CO) is a gas you cannot see or smell. Anything that burns fuel makes it. In an outage, the deaths come from the fixes: a generator in the garage, a grill brought inside, an oven used for heat. This entry goes deeper than the outage overview in [Power Outage Bug-In](power-outage-bug-in.md) and the survey rules in [`09_urban_survival.md`](../../../09_urban_survival.md#carbon-monoxide-and-fire-heat-light-and-cook-without-hurting-anyone) and [`03_fire.md`](../../../03_fire.md#fire-indoors-in-tents-and-in-vehicles-the-carbon-monoxide-rule).

> ⚠ **CARBON MONOXIDE: colorless, odorless, and fast. It kills people asleep before they feel anything.**
>
> More than 400 Americans die each year from CO poisoning that is not linked to fires [7]. People who are asleep, drunk, or on other substances can die before they have symptoms [7]. A generator in a confined space can produce high CO levels within minutes, and deadly levels can linger for hours after it is shut off [2]. Not smelling exhaust does not mean you are not exposed [2].
>
> **The rule:** nothing burns inside a tent, vehicle, garage, snow shelter or home, except a vented fireplace or stove in working order. A generator runs outdoors only, at least 20 ft from windows, doors and vents. A vent or a cracked window is not a fix [2][7][10].

---

## Recognize

### The signs of CO poisoning

- Headache, dizziness, weakness, upset stomach, vomiting, chest pain, confusion [7].
- Early signs are like the flu, without the fever [4]. That is why CO gets missed.
- Several people in the home feel sick at once, or feel better outside and worse back inside (general practice).
- Later signs: mental confusion, vomiting, loss of muscular coordination, loss of consciousness [4].

### The setups that cause it

- A generator in a garage, basement, crawlspace, shed or other enclosed or partly enclosed space, even with doors or windows open, or with fans running [2][7].
- A generator outdoors but too close to a window, door or vent, or with its exhaust pointing at the house [1][2][3].
- A car or truck running in a garage attached to the house, even with the door open [7].
- A charcoal grill, hibachi, lantern or camp stove used inside a home, tent or camper [6][8].
- A gas range or oven used to heat the home [8][10][11].
- An unvented fuel-burning heater (kerosene, propane, gas or camp heater) used inside a home, tent, camper or vehicle [6][11].
- A fireplace or stove that is unvented, blocked or in poor repair [5][7].

---

## ⚠ RED FLAGS / EVACUATE

- **A CO alarm sounds.** Never ignore it. Everyone goes outside now, then call 911 or emergency services from outside. Do not go back in until responders say you may [3][4].
- **Anyone feels dizzy, weak, sick or confused while a generator, heater, stove or vehicle is running nearby.** Get to fresh air right away and call 911 [2][3]. Do not wait to see if it passes.
- **Someone is asleep and cannot be woken, or is unconscious.** Everyone who can walk goes outside now. Call 911 from outside and say that someone is still inside (general practice). Do not go back in [4].
- **You smell gas.** Leave at once; no light switches or phones inside. Call the gas company from outside (see [`09_urban_survival.md`](../../../09_urban_survival.md#when-to-leave)).
- If phones are down, send a person to reach help while everyone stays in fresh air (general practice).

## Respond (prevention steps, in order)

### 1. Alarms first, before you start or light anything

1. Put battery-operated or battery-backup CO alarms on every level of the home and outside sleeping areas [3][5][10].
2. Do not mount them in kitchens or above fuel-burning appliances, near heating vents, or where furniture or drapes can cover them [4].
3. Test them monthly [1][2][5]. Check or replace the batteries when the clocks change in spring and fall [7]. Replace the alarm per the maker's instructions or every 5 years [7].
4. The test button checks the circuit, not the sensor [4]. Newer alarms signal when they need replacing [4]. A chirp usually means a low or dead battery (general practice). Replace it.
5. Interconnected alarms are best: when one sounds, they all sound [1].
6. Plug-in or hard-wired alarms need a battery backup, because an outage turns them off [4][5].

### 2. Portable generator

1. **Outdoors only. At least 20 ft from windows, doors, vents and attached garages**, with the exhaust pointing away from the house and from any building people use [1][2][7][10].
2. **Never in a garage, even with the door open.** Never in a basement, crawlspace, shed, or any enclosed or partly enclosed space. Fans, open doors and open windows do not make it acceptable [2][7].
3. Run a battery-operated or battery-backup CO alarm in the home while the generator runs [7].
4. Keep the generator dry. Touching a wet generator, or a device plugged into one, can shock you [10]. In wet weather, put it under an open, canopy-like cover on a dry surface where water cannot pool under it, never indoors or beside an opening [2].
5. Connect appliances with heavy-duty outdoor extension cords, long enough to keep the unit far from the house. Check the whole cord for cuts [2][10].
6. **Refuel only when the generator is off and cool.** Fuel spilled on hot engine parts can ignite [2][10].
7. Keep fuel outside living areas, in labeled non-glass safety containers, away from fuel-burning appliances such as a gas water heater [2].
8. Follow the maker's instructions [10].

### 3. Never wire a generator into the house through an outlet (backfeeding)

1. **Never plug a generator into a wall outlet to power the house wiring.** This is called backfeeding. It can electrocute utility workers and neighbors served by the same transformer, and it bypasses some household circuit protection [2][9].
2. To power house circuits, use a **properly installed transfer switch**: one approved for the use and installed to the maker's instructions by a qualified electrician [13]. Do not attach a portable generator directly to a building's wiring without one [13].
3. A generator feeding a building through a transfer switch also has to be grounded as OSHA describes. That is an electrician's job [13].
4. CDC adds that the main circuit breaker must be off and locked out before the generator starts, to protect utility workers [9].
5. Rules on permits, inspection and utility notice vary by place. Ask your utility and local building office (general practice).

With no transfer switch, plug appliances straight into the generator and leave the house wiring alone [13].

### 4. Battery or solar power stations

A battery or solar power station makes no exhaust and can be used indoors [11]. For anyone with a critical need, this removes the CO risk. Follow the maker's instructions, and keep it clear of bedding and out of heat (general practice). See [`11_essential_gear.md`](../../../11_essential_gear.md).

### 5. Space heaters

1. Electric heater: plug it directly into a wall outlet [5], never a power strip [1]. Keep it at least 3 ft from anything that can burn, such as bedding, curtains and furniture [5][12].
2. Turn space heaters off when you leave the room or go to bed [12]. Never leave a portable heater on while sleeping [5]. Look for one with automatic shut-off (general practice).
3. Never run a heater cord under rugs or carpet [1].
4. **Unvented fuel-burning heaters (kerosene, propane, gas, camp or catalytic).** Do not run one inside a home, tent, camper or vehicle, with a window open or not (this entry's rule, matching the survey guide). The agencies warn most strongly against one where people sleep: CPSC says not to use fuel-burning portable heaters while sleeping in enclosed areas such as tents, campers and other vehicles [6], and EPA says never to run a gas-burning heater in a poorly vented or closed room [11]. The only fuel-burning heat indoors is a fireplace or stove built to vent outdoors and kept in working order [7][11].
5. Never refuel any fuel-burning heater while it is running or hot; turn it off and let it cool first [5]. It should not be running inside (item 4). If you cannot heat the home without one, dress and bed for the cold instead (see [`09_urban_survival.md`](../../../09_urban_survival.md#cold)).

### 6. Fireplaces, wood stoves and furnaces

1. The one exception to "burn nothing indoors" is a fireplace or stove built to vent and kept in working order [7][11].
2. Have a professional inspect furnaces, boilers, fireplaces, chimneys, flues and vents every year [5][7][12].
3. Open the fireplace damper before lighting and keep it open until the ashes are cool [11].
4. Never burn charcoal in a fireplace [11]. Never cook or burn anything on a stove or fireplace that is not vented [7].

---

## ⚠ DO NOT

- **Do NOT run a generator in a garage, basement, shed, crawlspace or any enclosed or partly enclosed space, even with doors and windows open** [2][7].
- **Do NOT place a generator within 20 ft of a window, door or vent**, or point its exhaust at the house [1][2][7].
- **Do NOT use a grill, hibachi, lantern or camp stove inside a home, tent or camper**, and do not cook on a charcoal grill in a garage, even with the door open [1][8].
- **Do NOT heat a home with a gas range or oven** [8][10][11][12].
- **Do NOT run a car in an attached garage**, even with the door open [7].
- **Do NOT backfeed.** No generator into a wall outlet [2].
- **Do NOT refuel a running or hot generator or heater** [2][5].
- **Do NOT run an unvented fuel-burning heater (kerosene, propane, gas, camp) inside a home, tent, camper or vehicle** [6][11].
- **Do NOT ignore a CO alarm, or wait for symptoms** [3][4].
- **Do NOT trust a vent, a cracked window or your nose.** CO has no smell [2].

---

## Failure modes

| What goes wrong | Why it kills | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Generator moved "just inside the garage door" because of rain | CO builds up fast and lingers for hours [2] | Open canopy-style cover outdoors, on dry ground, far from openings [2] |
| Generator 20 ft away but exhaust faces a window | Fumes can still reach the opening [2] | Point exhaust away from every building people use [2] |
| Alarm batteries dead when the outage starts | No warning for sleepers | Test monthly, change batteries at clock changes [1][7] |
| Alarm sounds and the family waits for symptoms | Sleepers die before symptoms [7] | Out first, then 911 [3] |
| Oven on "just to take the edge off" | CO and fire risk [8][10] | Go to a warming location or a home with power |
| House wiring fed from a wall outlet | Electrocutes line crews and neighbors [2] | Transfer switch from an electrician, or plug in directly [13] |
