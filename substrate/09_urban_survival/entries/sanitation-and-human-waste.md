## Recognize

### Early Signs of Sanitation Failure

- **Toilet will not flush.** Water pressure has failed or sewage system is backed up.
- **Foul odor from drains or around the home.** Indicates sewage backup or contamination nearby.
- **Multiple toilets not flushing simultaneously.** Neighborhood or municipal sewage system failure (not just your home's plumbing).
- **Water discoloration** or boil-water notice issued by local authorities.
- **Public alerts** for sewage spills, contaminated water, or sanitation emergencies.

### Signs of Disease Risk (Fecal-Oral Transmission)

- **People sharing inadequate sanitation facilities** (one toilet for many people; no handwashing access).
- **Food or drinking water contaminated** with human or animal waste.
- **Diarrhea or vomiting** appearing in people who share the same living space.
- **Proximity of waste disposal** to food preparation, drinking water, or living areas.
- **Poor handwashing practices** (dirty hands touching food or faces).

### Disease Symptoms (When Contamination Has Occurred)

- **Onset: 1–10 days after exposure** (varies by pathogen).
- **Cholera & severe dysentery:** watery diarrhea ("rice water stools"), rapid dehydration, electrolyte loss, shock, death if untreated within hours.
- **Typhoid:** sustained high fever (104–106°F), headache, body aches, delirium, rose spots on trunk (pink rash), may progress to perforation and death.
- **Hepatitis A:** jaundice (yellowing of skin/eyes), abdominal pain, nausea, fatigue, dark urine.
- **Giardiasis & cryptosporidiosis:** chronic diarrhea, cramps, weight loss.

**These are leading killers in disasters where sanitation fails.** Prevention (proper waste containment + handwashing) is far more effective than treatment.

---

## ⚠ RED FLAGS / EVACUATE

**Seek medical help / call emergency services immediately if:**

1. **Acute watery diarrhea with signs of severe dehydration:** extreme thirst, no urination for >8 hours, confusion, dizziness upon standing, rapid/weak pulse, sunken eyes.
2. **High fever (>103°F) with headache, confusion, or body aches** — may indicate typhoid or other serious infection.
3. **Multiple people in the same household with acute diarrhea** — suggests common contamination; risk of rapid disease spread.
4. **Blood or mucus in stool** with severe abdominal pain.
5. **Signs of cholera (in endemic regions or if multiple cases reported):** rice-water diarrhea + rapid dehydration + shock — **this is life-threatening within hours; evacuate immediately for IV rehydration.**
6. **Jaundice (yellowing of skin/eyes)** — may indicate hepatitis or other serious liver infection.
7. **Persistent diarrhea in young children (<5 years), elderly, or people with compromised immunity** — dehydration risk is extreme in these groups.

**Action:** If safe to leave, go to an emergency room or hospital. If unsafe (disruption ongoing), call emergency services; they will advise on treatment or evacuation.

## Respond

### Phase 1: Immediate Sanitation Setup (Before Long-Term Failure)

**Do this as soon as you realize plumbing may fail (24–48 hours into an outage or emergency):**

1. **Fill all bathtubs, large containers, and sinks with water** — you'll need this for flushing toilets and handwashing. A filled bathtub provides ~50–80 gallons.
2. **Keep the toilet as a short-term backup.** If water pressure fails but the bowl still drains, you can pour collected water down the toilet to flush. But **do NOT plan on this long-term** — it wastes water and risk of backup increases.
3. **Identify a waste-disposal location.** If plumbing fails and you have a yard/outdoor space:
   - Choose a spot ≥100 feet away from water sources (wells, streams, springs).
   - ≥50 feet away from food storage areas.
   - ≥100 feet downslope if on sloped terrain (waste won't flow toward occupied areas).
4. **Gather supplies:** tarps, plastic bags, lime (calcium hydroxide, NOT quicklime), digging tools, hand-sanitizer, soap, water containers.

### Phase 2: Twin-Bucket ("Honey Bucket") System (Most Practical)

The twin-bucket system is the simplest and most effective for 1–4 weeks without plumbing.

**Setup:**
1. **Bucket 1 (Waste):** A sturdy 5-gallon bucket with a tight-fitting lid (paint buckets work; drill a toilet-seat-shaped hole in the lid if desired, or keep lid off during use).
2. **Bucket 2 (Rinse/Hand-wash):** Another 5-gallon bucket filled with collected water, soap, and a small cup or ladle for rinsing hands.

**Use:**
1. **Line the waste bucket with a heavy plastic bag.** Tie handles to the bucket rim.
2. **Use the bucket** — defecate or urinate into the bag.
3. **After each use,** add a handful of lime (calcium hydroxide) to the waste bucket — this kills pathogens, reduces odor, and aids decomposition. Lime is essential, not optional.
4. **Immediately wash your hands** in Bucket 2 (rinse water + soap), or use hand-sanitizer (≥60% alcohol) if water is scarce.
5. **Seal the waste bucket** with the lid between uses.
6. **Once the waste bucket is 2/3 full** (or daily, whichever comes first), tie the plastic bag, remove it, and prepare a new bag.

**Disposal of full bags:**
1. **Dig a cathole** (see below) or use a prepared pit (if multi-family, dig one shared pit).
2. **Place the sealed bag in the pit,** or empty the bag into the pit and bury the waste.
3. **Cover with soil** (≥12 inches) and lime.
4. **Keep records** — mark where waste is buried so it's not disturbed and can be avoided for future water sources.

### Phase 3: Cat-Hole Method (If Buckets Unavailable)

For temporary or dispersed situations where buckets are not available:

1. **Choose a site:** ≥100 feet from water; ≥50 feet from food; ≥200 feet from living quarters if possible.
2. **Dig a hole:** 6–8 inches deep, 12 inches wide.
3. **Defecate in the hole,** cover with a handful of soil, then add lime (1–2 tablespoons).
4. **Cover the hole completely** with soil.
5. **Do NOT use cat-holes in groups longer than a few days** — multiple holes close together create a localized contamination zone. For longer outages, use the twin-bucket system or dig a communal latrine pit.

### Phase 4: Latrine Pit (For Extended Outages or Groups)

If 4+ people and the outage will last >2 weeks:

1. **Dig a pit:** 2–3 feet deep, 3–4 feet long, 1–2 feet wide — location same as cat-hole rules (≥100 feet from water, downslope).
2. **Build a seat or frame:** place a wooden board across the pit with a hole for the toilet seat, or simply mark the edge so people know where to aim.
3. **Surround with privacy:** use a tent, tarp, or branches for privacy.
4. **After each use:** add a handful of soil, then lime.
5. **Cover the pit when nearly full:** bury with ≥12 inches of soil; mark the location so it's not dug into later.
6. **Dig a new pit** — don't reuse the same location for ≥6 months.

### Phase 5: Greywater and Handwashing

**Greywater (rinse water, wash water, non-fecal):**
- Keep greywater separate from fecal waste.
- Pour greywater ≥30 feet away from food areas and water sources.
- You can use greywater to water plants or dig a percolation pit (shallow hole where it drains into soil), but NOT into wells or streams.

**Handwashing (CRITICAL — the biggest disease-prevention step):**
1. **Wash hands after every bowel movement or urination.**
2. **Wash before eating, drinking, or preparing food.**
3. **Use soap and water if available.** Soap + water removes pathogens better than water alone.
4. **If water is scarce, use hand-sanitizer (≥60% alcohol)** — it's effective at reducing bacterial and viral spread.
5. **Wash under fingernails** (pathogens hide there).
6. **Dry hands** on a clean cloth or air-dry; don't wipe on shared cloths.

### Phase 6: Food Safety During Sanitation Failure

1. **Do NOT eat raw vegetables or fruits** that may have been watered with contaminated water.
2. **Cook all food thoroughly** — heat kills most pathogens. Boil vegetables and eggs; cook meat to internal temps (see game-animal entries).
3. **Drinking water:** boil for ≥1 minute, or use bleach (2 drops per liter, let sit 30 min before drinking).
4. **Do NOT share cups, utensils, or drinking vessels.** Each person gets their own.
5. **Keep food preparation surfaces clean** — wipe with soap and water or dilute bleach.

### Phase 7: Disease Monitoring and Care

1. **If someone develops diarrhea or vomiting:**
   - Isolate them from food preparation if possible (use separate bucket/bathroom setup).
   - Increase their handwashing and enforce strict hygiene around shared areas.
   - Monitor hydration — have them drink small sips of water or electrolyte solution (salt + sugar in water, or sports drinks if available).
   - Do NOT share eating utensils or drinking cups.

2. **If multiple people become ill with acute diarrhea or fever,** this is a disease outbreak signal — increase sanitation rigor, isolate the ill, and seek medical help.

3. **Keep records** of who is sick, when symptoms started, and symptoms — this helps identify the contamination source and aids medical responders if evacuation becomes necessary.

## ⚠ DO NOT

- **Do NOT defecate near water sources** (wells, streams, stored water containers). Even if far away, contaminated soil can leach into groundwater.
- **Do NOT use the same hole** for waste and water collection or food storage.
- **Do NOT skip handwashing.** This is the #1 failure mode in outages; every family member must wash hands after toileting and before food.
- **Do NOT dispose of waste in storm drains, gutters, or creeks.** This spreads disease to downstream users and natural areas.
- **Do NOT reuse a waste bucket without thoroughly cleaning it.** Pathogens survive on surfaces; rinse with boiled water or dilute bleach (1:10 bleach:water).
- **Do NOT mix human waste with food waste** — bury them separately, far apart.
- **Do NOT let children play near waste disposal areas.** Young children are at highest risk for fecal-oral transmission.
- **Do NOT assume diarrhea is minor.** In infants (<2 years), elderly, or people with compromised immunity, dehydration from diarrhea can be fatal within hours.
- **Do NOT attempt to treat cholera or severe dysentery** without medical care — these require IV rehydration and antibiotics. Evacuate if possible.
- **Do NOT neglect lime.** Without it, waste remains infectious for weeks and odor attracts vectors (flies, rats). Lime is not optional.

---

## Medical Continuity

If someone requires ongoing medication or medical care during sanitation failure:

1. **Diarrhea/vomiting may interfere with oral medication absorption.** Consult a telehealth provider or pharmacist on timing or alternatives if gut absorption is compromised.
2. **Electrolyte loss from diarrhea** can destabilize people on certain medications (diuretics, cardiac drugs, antihypertensives). Monitor closely and seek medical advice.
3. **If you have people with mobility issues or elderly residents,** ensure waste disposal is as close to living areas as practical while still following distance rules (this is a balance — closer is less safe for water, but accessibility matters for compliance).
