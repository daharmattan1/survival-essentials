> **Not medical advice.** This entry gives commonly published household sanitation steps from health-agency pages. For illness, call a clinician or 911. Local health officials' instructions override anything here.

## Recognize

### Signs that sanitation has failed

- Toilets will not flush (no water pressure, or the sewer is backed up).
- Several toilets or drains back up at once, or there is a sewage smell, which can mean a neighborhood or system problem rather than your plumbing.
- Local officials issue a boil-water advisory or a sewage warning.

### Signs of illness spreading

- Diarrhea or vomiting in more than one person who shares a space.
- Waste near food, water, or places where children play.
- Hands not being washed after the toilet, after diapers, or before food.

---

## ⚠ RED FLAGS / EVACUATE

**Call a doctor or clinic, or 911 if severe, for any of these (CDC):**

1. Bloody diarrhea.
2. Diarrhea that lasts more than 3 days.
3. High fever (over 102 °F).
4. Vomiting so often that liquids cannot be kept down.
5. Signs of dehydration: not urinating much, dry mouth and throat, dizziness when standing.

If someone seems seriously ill, call 911.

---

## Respond

### Step 1: Ask local officials first

Local officials know whether the sewer or water system is working and how waste should be handled in your area. Listen for instructions from your city, county, or state health department, and follow them over this entry.

### Step 2: Set up a bucket toilet

1. Use a sturdy 5-gallon bucket (OSU suggests two buckets, one for urine and one for solid waste; only the solid-waste bucket needs a liner).
2. Line the bucket with a heavy-duty plastic bag. Washington State DOH says to line the toilet bowl or bucket with a kitchen garbage bag inside a second bag (double-bag).
3. After each use, add an absorbent to the solid waste: kitty litter, peat moss, lime, or something similar that absorbs liquid (Washington DOH), or about a half cup of carbon material (OSU). The sources list lime only as one absorbent option; neither says lime kills germs.
4. Fill the solid-waste bucket no more than half full (OSU).
5. When you change the bag, wear disposable gloves, double-bag the waste, and tie or seal it tightly (OSU).

### Step 3: Store and dispose of waste as officials direct

1. **Do not put human waste into the normal garbage unless local officials say you may.** OSU says it needs special treatment and should not go to a landfill with other household waste, and that you may have to store it until the public sanitary system is fixed.
2. Store sealed bags separately from other garbage, in a covered container, far from food and water, and away from children, pets, and wildlife (Washington DOH).
3. Washington DOH suggests putting sealed bags in a garbage can in the garage for disposal. Practices differ by place, so ask your local health department or sewer authority which one applies to you.
4. **Burial is a last resort and only where officials allow it.** If you are in a rural area and must bury waste, OSU says not to bury it within 100 feet of a water source or where contaminants can leach downhill into one; Washington DOH says to dig on dry land at least 100 feet from wells, homes, and open water, then cover and pack down the soil.
5. Never dump waste in storm drains, creeks, or yards where people or animals will contact it (general practice).

### Step 4: Wash hands every time

1. **Wash with soap and clean running water for at least 20 seconds** after using the toilet, after changing diapers, and before preparing or eating food (CDC).
2. **If you have no soap and water, use an alcohol-based hand sanitizer with at least 60% alcohol.** Sanitizer is not as effective when hands are visibly dirty or greasy, so wash those hands with soap and water when you can (CDC).
3. A simple station is a jug of clean water with a spigot, soap, towels, and a catch bucket. Do not try to reuse the water from the catch bucket, and sanitize the spigot handle regularly (OSU).
4. Wet wipes or sanitizer are acceptable when running water is unavailable (Washington DOH).

### Step 5: Drinking water during a water failure

1. If officials issue a boil advisory, bring water to a full rolling boil for 1 minute (3 minutes above 6,500 feet), let it cool, or use commercially bottled water (CDC).
2. For other treatment methods, see the water chapter and follow local instructions.

### Step 6: If someone gets sick

1. Everyone washes hands with soap and water after contact with the sick person or their waste.
2. Use the red flags above to decide when to call a clinician or 911.

---

## ⚠ DO NOT

- **Do NOT put human waste in the regular trash** unless local officials say that is allowed.
- **Do NOT bury waste or empty buckets within 100 feet of wells, streams, open water, or stored water.**
- **Do NOT dump waste in storm drains, gutters, or creeks.**
- **Do NOT skip handwashing.** Use soap and water, or sanitizer that is at least 60% alcohol when hands are not visibly dirty.
- **Do NOT count on lime to kill germs.** The sources list it only as an absorbent.
- **Do NOT let children handle waste buckets or play near storage or disposal areas.**
- **Do NOT wait on diarrhea that is bloody, lasts more than 3 days, comes with high fever, or comes with signs of dehydration.** Call a clinician.

---

## Med Continuity

1. Vomiting and diarrhea can change how well oral medicines work. Call a pharmacist or clinician for advice on timing or alternatives instead of changing doses on your own.
2. Diarrhea can lead to dehydration, which is especially risky for people who take medicines for blood pressure, heart conditions, or fluid balance. Tell a clinician early.
3. Do not change, stop, or ration a prescription without a clinician's advice. See the `chronic-med-continuity` entry.
