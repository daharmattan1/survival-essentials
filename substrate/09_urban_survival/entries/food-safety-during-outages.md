# Food Safety During Outages and Floods

**This is general food-handling information from government pages. It is not medical advice. For severe illness, call a doctor or 911. For medicine and infant feeding, ask a pharmacist or clinician; this entry gives no dosing.**

Cold food spoils quietly. You cannot tell by looking or smelling [3], and tasting it is the one test the agencies all rule out [1][4]. So the rules here are clocks and thermometers. The same clocks are in the survey guide ([Food: what to keep and what to throw out](../../../09_urban_survival.md#food-what-to-keep-and-what-to-throw-out)) and the outage overview in [Power Outage Bug-In](power-outage-bug-in.md). This entry adds thermometer use, refreezing, floodwater, infant formula and refrigerated medicine.

---

## Recognize

### When these rules apply

- The power is out and you do not know when it returns.
- A refrigerator or freezer has stopped, or the door was left open.
- Floodwater or stormwater may have touched food, cans, bottles or cooking gear [4].

### Signs someone ate food that turned

CDC lists diarrhea, stomach pain or cramps, nausea, vomiting and fever, starting within hours or taking several days [9].

---

## ⚠ RED FLAGS / EVACUATE

See a doctor for any of these [9]. Call 911 if the person is very ill, confused or cannot be woken (general practice):

- Bloody diarrhea.
- Diarrhea lasting more than 3 days.
- A high fever, over 102 F.
- Vomiting so often that liquids will not stay down.
- Signs of dehydration: not urinating much, dry mouth and throat, dizziness on standing [9].

Babies, older adults, pregnant people and anyone with a weak immune system are the people to protect first (general practice). See [Dehydration and Diarrhea](../../06_first_aid/entries/dehydration-and-diarrhea.md).

## Respond

### 1. Before the outage

1. Put an appliance thermometer in the fridge and the freezer. Fridge at 40 F or below. Freezer at 0 F or below [1][3][4].
2. Freeze water jugs to fill empty space in the freezer (general practice).

### 2. During the outage: the clocks

Keep the doors closed. The numbers:

- **Closed fridge: about 4 hours** [1][3][5][10].
- **Full freezer: about 48 hours. Half-full freezer: about 24 hours** [1][3][5][10].
- **No thermometer and the power has been out longer than 4 hours:** throw out perishables [1][2][4].
- **Thermometer shows food at 40 F or higher for 2 hours or more:** throw it out [5].
- **Perishables left at room temperature for 2 hours go out, or 1 hour when it is above 90 F** [2][3].
- **Food kept in a cooler with ice at 40 F or below** can stay [1][4].
- **When in doubt, throw it out** [1][5].

Before the 4 hours are up, move perishables into a cooler with ice or frozen gel packs and keep them at 40 F or below (see [First day](../../../09_urban_survival.md#first-day) in the survey guide) [1].

### 3. What to throw out after 4 hours without power

Raw or cooked meat, poultry and fish; eggs and egg substitutes; luncheon meat and hot dogs; leftovers; casseroles, soups, stews and pizza; mixed salads; gravy and stuffing; milk, cream, yogurt, sour cream and soft cheeses; cut fresh fruit and vegetables; cooked vegetables; opened juices; creamy dressings; batters and doughs; cream-filled pastries; garlic in oil [1]. Opened mayonnaise, tartar sauce and horseradish go if they were held above 50 F for more than 8 hours [1].

**These can stay:** mustard, ketchup, pickles, jams and non-creamy dressings; whole fresh fruit and vegetables; unopened juices; dried fruit; baked goods without cream fillings; hard and processed cheeses; butter and margarine; herbs; flour; nuts [1].

### 4. Cold storage tricks

1. **Do not put food in the snow.** Outside temperatures vary, and the food can be exposed to dirty conditions and animals. Make ice instead: fill buckets, empty milk containers or cans with water and leave them outside to freeze [1].
2. Dry ice: 50 pounds should hold an 18-cubic-foot freezer for 2 days. Never touch it bare-handed [1]. Handle it by the maker's and supplier's instructions. Use it only in a ventilated space, never in a closed car or small room (general practice).

### 5. When the power returns

1. Use a thermometer, not your nose. Never taste food to check it [1][4].
2. Frozen food that still has ice crystals, or is at 40 F or below, can be cooked or refrozen [1][3][4].
3. Perishables in the fridge follow the clocks in step 2.
4. If you have no thermometer for the fridge, use the 4-hour rule.
5. After you throw food out, clean the fridge and freezer of spills before restocking (general practice).

### 6. Food that touched floodwater

1. Do not eat any food that may have come into contact with floodwater or stormwater [3][4].
2. Throw out food in cardboard boxes, including juice, milk and baby-formula boxes [4].
3. Throw out food in jars or bottles with screw caps, snap lids, crimped caps, twist caps or flip tops [4].
4. Throw out home-canned food, and any canned food or container that is bulging, open or damaged [4].
5. Throw out wooden cutting boards, baby bottle nipples and pacifiers that touched floodwater [4].
6. Commercially canned food in an intact, undamaged can can be saved. Remove the label if you can and note the expiration date. Brush or wipe off dirt or silt, wash the can in hot, soapy water, and rinse it with clean water [4]. Then sanitize it one of two ways [4]:
   - Soak it for 15 minutes in a solution of 1 cup (8 oz or 240 mL) of unscented household bleach in 5 gallons of water.
   - Or put it in a pot of water, bring it to a boil, and keep boiling for 2 minutes.

   Relabel the can with a marker, including the expiration date, and use the food as soon as possible [4]. Never mix bleach with ammonia or any other cleaner [11].
7. Use bottled, boiled or treated water for washing anything food touches (see [Boil Disinfection](../../02_water/entries/boil-disinfection.md)).

### 7. Infant formula and baby feeding

1. In an emergency, ready-to-feed formula is the CDC's best option because it needs no added water [8].
2. With no working fridge, make fresh formula for each feeding [8]. Mix it with bottled water, using the exact amounts of water and formula on the formula container [8]. If you have no bottled water, see [Boil Disinfection](../../02_water/entries/boil-disinfection.md).
3. Formula cans that touched unsafe water, or have dents, rust spots, puffy ends or a broken seal: throw them out at once [8]. Cardboard formula boxes that touched floodwater: throw them out [4].
4. Opened or prepared formula that sat in a fridge through an outage longer than 4 hours: throw it out (this entry's rule, the safer choice, using the perishable clock).
5. Questions about feeding a baby or about breast milk: ask a pediatric clinician. Do not dilute or stretch formula.

---

## ⚠ DO NOT

- **Do NOT taste food to decide.** Do not judge by smell or looks either [1][3][4].
- **Do NOT open the fridge or freezer to check.** Each opening shortens the clock.
- **Do NOT keep food that sat at 40 F or higher for 2 hours or more** [5].
- **Do NOT put food in the snow** [1].
- **Do NOT eat food that touched floodwater**, or use bottles, nipples or cardboard that did [4].
- **Do NOT use refrigerated medicine that you doubt without calling a pharmacist or clinician first** (next section).
- **Do NOT dilute, stretch or "top up" infant formula** (general practice).

## Med Continuity

This section gives no dosing, timing or rationing. Dosing questions go to a pharmacist, your prescriber, or 911.

1. **Refrigerated medicine that is not life-sustaining:** Ready.gov and CDC say to throw it away if the power is out for more than a day, unless the drug's label says otherwise, and to ask a doctor or pharmacist for a new supply [5][6]. If it is life-sustaining (insulin, for example), go to item 2 before you throw anything out.
2. **Life-sustaining refrigerated medicine (insulin, for example) that has warmed:** do not decide alone. Call a pharmacist or your prescriber now [7]. If you cannot reach one, call 911 or go to an emergency department (this entry's rule, matching the survey guide). This entry does not say how long a warmed medicine can be used. Insulin has its own manufacturer and FDA storage limits. See the survey guide's [Medicines and power-dependent devices](../../../09_urban_survival.md#medicines-and-power-dependent-devices) and [Chronic Medication Continuity](../../06_first_aid/entries/chronic-med-continuity.md).
3. **When unsure whether a medicine is still usable:** contact a pharmacist, health care provider or the manufacturer [7].
4. **Medicine that touched floodwater or unsafe water:** discard it, even in its original container [7].
5. For a prescribed device (an auto-injector, for example): use it as its label and the prescriber direct, and call 911 for a reaction.

## Failure modes

| What goes wrong | Fix |
|---|---|
| Opening the fridge "just to look" | Keep it shut; read the thermometer from outside if you have one |
| No thermometer and a guess | Out longer than 4 hours: throw out perishables [1] |
| Snow used as a freezer | Make ice outdoors instead [1] |
| "It smells fine" | Smell is not a test [3] |
| Refrozen thawed food with no ice crystals and above 40 F | Only food with ice crystals, or at 40 F or below, can be cooked or refrozen [1][4] |
| Cans and jars from a flooded pantry | Cardboard, screw caps and damaged cans go; intact commercial cans can be cleaned [4] |
