# Chronic Medication Continuity

**This entry is NOT medical advice and does not replace your clinician or pharmacist. It lists storage facts and planning steps published by government health agencies, and it tells you when to get help. It does not tell you how to change, skip, split, stretch, or ration any dose. Dose questions go to a clinician, a pharmacist, or 911.**

---

## Recognize

### Medicine at risk

- Power is out and a medicine is normally kept in a refrigerator (insulin is the common example).
- Medicine has been exposed to heat, direct sun, or freezing.
- You cannot reach a pharmacy or are running low on a prescription.
- You have been evacuated without your medicines or devices.

### Warning signs that need care

**High blood sugar and diabetic ketoacidosis (DKA)** (CDC): being very thirsty, urinating a lot, fast and deep breathing, dry skin and mouth, flushed face, fruity-smelling breath, headache, muscle aches, being very tired, nausea and vomiting, stomach pain.

**Low blood sugar** (CDC): fast heartbeat, shaking, sweating, nervousness or anxiety, irritability or confusion, dizziness, hunger. CDC defines severe low blood sugar as below 54 mg/dL, and often someone else has to help.

**Heart attack** (CDC): chest pain or discomfort, feeling weak, light-headed or faint, pain in the jaw, neck, back, arms or shoulders, shortness of breath.

**Stroke** (CDC): sudden trouble walking, dizziness or loss of balance; sudden trouble seeing; sudden numbness or weakness in the face, arm or leg, especially on one side; sudden confusion or trouble speaking; sudden severe headache.

---

## ⚠ RED FLAGS / EVACUATE

**Call 911 or go to an emergency room now for any of these:**

- **Possible DKA:** CDC says to go to the ER or call 911 immediately if blood sugar stays at 300 mg/dL or above, breath smells fruity, you are vomiting and cannot keep food or drink down, you have trouble breathing, or you have several DKA signs. High ketones are a medical emergency. Your clinician may have given you different numbers; follow them.
- **Severe low blood sugar** (CDC: below 54 mg/dL, and someone usually has to help), or a person with diabetes who loses consciousness (CDC's First Aid for Seizures page lists this as a 911 trigger). Call 911.
- **Heart attack signs:** call 9-1-1 immediately (CDC).
- **Stroke signs:** call 9-1-1 right away (CDC).
- **Seizure:** call 911 if it lasts longer than 5 minutes, another follows soon after, the person has trouble breathing or waking up afterward, is injured, is in water, has never had a seizure before, is pregnant, or has diabetes and loses consciousness (CDC).

**For any other symptom after a missed, spoiled, or stretched-out medicine,** call your clinician, a pharmacist, or 911 if it is severe.

---

## Respond

### Step 1: Call before you change anything

1. **Call your prescriber or pharmacist.** Ready.gov says to consult your doctor or pharmacist immediately for a new supply. FDA says to follow your doctor's instructions when possible and to see a doctor as soon as you can.
2. **Ask the pharmacist about an emergency refill.** Rules vary by state: some states let pharmacists dispense a limited emergency supply without a new prescription, often tied to a declared emergency; others do not (Healthcare Ready review). CDC advises asking your provider about a 30-day or longer emergency prescription refill.
3. **Do not change, split, stretch, skip, or ration a dose on your own.** Ask a clinician or pharmacist what to do.

### Step 2: Insulin storage when the refrigerator fails (FDA)

These are FDA storage figures. Your product label controls, and products differ.

1. **Vials and cartridges** (many products) may be left unrefrigerated at 59 °F to 86 °F for up to 28 days. Some products have different limits (for example, one product's label allows up to 12 weeks below 86 °F), so check the label or the manufacturer.
2. **Keep insulin as cool as possible,** away from direct heat and out of direct sunlight. Extreme temperatures make insulin less effective.
3. **Never freeze it.** If you use ice to keep insulin cool, avoid letting it freeze. Do not use insulin that has been frozen.
4. **Pump insulin** that has been exposed to temperatures above 98.6 °F should be thrown away (FDA). If it is your only supply, call a clinician or pharmacist first. Diluted or transferred insulin should be thrown away within two weeks.
5. **Heat- or cold-exposed vials.** Once you can get properly stored insulin, replace vials that were exposed to extreme heat or cold (FDA). FDA notes that in an emergency, insulin that got warm may still be needed, so do not throw it out and do not decide alone: call a pharmacist or your prescriber now (FDA), and if you cannot reach one, call 911 or go to an emergency department (this entry's rule). If your insulin froze, do not use it (FDA) and call a clinician or pharmacist now. If it is your only supply and it got very hot, call a clinician or pharmacist now.
6. **If insulin has been kept hotter than the label allows,** ask a clinician or pharmacist before using it. For questions about whether a product is still usable, the manufacturer's customer service line is a source FDA names.
7. **Do not switch insulin products on your own.** FDA says switching should be done under a doctor's care, with close monitoring of blood sugar.

### Step 3: Other prescription medicines

1. **If a medicine that is normally refrigerated and NOT life-sustaining has been without power for more than a day,** discard it unless the label says otherwise, and call your doctor or pharmacist for a new supply (Ready.gov). If a life depends on a refrigerated drug that has already warmed, do not decide alone whether to use it: call a pharmacist or the prescriber right away (FDA, Safe Drug Use After a Natural Disaster), and if you cannot reach one, call 911 or go to an emergency department (this repo's rule, matching 09_urban_survival.md).
2. **If you miss or run out of any prescription** (including blood thinners, seizure medicines, heart medicines, and steroids), call your prescriber or pharmacist the same day. Do not double up, skip, or cut doses to make a supply last.

### Step 4: Plan ahead

1. **Keep a supply** of prescription medicines in your emergency kit: CDC advises at least 1 week (a 7 to 10 day supply in its pregnancy guidance), plus a cooler for refrigerated medicines. Organize and protect prescriptions, over-the-counter drugs, and vitamins (Ready.gov; CDC).
2. **Write your medical needs, including prescriptions and equipment, in your family plan** (Ready.gov Make A Plan). Keep a contact list of the people who support your health with the kit (CDC).
3. **Ask your provider now** about a longer emergency supply or refill, and ask your pharmacist how emergency refills work in your state.
4. **Take your medicines and devices with you** if you evacuate.

---

## ⚠ DO NOT

- **Do NOT change or ration a prescription dose without a clinician.** That includes insulin, blood thinners, seizure medicines, heart medicines, and steroids.
- **Do NOT skip, split, or stretch doses to make a supply last.** Call a pharmacist or clinician instead.
- **Do NOT use insulin that has been frozen** (FDA). If insulin got hot and it is your only supply, call a clinician or pharmacist before throwing it away.
- **Do NOT switch insulin products without a doctor's guidance.**
- **Do NOT wait on chest pain, stroke signs, a long or repeated seizure, a very high or very low blood sugar, or fruity breath.** Call 911.

---

## Med Continuity

- **Storage:** follow the label; FDA insulin figures above.
- **Planning:** a kit with at least a week of medicines, a written list of needs, a cooler for refrigerated medicines, and the numbers for your prescriber and pharmacy.
- **Refills:** ask your pharmacist about emergency refills where you live. Rules vary by state.
- **Any dose question:** a clinician, a pharmacist, or 911 for symptoms.
