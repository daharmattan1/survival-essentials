# Burns (Thermal), With Pointers for Chemical and Electrical Burns

**This entry describes commonly taught first aid for burns when professional care is late or out of reach. It is general information, not medical advice. If you can call 911 (or your local emergency number), call first, then use this page while you wait. Reading is not training: take a hands-on first aid course.**

Survey doorway: [03_fire.md, burns first aid](../../../03_fire.md#fire-and-your-body-burns-first-aid), with the short version in [06_first_aid.md](../../../06_first_aid.md#burns). This entry follows the 03_fire guide step for step and goes a little deeper.

---

## Recognize

A burn is damage to skin (and sometimes the tissue under it) from heat, flame, hot liquid, steam, a hot object, a chemical or electricity.

- **Warning looks:** red, brown, black or white skin; swelling; blisters that may ooze [1].
- **Pain can mislead.** Extreme pain, or almost no pain, are both warning signs [4]. A deep burn can feel numb [2].
- **Deep or full-thickness burns** look white, charred or blackened, and the skin may be numb [2].
- **Size check:** a burn as big as the person's palm or bigger counts as large [2].
- **Smoke breathed in** is a separate danger: soot in the nose or mouth, singed hair, a dry cough (general practice; the sources opened say only "breathed in smoke" [2][4]). It can swell the airway even when the skin burn looks small.

---

## ⚠ RED FLAGS / EVACUATE

**Call 911 first if any one of these is true.** This list has the same 911 triggers as the 03_fire guide's step 2. It is separate from the cooling choice below: a burn can be on the cool-water track and still need 911 [1][2].

- Deep, white, charred or numb skin [1][2]
- A burn as big as the person's palm or bigger [2]
- A burn on the face, mouth, nose, hands, feet, joints or groin [1][4]
- A burn that goes all the way around a limb, the chest or the neck [1]
- A chemical or electrical burn [1][2][5]
- Smoke breathed in (soot in the nose or mouth, singed hair, dry cough; this entry's wording of the signs, general practice) [2][4]
- Signs of shock: pale, cool, clammy skin, fast weak pulse, fast breathing, confusion (signs as listed in [06_first_aid.md](../../../06_first_aid.md#shock)) [2]
- A very young or very old person [1]

If phones are down, send someone to reach help and start care without waiting (general practice).

**Get medical care even if the 911 list above does not apply** when the burn blisters [1][4], a child is burned [1], or you are not sure how serious it is [2]. A deep burn may need a tetanus booster [3]. The CDC treats burns as major wounds that call for a booster if the last tetanus shot was 5 or more years ago [8]. See [wound-infection-management.md](wound-infection-management.md) and [06_first_aid.md](../../../06_first_aid.md#wounds-infection-and-tetanus).

## Respond

### Step 1: Stop the burning and get away from the source

1. Check the scene for danger to you first: fire, hot liquid, live power, fumes (general practice).
2. If clothes are on fire: stop, drop and roll. Smother flames. A thick coat or blanket wrapped around the person also puts flames out [2].
3. Get the person away from the heat source [4].

### Step 2: Call 911 if any red flag above is true

Have someone call while you keep working. Alone, use speaker.

### Step 3: Choose the cooling track

A burn that is **deep, white or charred, as big as the person's palm or bigger, or comes with signs of shock** goes on the **large or deep track**. **Every other burn goes on the cool-water track**, including a small burn on a hand or face while you wait for help (matches the 03_fire guide, step 3 [2][5]).

#### Cool-water track (small or shallow burns)

1. Cool the burn with cool running water, not ice-cold. Aim for 20 minutes if you can, ideally within 3 hours of the burn [5].
2. Lukewarm water is acceptable if cool water is not available. With no tap, use a cool wet cloth or compress [1][5].
3. Sources differ on how long. NHS says 20 minutes. The Red Cross says 5 to 20. MedlinePlus says at least 5 to 30. Mayo says about 10. The Massachusetts sheet says "several minutes" [1][2][3][4][5]. This entry's rule: follow the 03_fire guide and aim for 20 minutes.
4. Cover loosely with a clean, dry, non-stick dressing [1][2], or cling film (plastic food wrap) laid on, not wrapped tight around the area [5].

#### Large or deep track

1. **Cool the burn itself briefly with cool (not cold) water or wet cloths. Keep the rest of the body warm, and stop if the person starts shaking** (this entry's rule, the 03_fire guide's cautious middle).
2. **Do not soak or immerse a large or deep burn in cold water.** MedlinePlus warns it can cause shock [2].
3. The sources differ. NHS and the Red Cross still advise cooling [1][5]. MedlinePlus advises against cold water on a severe burn [2].
4. Cover with a dry, clean cloth. Raise the burned part above the heart if you can [2][3].
5. To prevent shock, lay the person flat on their back and cover them. If there is no head, neck, back or leg injury, raise the feet about 12 inches [2][9]. If raising the legs hurts, leave them flat [9]. If the person is less alert or may vomit, use the recovery position (on their side) instead [10]. Details: [bleeding-control-and-shock.md](bleeding-control-and-shock.md).
6. If they are struggling to breathe, let them sit up. No pillow under the head if you suspect an airway burn [2] (sitting up is general practice).
7. Keep them warm. Large burns can bring on hypothermia, especially in children [1].
8. Check breathing. If breathing stops, start rescue breathing or CPR if you have learned how, or as the dispatcher directs [2].
9. Nothing by mouth for a severe burn [2].

### Either track

- Take off rings, watches, tight items and clothing near the burn **before swelling starts**, but leave anything stuck to the burn [1][3].
- Do not try to clean a severe burn; that can damage the skin more [1].
- Blisters stay as they are (see ⚠ DO NOT).
- For pain: an over-the-counter pain reliever may be used as its label directs. Do not give aspirin to children [2]. Pain medicine is not a reason to delay 911.

### Chemical burns (pointer only)

Call 911 for any chemical burn [2]. The 06_first_aid guide has the steps: get the person away from the chemical, remove contaminated clothing without contaminating yourself, brush off dry chemical first (away from the eyes), then flush with cool running water for 15 minutes or more. Dry lime and reactive metals such as sodium, potassium, magnesium, phosphorus or lithium are not flushed with water [6]. A chemical in the eye: flush with running water for at least 15 minutes and get help right away [6]. For a swallowed or inhaled chemical, call Poison Control: 1-800-222-1222. Do not try to neutralize the chemical without advice from poison control or a clinician [6]. See [06_first_aid.md](../../../06_first_aid.md#burns).

### Electrical burns (pointer only)

Do not touch the person with bare hands while they are still in contact with the power. Turn the power off if you can do it safely: unplug the cord, switch off the breaker, or pull the fuse. Turning off an appliance may not stop the current [7]. If you cannot, push the person away with a dry, non-conducting object such as a broom or rubber mat, never a wet or metal one. Stay at least 20 feet (6 meters) away from a person held by high-voltage lines until the power is off [7]. Then call 911, check breathing, and start CPR if needed [7]. Even a small electrical burn can hide serious internal injury and needs a clinician [1].

## ⚠ DO NOT

- **Do NOT put ice or ice water on a burn.** It can damage skin further [1][4].
- **Do NOT put butter, mayonnaise, petroleum jelly, oil, grease, creams, sprays, toothpaste or home remedies on a burn.** Greasy things trap heat [1][3][4]. (Some sources allow a clinician-approved ointment on broken skin later [2][3]. This entry's rule, matching the 03_fire guide: put nothing on it while you are giving first aid.)
- **Do NOT pop or break blisters.** The unbroken skin keeps germs out. If a blister breaks by itself, clean it gently and cover it loosely, and get medical care [3][4].
- **Do NOT pull off clothing or dirt that is stuck to the burn** [2][3][4].
- **Do NOT use sticky bandages or adhesive dressings** [1][5].
- **Do NOT soak or immerse a large or deep burn in cold water** [2].
- **Do NOT breathe, blow or cough on a burn** [2].
- **Do NOT wait to call 911** because a burn "does not look that bad". Pain level and look can mislead [1][2].
- **Do NOT touch a person who is still touching live power.** Cut the power first [7].

---

## What goes wrong

- Cooling for 30 seconds, then stopping. Run the water for the full time.
- Ice, to "cool it faster". It makes tissue injury worse.
- Butter or ointment on a fresh burn, which holds heat in.
- Rings left on until the finger swells.
- A person with a large burn left wet and cold. Keep the rest of the body warm.
- A small-looking burn on the hand, face or groin treated at home with no clinician.
- Missing smoke inhalation because the skin burn looks minor.
