# Fractures, Sprains and Splinting

**This entry describes commonly taught first aid for bone and joint injuries when professional care is late or out of reach. It is general information, not medical advice. If you can call 911 (or your local emergency number), call first, then use this page while you wait. Reading is not training: take a hands-on first aid course.**

Survey doorway: [06_first_aid.md, broken bones and sprains](../../../06_first_aid.md#broken-bones-and-sprains). This entry matches that section and goes deeper.

---

## Recognize

**Treat every muscle or bone injury as a possible fracture (a broken bone) until a clinician says otherwise** [1]. An X-ray is usually needed to tell a sprain from a break [1].

**Signs of a fracture** [1][2]:

- The person cannot or will not move the area, or cannot put weight on it
- The area looks bent, crooked, out of place or misshapen
- Pain, swelling and bruising
- Numbness, tingling or coldness at or below the injury
- A grating feeling or sound, or a pop or snap at the moment of injury
- Bleeding, or bone showing through the skin

**A sprain** is a stretched or torn ligament, one of the bands of connective tissue that join bones at a joint [4]. It causes joint or muscle pain, swelling, stiffness and bruising [3]. A fracture can look exactly like this. When in doubt, treat it as a fracture.

**A dislocation** is a joint knocked out of its normal position. A dislocated joint typically needs immediate medical treatment [4].

---

## ⚠ RED FLAGS / EVACUATE

**Call 911 or move toward emergency care now if any of these is true** [1][2][3][5]:

- The person cannot or will not move the area, or it looks bent or out of place
- The area below the injury is **cold, numb, tingling, pale, clammy or blue**
- **Bleeding, or bone through the skin** (an open fracture)
- The injury is in the **head, neck, spine or back, pelvis, hip, or upper leg (thigh)**
- Signs of shock: pale, cool, clammy skin, fast weak pulse, fast breathing, confusion
- The person is unresponsive, losing consciousness or not breathing normally
- Severe bleeding, or you cannot hold the injured part still on your own
- A joint **out of position**, or **a pop with immediate trouble using the joint** [3]
- A suspected broken rib with trouble breathing, shock, or a person who cannot be moved comfortably [1]

**Suspected spine, neck, hip, pelvis or thigh injury:** do not move the person unless you must, for example to escape fire or rising water, or to start CPR or control bleeding [1][2]. Keep them still and calm and wait for responders. Do not try to reposition a suspected spine injury [2].

If phones are down, send someone to reach help and start care without waiting (general practice). Splint (below) if you must move the person or help is far.

## Respond

### Step 1: Safety, breathing, bleeding

1. Check the scene for danger to you (general practice).
2. Check breathing. Start CPR if the person is not breathing normally and you have learned how, or as the dispatcher directs [2].
3. Control bleeding with direct pressure on a clean cloth. If bleeding is life-threatening on a limb, see [bleeding-control-and-shock.md](bleeding-control-and-shock.md) [2].

### Step 2: Keep the person still

1. Have the person rest and stay still. Avoid moving or straightening the injured part [1].
2. Check for other injuries. Look at the whole person, not just the one that hurts [2].
3. Keep the person from getting too cold or too hot [1].

### Step 3: Open wound over a break

1. Cover the wound with a sterile dressing if you have one, or a clean cloth [2].
2. Do not breathe on the wound or probe it [2].
3. **Do not push a protruding bone back in** [5]. Open fractures carry a high risk of infection, so this needs a clinician [4].

### Step 4: Check circulation, then splint if needed

**Splint only if you must move the person or help is far.** If help is minutes away, keep the person still and wait.

1. **Check circulation first.** Look at the skin, feel for warmth, ask about numbness or tingling. MedlinePlus also describes pressing firmly on the skin beyond the break: color should return in about 2 seconds after you let go. Pale or blue skin, numbness, tingling or a lost pulse means poor circulation [2]. Write down what you found, so responders know what changed (general practice).
2. **Splint in the position found.** Do not try to line up a broken bone. Do not straighten it [1][5]. The 06_first_aid guide allows one exception, as MedlinePlus does: if circulation is blocked and no trained help is near, gently move the limb toward a normal resting position [2]. The Red Cross and Mayo say simply not to straighten or realign [1][5]. This entry's rule: do not straighten unless circulation is blocked and no trained help can reach you, and stop if it hurts more or meets resistance (general practice).
3. **Splint the joint above and the joint below the break.** A splint is anything stiff held against the limb so the injured part cannot move [2][5]. Pad it [5], and tie it snugly, not tight (general practice). Put ties above and below the break, not over it (general practice).
4. **Improvised splint materials:** MedlinePlus names a rolled-up newspaper or strips of wood [2]. Other materials (general practice): a board, a rolled magazine, a stick, a rolled jacket or blanket; the other leg, or the body, as a splint; cloth strips, belts or tape for ties; a scarf or shirt as a sling for the arm.
5. **Check circulation again right after the splint, and every little while** [2]. Pale, blue, cold, numb or tingling below the splint means it is too tight or the circulation is blocked: loosen it and recheck (general practice). Swelling can tighten a splint over time (general practice).
6. **Ice**, if the skin is unbroken: a cold pack wrapped in a thin, dry cloth, 20 minutes or less, then wait 20 minutes before using it again [1]. Never ice directly on the skin [3]. Raising the limb can help [2].
7. **Shock:** lay the person flat on their back and cover them. If there is no head, neck, back or leg injury, raise the feet about 12 inches [2][6]. With a leg injury, do not raise the legs [6]. If raising the legs hurts, leave them flat [6]. If the person is less alert or may vomit, use the recovery position (on their side) instead [7]. Details: [bleeding-control-and-shock.md](bleeding-control-and-shock.md).
8. **Stay with the person.** Reassure them and watch breathing and responsiveness [1].

### Sprain care, when no red flag applies

1. Wrap a bandage snugly but not tight to limit movement [3].
2. Raise the joint above the heart [3].
3. Rest the joint for several days; protect it with a sling, brace or crutches if you have them [3].
4. Ice wrapped in cloth, not on bare skin [3].
5. Pain relievers: use as the label directs. Do not give aspirin to children [3].
6. See a clinician if swelling is not improving in 2 days, there are signs of infection, or pain lasts for weeks [3].
7. A mild sprain usually heals in about 7 to 10 days; a bad one can take several weeks [3].

---

## ⚠ DO NOT

- **Do NOT test the bone** by moving it or asking the person to bear weight; a person who can move the limb may still have a broken bone [1][2]. (Pressing on the skin beyond the break is only the circulation check in Step 4.)
- **Do NOT try to line up a broken bone or push a protruding bone back in** [1][2][5].
- **Do NOT straighten the limb** unless circulation is blocked and no trained help is near [2].
- **Do NOT move a person with a possible spine, neck, hip, pelvis or upper-leg injury** unless you must [1][2].
- **Do NOT splint only the break.** Include the joint above and below [2][5].
- **Do NOT tie a splint tight,** especially over swelling (general practice).
- **Do NOT breathe on or probe an open fracture** [2].
- **Do NOT put ice on bare skin,** or keep it on longer than 20 minutes at a time [1][3].
- **Do NOT give aspirin to a child** [3].
- **Do NOT put stress on a sprained joint** [3].
- **Do NOT try to put a dislocated joint back** (general practice). It needs a clinician [3][4].

---

## What goes wrong

- Testing the bone to "see if it is really broken."
- Splinting over a joint only, so the break still moves.
- A splint tied tight over swelling, which cuts off blood flow.
- Never rechecking the fingers or toes after splinting.
- Straightening a limb that was fine, instead of splinting it as found.
- Treating "a pop and now it will not work" as a sprain.
- Moving someone with a neck, back or hip injury to make them comfortable.

## Med Continuity

The sources opened for this entry do not cover prescription medicines and fractures, so this entry gives no medicine-specific guidance and no dosing. Tell responders about any blood thinner or other medicine the person takes. Medicine questions go to a clinician, a pharmacist or 911.
