# Anaphylaxis and Severe Allergic Reaction

**This entry describes commonly-taught field response for a severe allergic reaction. It is general information, NOT medical advice. Call 911 (or the local emergency number) for any sign of a severe reaction, even if the person improves. Reading is not training: a clinician or allergist teaches the person's own device, and a Red Cross class teaches the rest.**

---

## Recognize

Anaphylaxis (an-uh-fuh-LAK-sis) is a severe, whole-body allergic reaction. Symptoms usually start within seconds or minutes of a sting, food, medicine or other trigger, and it can be life-threatening [1][7]. CDC calls it rapid in onset and says it may cause death [9].

### Signs of a severe reaction

Any one of these means treat it as severe:

- **Breathing:** trouble breathing, wheezing, a tight throat, a hoarse voice, trouble swallowing [1][5].
- **Swelling** of the face, lips, tongue or eyes [1][2].
- **Circulation:** dizziness or lightheadedness, fainting, a fast heartbeat, confusion or a change in responsiveness [1][2][5]. AAAAI also lists pale or blue color [5].
- **Hives or flushing together with** any of the above, or with vomiting, stomach pain or diarrhea.
- **Vomiting after a sting, food or medicine,** especially with hives or faintness [1][5].

Young children may show it as a sudden change in behavior or crying [5]. Hives are not always present, so no rash does not rule it out [3].

The most dangerous symptoms are low blood pressure, breathing trouble and loss of consciousness [5].

### A mild reaction

A few itchy hives, itchy skin or a runny nose, with none of the signs above, may be a mild reaction. Watch the person closely. NIAID says that if progression or increased severity is noted, epinephrine should be given immediately [4]. If you are unsure which it is, treat it as severe and call 911 (this entry's rule, matching AAAAI: if uncertain, it is safest to call 911) [6].

---

## ⚠ RED FLAGS / EVACUATE

- **Any sign in the severe list above.** Call 911 right away [1][2][5].
- **Use the person's own prescribed epinephrine device without waiting to see if it passes.** Do not wait for symptoms to go away on their own [5][6].
- **Feeling better is not the all-clear.** Symptoms can go away and then return hours later, so emergency care is still needed after epinephrine [3][5][7].
- **Fainting, floppy, pale, cold or clammy, or not breathing normally.** This is shock or worse. Call 911, and if breathing stops, start CPR [1][2].
- **No device, no phone.** Send someone to reach help and keep the person under watch (general practice).

---

## Respond

1. **Call 911,** or have someone call. On a mobile phone, use speaker and keep helping. Do not let calling delay the epinephrine device if the person has one [2][4]. If you are alone and use the device first, call 911 immediately afterward [2][9].
2. **Ask whether they have a prescribed epinephrine auto-injector** (or prescribed nasal spray). If they do, help them use it right away, exactly as its label and the prescriber direct [1][2][3][6].
   - **No device of their own:** call 911, tell the dispatcher it may be anaphylaxis, ask whether an epinephrine auto-injector is available, and follow the dispatcher's instructions (general practice). MedlinePlus says an epinephrine injector should be used immediately if it is available [1].
   - The label shows where to inject and how long to hold it. A common injection site is the outer middle of the thigh [2]; the device label says whether it can be used through clothing (general practice).
   - Note the time you used it. Tell the responders and hand them the used device [2].
   - Whether and when to use a second device is set by the label and the prescriber. This entry gives no dose numbers or timing. Responders take over from there (general practice).
3. **Position the person:**
   - **Lying flat with the legs raised about 12 inches** is the default, if it is tolerated. Do not do this if a head, neck, back or leg injury is suspected, if it causes discomfort, or if the person is vomiting [1][3][4].
   - **Sit them up** if breathing is hard, or if lying down makes it worse. Do not put a pillow under the head of a person having trouble breathing [1][2].
   - **If they vomit,** turn the person or their head to one side so they do not choke, unless a spine injury is suspected [8].
4. **Stay with them.** Keep checking breathing and responsiveness until help arrives. Calm and reassure them. Keep them from getting too cold or too hot [1][2].
5. **If breathing stops,** start CPR as the 911 dispatcher directs [1].
6. **Stings:** if a stinger is still in the skin, scrape it off with a firm straight edge such as a credit card or a fingernail. Do not squeeze it or use tweezers, because squeezing can release more venom [1][7].
7. **Go to the emergency department even if the person looks fine.** Observation for several hours is standard, because symptoms can return [3][4][5][7].

If shock is suspected, see [`bleeding-control-and-shock.md`](bleeding-control-and-shock.md) for the shock steps, and the guide section at [06_first_aid.md](../../../06_first_aid.md#severe-allergic-reaction-anaphylaxis).

---

## ⚠ DO NOT

- **Do NOT use an antihistamine pill or any over-the-counter allergy medicine as a replacement for epinephrine or for 911.** Antihistamines act too slowly. Epinephrine, not antihistamines, should be given first when severe symptoms appear [4][6]. If the person has an OTC antihistamine at hand for mild itch, follow its label, and still call 911 for any severe sign (general practice).
- **Do NOT wait to see whether symptoms pass** before using the person's epinephrine or calling 911 [5][6].
- **Do NOT give anything by mouth** if the person is having trouble breathing [1].
- **Do NOT assume allergy shots the person has had will protect them** [1].
- **Do NOT stand or walk the person** who is faint or in shock. Keep them down or seated as above (general practice).
- **Do NOT squeeze a stinger or pull it with tweezers** [1][7].
- **Do NOT lay the person flat** if they are vomiting or having trouble breathing [1][2].
- **Do NOT leave the person alone,** and do not skip the emergency department because they improved [3][5][7].

---

## Med Continuity

If you or a family member has a prescribed epinephrine device, carry it at all times and learn how to use it before an emergency [5][7]. Allergists often advise keeping two doses on hand, since one may not be enough and a reaction can return [7]. Check the expiry date and storage on the label, and ask a clinician or pharmacist about refills. This entry gives no dosing, and changes to prescribed medicine go through a prescriber. See [`chronic-med-continuity.md`](chronic-med-continuity.md) for storage and refill basics.
