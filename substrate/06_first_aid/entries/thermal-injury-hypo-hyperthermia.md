# Thermal Injury — Hypothermia and Hyperthermia

**This entry describes commonly-taught field response for thermal injuries when professional care is unavailable. It is NOT medical advice. Seek professional care whenever possible, especially for severe hypothermia or heat stroke.**

---

## Recognize

### HYPOTHERMIA (core temperature <35°C / 95°F)

**Mild hypothermia (32–35°C / 90–95°F):**
- Vigorous shivering
- Alert and oriented, but withdrawn or confused
- Rapid heart rate and breathing
- Normal skin color (may be pale)
- Mild loss of coordination

**Moderate hypothermia (28–32°C / 82–90°F):**
- Shivering stops or becomes intermittent
- Profound confusion, apathy, poor judgment
- Slow, weak pulse (may be difficult to detect)
- Slurred speech
- Loss of fine motor control (cannot open/close hands, dropped objects)
- Possible irrationality or bizarre behavior ("paradoxical undressing" — removing clothes despite cold)

**Severe hypothermia (<28°C / <82°F):**
- No shivering (body has stopped generating heat)
- Unconsciousness or barely rousable
- Slow, barely detectable pulse (may be imperceptible for >60 seconds)
- Slow, shallow breathing (1–2 breaths/min)
- Rigid muscles (body in "pugilistic" stance)
- Fixed, dilated pupils
- Appearance of death (may look dead but is still salvageable — see DO NOT)

---

### HYPERTHERMIA / HEAT ILLNESS

**Heat cramps:**
- Painful muscle contractions in legs, arms, or abdomen
- Heavy sweating
- Core temperature normal or mildly elevated

**Heat exhaustion (core temp 37–40°C / 99–104°F):**
- Heavy profuse sweating
- Weakness, dizziness, nausea
- Pale, clammy skin
- Rapid, weak pulse
- Mild confusion or irritability
- Headache
- Preserved consciousness

**Heat stroke (core temp >40°C / >104°F) — MEDICAL EMERGENCY:**
- Cessation of sweating (dry skin, red/mottled)
- Severe confusion, altered mental status, loss of consciousness
- Rapid, strong pulse (initially), then slow/weak
- High core temperature (rectally if measured)
- Possible seizures or loss of consciousness
- May have history of heat exhaustion that progressed

---

## ⚠ RED FLAGS / EVACUATE

### Hypothermia
- **Severe hypothermia (core <28°C / <82°F):** unconscious, no detectable pulse, rigid muscles
- **Moderate hypothermia with altered mental status:** confusion, poor coordination, slow pulse
- **Cardiac instability:** any bradycardia (very slow pulse <40 bpm) or irregular heartbeat (dysrhythmia)
- **Hypothermia + drowning:** any water immersion with suspected core temp loss
- **Unable to rewarm in field:** if rewarming is not feasible or the person cannot be stabilized, evacuation is urgent

### Hyperthermia
- **Heat stroke (core >40°C / >104°F):** altered mental status, cessation of sweating, loss of consciousness
- **Rapidly rising core temperature:** any core temp approaching 40°C with worsening confusion
- **Seizures or loss of consciousness** in the heat
- **Cardiovascular instability:** severe tachycardia (>120 bpm), hypotension, syncope

**Hypothermia and heat stroke are both life-threatening. Evacuate if field stabilization is not possible.**

## Respond

### HYPOTHERMIA

**Mild to moderate hypothermia (field-manageable if core >28°C and conscious):**

1. **Move to shelter** (stop heat loss).
   - Remove from wind, rain, wet clothing.
   - Insulate from ground (place on dry material, not on bare earth).
   - Do NOT move abruptly or vigorously (rough handling can trigger cardiac dysrhythmia in severe hypothermia).

2. **Remove wet clothing** (if possible without excessive movement).
   - Wet fabric accelerates heat loss.
   - Replace with dry clothing or layers.
   - Cover the head (significant heat is lost from the head; keep it covered).

3. **Warm passively (core 32–35°C).**
   - Wrap in insulating blankets, sleeping bags, or dry clothing.
   - Allow the person to shiver (shivering generates internal heat — do NOT suppress it).
   - Provide warm (not hot) drinks if conscious and able to swallow: warm sugar/carbohydrate-containing fluids (tea, broth, diluted sports drink). **Do NOT give alcohol** (increases heat loss).

4. **Warm actively (only if core 28–32°C and rewarming resources available).**
   - **Warmed blankets / hot water bottles:** place against trunk (armpits, groin, neck), NOT limbs. Limb rewarming can cause "afterdrop" — peripheral vasodilation draws heat from the core.
   - **Warm immersion (bath):** if available and core 28–32°C, immerse in warm water (40–42°C / 104–108°F, not hot). This is core-direct rewarming. Only if full bath is available; do NOT use hot water.
   - **Continue passive measures:** insulation + monitoring.

5. **Monitor vital signs:** heart rate, breathing, consciousness. Hypothermia victims can deteriorate suddenly or recover surprisingly well — keep close watch.

6. **Evacuation:** evacuate if core temperature is not rising, if the person cannot be kept warm, or if conditions worsen.

**Severe hypothermia (<28°C, unconscious, no detectable pulse):**
- **Assume NOT dead.** Severe hypothermia victims have recovered after appearing lifeless for extended periods (one case: resuscitated after 6+ hours without a pulse).
- **Move minimally and gently.** No rough jostling.
- **Begin passive rewarming** (insulation, shelter).
- **Check pulse for ≥60 seconds** (hypothermia slows the heart so much that a weak pulse is hard to detect). If no pulse after 60 seconds of careful assessment, begin CPR.
- **Evacuate for active rewarming (ECMO / extracorporeal rewarming in hospital).** Only a hospital can fully rewarm a severely hypothermic person; field core rewarming is insufficient.
- **Do NOT assume death in the field.** Transport the person to professional care.

### HYPERTHERMIA / HEAT ILLNESS

**Heat cramps:**
1. Move to shade.
2. Stretch the cramped muscle gently.
3. Drink water or diluted electrolyte solution (sports drink, or DIY: 1/4 tsp salt + sugar in 1 L water).
4. Cramps usually resolve in minutes; if severe or persistent, monitor for heat exhaustion progression.

**Heat exhaustion (field-manageable):**
1. **Move to shade or cool environment immediately.**
2. **Cool the body:** apply water (spray, pour, or sponge) to exposed skin (face, neck, armpits, groin). The evaporation cools the body.
   - **Fan the person** (increases evaporative cooling).
   - **Wet cloths or ice packs** to major vessels: neck, armpits, groin (fastest core cooling).
3. **Rehydrate:** drink water slowly in small sips (if conscious). If nausea, drink smaller volumes more frequently.
4. **Monitor:** if symptoms resolve quickly (within 30 min) with cooling + hydration, further rest may be sufficient. If symptoms persist or worsen → escalate to heat stroke protocol.

**Heat stroke (medical emergency):**
1. **Cool aggressively and evacuate immediately.**
   - Apply water (spray, immerse if possible) to the entire body.
   - Use ice packs on neck, armpits, groin (major blood vessels near skin).
   - Fan vigorously (maximize evaporative cooling).
   - **If water immersion available (pool, stream, tub):** immerse the person in the coldest water available to accelerate core cooling. For exertional heat stroke, cold or ice-water immersion is the gold standard — do not withhold it. Keep the airway clear and monitor continuously.
2. **Fluid intake:** if conscious and can swallow, give small sips of water. If unconscious, do NOT force fluids (risk of aspiration).
3. **Monitor mental status and vital signs** continuously. Seizures or cardiac instability may occur.
4. **Evacuate for IV fluids and monitoring.** Heat stroke progresses to organ failure and death without aggressive hospital care.

## ⚠ DO NOT

### Hypothermia

- **Do NOT rapidly rewarm frostbite or extremities if refreezing is possible.** Frostbite thawed and refrozen causes worse tissue damage. If a frostbitten limb may be refrozen during evacuation, leave it frozen until professional care can maintain warmth.
- **Do NOT use extreme heat (hot water >42°C, fires, heating pads held against skin).** Burns and afterdrop (peripheral vasodilation causing core temp drop) worsen the outcome.
- **Do NOT massage or rub the limbs.** This damages frostbitten tissue and moves cold blood toward the core.
- **Do NOT remove a hypothermia victim from shelter or move roughly.** Cardiac dysrhythmia can be triggered by jostling.
- **Do NOT give alcohol.** Alcohol dilates blood vessels, increasing heat loss.
- **Do NOT declare someone dead without trying to rewarm them.** Severe hypothermia victims can recover after appearing lifeless.
- **Do NOT delay evacuation for someone with severe hypothermia or heat stroke.** These require hospital-level care (active rewarming / IV cooling).

### Hyperthermia

- **Do NOT give salt tablets without water.** Salt alone worsens dehydration.
- **Do NOT delay cooling for heat stroke — speed of core cooling is the priority.** For exertional heat stroke, cold-water or even ice-water immersion is the gold standard and most effective method when available; do not withhold it. The main precautions are: keep the airway clear (unconscious patients should not be left face-down in water), and do not interrupt cooling to wait for gentler options. If immersion is not possible, apply as much ice/cold water to the skin as available.
- **Do NOT give large fluid boluses** (forcing rapid drinking). Small sips prevent nausea and aspiration risk.
- **Do NOT continue physical exertion if heat exhaustion or heat stroke is suspected.** Rest in shade is mandatory.
- **Do NOT attribute confusion or loss of consciousness to exhaustion alone.** Heat stroke must be ruled out; assume the worst if mental status changes in the heat.

## Med Continuity

**Hypothermia + chronic medications:**
- Beta-blockers (slowing the heart) worsen hypothermia outcomes.
- Antidepressants (SSRI, tricyclics) can impair heat regulation.
- If the person is on such medications and becomes hypothermic, lower your threshold for evacuation.

**Heat stroke + chronic medications:**
- Diuretics and anticholinergics (antihistamines, anticholinergic antidepressants) impair sweating and heat loss.
- Stimulants (caffeine, pseudoephedrine, amphetamines) increase metabolic heat production.
- Thyroid meds increase metabolism.
- If on any of these and showing heat illness signs, escalate to evacuation sooner.
