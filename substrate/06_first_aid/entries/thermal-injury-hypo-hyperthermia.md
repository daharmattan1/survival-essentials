# Thermal Injury — Hypothermia and Hyperthermia

**This entry describes commonly-taught field response for thermal injuries when professional care is unavailable. It is NOT medical advice. Hypothermia and heat stroke are emergencies: call 911 (or the local emergency number) and get the person to care.**

---

## Recognize

### HYPOTHERMIA (body temperature below 95 F)

You usually cannot measure body temperature in the field. Go by signs, and call 911 whenever you suspect it [1][3].

- Shivering, then shivering that stops as it gets worse [2]
- Pale, cold skin [1][2]
- Confusion, drowsiness, indifference, poor thinking [1][2]
- Weakness, loss of coordination [1]
- Slowed breathing or heart rate [1]
- Glassy stare; stiff, rigid muscles; unresponsive [2]

The Red Cross marks confusion or indifference, a glassy stare, loss of shivering, slow or absent breathing, unresponsiveness, a slow or absent heartbeat and rigid muscles as needing immediate emergency care [2]. Outside a hospital, the Wilderness Medical Society guideline says diagnosis and treatment are guided by how the person looks and acts, because body temperature readings are hard to take accurately [4].

### FROSTBITE
Numbness; skin that feels cold to the touch, looks waxy, or is white, yellow, blue or red [6]. Blisters, black skin or signs of hypothermia need emergency care [6].

### HEAT ILLNESS

**Heat cramps:** muscle cramps and pain or spasms in the abdomen, arms or legs [11].

**Heat exhaustion:** heavy sweating, paleness, muscle cramps, tiredness, weakness, fast or weak pulse, dizziness, headache, fainting, nausea or vomiting [7].

**Heat stroke, an emergency:** body temperature above 103 F, red hot skin that may be dry, rapid strong pulse, dizziness, confusion, or unconsciousness [7][8]. MedlinePlus adds slurred speech and seizures and gives above 104 F [9]. The sources differ, so do not wait for a thermometer: confusion in the heat is enough (this guide's judgment). Do not rule out heat stroke because the person is still sweating; CDC/NIOSH lists profuse sweating as possible [11].

---

## ⚠ RED FLAGS / EVACUATE

### Cold
- **Any suspected hypothermia: call 911** [1]. CDC: below 95 F means get medical attention immediately [3].
- **Sleepiness or confusion** in a cold person [1][2].
- **Shivering that stops, glassy stare, rigid muscles, slow breathing, unresponsive** [2].
- **Frostbite with blisters, black skin or signs of hypothermia** [6].
- **Unconscious and not breathing:** start CPR and keep warming (see Respond) [1][3].

### Heat
- **Heat stroke:** confusion, slurred speech, fainting, seizure or unconsciousness in the heat. Call 911 or get to a hospital immediately [7][9][10].
- **Heat exhaustion that gets worse, or does not improve in about an hour, or with vomiting:** get medical care right away [8].
- **Any seizure or loss of consciousness in the heat.**

## Respond

### HYPOTHERMIA

1. **Call 911 (or have someone call).** Stay with the person [1].
2. **Gently get them out of the wind, rain and off the cold ground** into shelter or a warm room [1][3]. Handle gently with as little exertion as possible. Rough handling can trigger a dangerous heart rhythm in a badly chilled person [1][4].
3. **Insulate:** cover the head and neck; put insulation under them and a vapor barrier (such as plastic sheeting) around them if you have it [1][2][4].
4. **Wet clothes:** once they are in a warm, sheltered place, take off wet or tight clothes, dry the person, and put on dry, loose clothes, with a hat [1][2][3]. The Wilderness Medical Society guideline says wet clothes should not come off until the person is in a warm environment [4].
5. **Warm gently, center first:** use your own body heat, skin to skin under dry layers. Warm the chest, neck, head and groin [1][3]. The Wilderness Medical Society guideline puts active heat on the armpits, chest and back for mild cases and the upper trunk for worse cases [4]. Keep the person lying flat or seated, not walking about [4].
   - **Sources differ on warm bottles and pads.** The Red Cross allows heating pads or warm water bottles when care is far away, wrapped in thin dry cloth and never against bare skin. MedlinePlus says no heating pad or hot water. The safer choice (this guide's judgment): use body heat and dry blankets first; if you use a warm bottle, wrap it, keep it on the trunk, and keep it warm, not hot [1][2].
6. **Drinks:** only if the person is alert, shivering, and can swallow easily: warm, sweetened drinks with no alcohol or caffeine. The Wilderness Medical Society guideline allows warmed high-calorie liquid or food in this case. Nothing by mouth for anyone who is drowsy, confused or unconscious [1][2][3][4].
7. **Keep checking** breathing and responsiveness until help arrives [2].
8. **If unconscious and not breathing normally:** start CPR (compression-only if that is your training) and use an AED if one is available. CDC says start CPR even if the person appears dead, and keep warming them [2][3].

### FROSTBITE
1. Treat hypothermia first. Get out of the cold; remove wet clothing and tight jewelry [5][6].
2. **Rewarm only if the part can stay thawed.** If it may refreeze, do not thaw it; wait until you reach a warm, safe place [5][6].
3. If medical help is far away, soak in warm (not hot) water. Test it with your own hand. The sources give different ranges (MedlinePlus 104 to 108 F for 15 to 30 minutes; the Red Cross 99 to 104 F, and 100 to 105 F in its FAQ), so use about 99 to 104 F, the cooler range, because numb skin burns easily (this guide's judgment, matching `06_first_aid.md`). CDC says water warm to the touch [3][5][6]. If you cannot soak the part, warm it slowly in warm air or against warm skin [6].
4. Afterward, wrap loosely in dry sterile dressing with gauze between fingers or toes [5][6]. Move the thawed part as little as possible [5].

### HEAT ILLNESS

**Heat cramps:** rest in a cool place and give sips of water unless the person is nauseated [8]. CDC/NIOSH suggests water plus a sports drink for cramps [11]. For a homemade electrolyte drink, use the WHO ORS recipe in `dehydration-and-diarrhea.md` (1 liter of treated or boiled-and-cooled water, 6 level teaspoons of sugar, 1/2 teaspoon of salt) [12]; do not invent a different salt mix (this guide's rule). Seek medical care if cramps last longer than 1 hour [8].

**Heat exhaustion:**
1. Move to a cooler place, preferably air-conditioned or shaded. Loosen or remove extra clothing [8][9].
2. Cool wet cloths or cool water on the skin, with cold compresses on the neck, groin and armpits, and fan the person [9].
3. If alert, give small sips of water or a sports drink [7][8][9].
4. Seek medical care right away if they vomit, get worse, or are not better in an hour [8].

**Heat stroke:**
1. **Call 911 or get the person to a hospital immediately** [7][8].
2. Move to a cool, shaded area and take off outer clothing [7][11].
3. **Cool rapidly.** The Red Cross preferred method is immersion up to the neck in cold water for 15 minutes or until the person is no longer confused. If you cannot, use ice packs, cold wet towels, a cold shower, or misting and fanning [10]. CDC/NIOSH also says a cold-water or ice bath if possible, with cold wet cloths or ice on the head, neck, armpits and groin [11]. Keep the face above water and do not leave the person alone in water (general practice).
4. Keep cooling for 20 minutes, or until their condition improves or help arrives [10].
5. **Give nothing by mouth** to a confused, vomiting or unconscious person. NWS says do not give fluids to a person with heat stroke [8][9].

## ⚠ DO NOT

### Cold
- **Do NOT use direct heat** on a cold person (hot water, heating pad against skin, heat lamp, fire, stove). Rapid rewarming can cause dangerous heart rhythms [1][2].
- **Do NOT give alcohol.** It feels warming but increases heat loss [1][2]. No caffeine [2].
- **Do NOT assume a person found motionless in the cold is dead** [1][3].
- **Do NOT rub or massage frostbitten skin,** hold it near a fire, stove or heating pad, or break blisters [3][5][6].
- **Do NOT thaw frostbite that may refreeze** [5][6].
- **Do NOT walk on frostbitten feet** unless you must [3].
- **Do NOT leave wet clothes on the person** once in a warm, sheltered place [2][4].
- **Do NOT give a drink to a drowsy, confused or unconscious person** [3][4].

### Heat
- **Do NOT give salt tablets** [8][9][11].
- **Do NOT give fever medicines** such as aspirin or acetaminophen for heat illness [9].
- **Do NOT give alcohol or caffeine** [9].
- **Do NOT give anything by mouth** to a confused, vomiting or unconscious person [8][9].
- **Do NOT wait for a thermometer** to treat confusion in the heat as heat stroke (this guide's judgment).
- **Do NOT delay 911 or cooling** for heat stroke [7][8][10].

## Med Continuity

The sources opened for this entry do not cover how chronic medicines change cold or heat risk, so this entry makes no medicine-specific claims and gives no dosing. If the person takes regular medicines or has a heart, kidney or other chronic condition, tell responders, and take medicine questions to a clinician, a pharmacist or 911.
