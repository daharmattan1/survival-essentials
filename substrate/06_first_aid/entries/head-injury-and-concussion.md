# Head Injury and Concussion

**This entry describes commonly taught first aid for head injuries when professional care is late or out of reach. It is general information, not medical advice. If you can call 911 (or your local emergency number), call first, then use this page while you wait. Reading is not training: take a hands-on first aid course.**

Survey doorway: [06_first_aid.md, head injury and blisters](../../../06_first_aid.md#head-injury-and-blisters). This entry matches that section and goes deeper. No medicine dosing appears here.

A concussion is a brain injury from a bump, blow or jolt to the head or body. You cannot see it, and you cannot judge how serious it is by looking. **Do not try to judge the severity yourself** [2].

---

## Recognize

After any bump, blow or jolt to the head, look for these concussion signs [1][3][4][5]:

- Dazed, stunned or confused about what just happened
- Headache or dizziness
- Nausea or vomiting
- Trouble remembering or concentrating, or answering questions slowly
- Clumsy, unsteady on the feet, or slowed reactions
- Sensitivity to light or noise
- Mood, behavior or personality change
- Loss of consciousness, even briefly [3]

Symptoms may not show up right away. They can take hours or days to appear [1][3][4].

**Babies, toddlers and preschoolers** may only act different: crying and irritability more than usual, needing to be held more, refusing to nurse or eat, sleeping more or less than usual, seeming unsteady on their feet [1].

---

## ⚠ RED FLAGS / EVACUATE

**Call 911 now** if the person has any of these after a head injury [1][3][4][5][6]:

- **A headache that gets worse and does not go away** [1]
- **Repeated vomiting.** The CDC says "repeated" [1]; the survey guide says nausea or vomiting. This entry's rule, matching the guide: any vomiting after a head injury is a reason to call, and vomiting more than once is a clear red flag [1][3].
- **A seizure** (shaking or twitching) [1][3]
- **One pupil larger than the other, or double vision** [1]; vision change [4]
- **Confusion that is increasing, not recognizing people or places, restlessness, agitation or unusual behavior** [1][4]
- **Very sleepy, cannot be woken, or cannot stay awake** [1][5]
- **Loss of consciousness**, even briefly [3]
- **Slurred speech, weakness, numbness or poor coordination;** cannot move an arm or leg [1][3][5]
- **Clear fluid from the nose or ears.** This can mean a skull fracture [6]. Also bruising around the eyes or behind the ears, or a deformity of the skull [6]. (Only the Victorian government source opened for this entry names these signs.)
- **Severe head or face bleeding** [3]
- **A severe headache or stiff neck** [3] (MedlinePlus puts this under "get medical help right away")
- **Stops breathing, or is confused, drowsy or unconscious** [3]
- **Any suspected serious head or neck injury** [3]
- **Symptoms that get worse at any time** [5]

**Infants and toddlers:** also call 911 for a baby who will not stop crying and cannot be consoled, or will not nurse or eat [1].

If phones are down, send someone to reach help and start care without waiting (general practice).

**Suspected neck or spine injury.** A head injury may mean a neck injury too [6]. Suspect the neck when the person is unconscious, or after a hard fall, a vehicle crash or a diving or sports collision (general practice). Other signs: neck pain, tingling or no feeling in the limbs, cannot move the limbs, an unnatural body position, loss of bladder or bowel control [6].

---

## Respond

### Step 1: Safety and help

1. Check the scene for danger to you (general practice).
2. Call 911, or have someone call, for any red flag above [4].
3. Check airway and breathing. If breathing stops, start rescue breathing or CPR if you have learned how, or as the dispatcher directs [3].

### Step 2: Neck and spine first

**If a neck or spine injury is possible, or the person is unconscious but breathing:**

1. **Keep the head, neck and back in line.** Hold the head with a hand on each side so it does not twist or roll [3][6]. A rolled towel or T-shirt around the neck can help keep the head still [6].
2. **Do not move the person** unless they are in immediate danger, for example from fire, traffic, rising water or a collapsing structure [3][6]. If they must be moved, move them as one unit, keeping head, neck and back in line (general practice).
3. **If they vomit,** roll the head, neck and body together onto the side, keeping the head and neck held in line [3].
4. **Do not remove a helmet** if you suspect a serious head injury [3].
5. If the airway is blocked, carefully tilt the head back and support it [6]. Only the Victorian (Australia) source opened for this entry gives this step; keeping the airway open comes before protecting the spine (general practice).

### Step 3: Keep them still and warm, and watch

1. Have the person rest and stay still. Leave them in the position found until EMS arrives [4][7].
2. Stop whatever activity they were doing [4].
3. Keep checking their breathing and how alert they are [4].
4. Keep them from getting too cold or too hot [4].
5. Care for other injuries and for shock (see [bleeding-control-and-shock.md](bleeding-control-and-shock.md)) [4].
6. Reassure them that help is coming [4].
7. Apply ice wrapped in a towel to a swollen area [3].
8. **Bleeding:** press a clean cloth firmly on a head wound; if blood soaks through, add another cloth on top and do not remove the first [3]. If you suspect a **skull fracture** (skull deformity, fluid from the nose or ears), do not press on it and do not remove debris: cover the wound with sterile gauze [3][6].

### Step 4: After a head knock when no red flag is present

No fixed length of watching time came from the sources opened. These steps follow what they say.

1. **Stay with the person and keep rechecking.** Signs can show up hours or days later [1][3][4]. Look again for every red flag above. Any red flag at any time means call 911 [5].
2. **Get a clinician to evaluate them**, even when they seem fine. This is the survey guide's rule, and the Red Cross says the same if 911 was not called [4].
3. **Sleep:** do not assume they must stay awake. The Red Cross says sleep is generally considered fine only after a healthcare provider has evaluated the person, and the provider may say to wake them from time to time to check [4]. Without a clinician available, follow the next two lines (this entry's rule).
4. If you cannot reach a clinician, do not leave the person alone, and keep checking how alert they are. If they become hard to wake, confused, or any red flag shows up, call 911 (general practice built on [1][4]).
5. **No return to activity the same day.** A child, teen or adult with a suspected concussion stays out of sports and strenuous activity on the day of the injury and until a healthcare provider clears them [2][5]. Returning is a medical decision [2]. AANS warns that a second blow before full recovery can cause acute and often fatal brain swelling, and that the second blow need not be strong [5].
6. **Do not drink alcohol or use street drugs for 48 hours after a serious head injury** [3].
7. For headache or other symptoms, **no painkillers while you are still watching for a serious injury**, because they can hide the signs [4]. Questions about medicine go to a clinician or pharmacist, or follow the label.

---

## ⚠ DO NOT

- **Do NOT move a person with a possible neck or spine injury** unless they are in immediate danger [3][6][7]. Do not try to reposition the head or neck.
- **Do NOT let the head or neck twist or roll.** Keep head, neck and back in line [3][6].
- **Do NOT give painkillers for a headache** while watching for serious injury; they can mask signs [4].
- **Do NOT let them return to sports or strenuous activity the same day** [2].
- **Do NOT judge severity yourself** [2]. Do not decide "it is just a bump" and leave.
- **Do NOT shake a dazed person** [3].
- **Do NOT remove a helmet** if serious injury is suspected [3].
- **Do NOT wash a deep or heavily bleeding head wound,** and do not pull out an object sticking out of it [3].
- **Do NOT press on a possible skull fracture** [3].
- **Do NOT pick up a fallen child** who shows any sign of head injury [3].
- **Do NOT leave the person alone** after a head injury (general practice).
- **Do NOT give anything by mouth** to someone who is drowsy, confused, vomiting or unconscious (general practice).

---

## What goes wrong

- A person "walks it off" and keeps playing or working, then gets worse.
- Everyone watches for the first hour, then stops. Signs can appear later.
- Painkillers dull the headache, so the warning sign disappears.
- A well-meaning helper sits the person up or turns their head, and a neck injury gets worse.
- A baby who is only fussy and not feeding is not taken seriously.
- A second hit before the first has healed.

---

## Med Continuity

The sources opened for this entry do not cover prescription medicines and head injury. This entry gives no medicine-specific guidance and no dosing. Tell responders about any blood thinner or other medicine the person takes, because it matters after a head injury. Medicine questions go to a clinician, a pharmacist or 911.
