## The Principle

**STOP** — Stop, Think, Observe, Plan — is the conscious interruption that prevents panic from spending your calories, your clear thinking, and your small window of control. Panic is a neural state, not a moral failing. When it happens, a three-to-four-step deliberate pause *stops the cascade* and returns you to agency. Without STOP, panic-driven thrashing (running in circles, hyperventilation, random decisions) consumes energy and kills options.

## Why It Holds

Under stress (lost, injured, cold, afraid), the amygdala hijacks the prefrontal cortex. Blood moves to large muscles, away from the reasoning brain. Breathing accelerates. You experience tunnel vision and time distortion. Your *instinct* is to run, fight, or freeze — all useful for a predator in the immediate past, all maladaptive for the injury you're managing now.

STOP works because it **explicitly engages the reasoning brain**. A few seconds of conscious, voiced deliberation — even whispered to yourself — interrupts the panic cascade and re-engages the prefrontal cortex. The act of **observing** (naming what you see, hear, feel) is grounding. Naming what you can *plan* (even something small: gather dry wood, drink water) restores agency and signal to the parasympathetic nervous system that you are not in immediate mortal danger.

Most survival failures are not technical — people *know* how to light a fire or find water — they are neural: panic, despair, or decision-paralysis leading to inaction or reckless action.

## How To Apply

**Stop.** Physically pause. Sit. Even if you are in motion or in pain, **three seconds of stillness interrupt the reflex.**

**Think.** Say it aloud if possible (whisper if not). Ask:
- Am I in immediate danger *right now* (fire, flood, collision)?
- Do I have an injury that needs first attention?
- What time of day is it? How much daylight remains?

**Observe.** Catalog what you have:
- Water/shelter materials nearby?
- Weather? Temperature, wind, visibility?
- Can I signal? (whistle, mirror, smoke, noise)
- Am I alone or with others?

**Plan.** Write a micro-plan (steps 1–3 maximum):
1. Example: "Get off the exposed ridge into the tree line; find a dry spot; make a fire lay by dark."
2. Not: "I will survive." (Too abstract, impossible to act on.)
3. The plan is permission to start: it answers "what do I do right now?" — the only time that matters.

**Apply STOP whenever:**
- You realize you are lost.
- Panic starts (shortness of breath, racing thoughts, urge to run).
- You reach a decision point (shelter here or keep moving? treat this injury or move to find help?).
- You are in a group and morale is dropping.

Repeat STOP every few hours or every time the situation changes.

## Common Failure

**Skipping STOP entirely.** People who are trained or overconfident often proceed straight to action without the cognitive reset. In a real emergency, overconfidence becomes panic when the first plan fails, and panic-driven thrashing consumes resources.

**STOP-ing but not planning.** People sometimes think the observation phase is enough ("I see the shelter, I see the water, I see we're alive") but do not anchor it to action. A plan without action is rumination, not survival. The output of STOP must be "do this next."

**Planning something too ambitious.** "Find a helicopter" or "walk out by sunset" are abstractions. The plan must be concrete and achievable within the next 30–60 minutes: "Stack wood. Make a platform off the wet ground. Get a fire started." Small wins restore agency and create momentum.

**Repeating panic.** If STOP-ing, observing, and planning happen but the group then ignores the plan or argues, panic cycles back. STOP is not a one-time event; it is a repeatable circuit-breaker that may need to run every few hours.
