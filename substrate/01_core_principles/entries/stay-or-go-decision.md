## The Principle

**Lost outdoors, the default is to stay put and be found.** Move only for an immediate threat to your life, or when your own list of conditions (below) is met. At home in a disaster, **an official evacuation order overrides everything**: go. Without an order, stay if your building is intact and you can keep warm or cool; leave when it turns hazardous.

Both halves are one rule: **decide before you are cold, tired and scared, and write the decision down for whoever comes looking.**

This entry is general information. Local officials, 911 dispatchers and search teams outrank it.

## Why It Holds

- **Searchers look where you said you would be.** The National Park Service (NPS) says: "Changing location will make it difficult for authorities to find you. Move only if there is an immediate threat to life" [2]. Plan for a night out (this guide's judgment; see [`01_core_principles.md`](../../../01_core_principles.md#lost-outdoors-stay-put-and-be-found)).
- **Moving while lost makes it worse.** The Forest Service: "Do not move at all until you have a specific reason to take a step," and "Do not walk aimlessly" [1]. The Army manual, in the same spirit: "Don't move just for the sake of taking action" [12].
- **Weather and dark raise the cost of walking.** Olympic National Park: in rain, snow and fog "you may be safer if you stay put rather than attempt to travel" [4].
- **An evacuation order exists because officials can see what you cannot.** Ready.gov: "Always follow the instructions of local officials" and "Leave early enough to avoid being trapped by severe weather" [8].
- **Staying inside can be the protection.** Ready.gov says there are situations when it is best to stay where you are, such as large amounts of debris in the air or authorities saying the air is badly contaminated [9].

## How To Apply

### Lost outdoors: the decision list

Do [STOP](survival-priorities-stop.md) first. This list matches the navigation guide ([`07_navigation.md`](../../../07_navigation.md#if-you-think-you-are-lost-right-now)).

1. **Name the last place you were sure of,** such as a junction, a lake or a sign. Note the time and the direction you have walked since (this guide's judgment).
2. **Take the first line that fits.**
   - **Immediate threat to your life** (rising water, fire, rockfall, lightning on a ridge): move, but only as far as the nearest better spot, then start this list again (this entry's rule, matching the navigation guide). NPS: move only if there is an immediate threat to life [2].
   - **Stay put** if it is near nightfall, you are hurt or worn out, or you are not very, very confident of the route [1]. Bad weather such as rain, snow or fog is also a reason to stay [4]. **This is the default.**
   - **Retrace your steps** only if it is daylight, you are not hurt, and you can see the way back, such as the trail or your own tracks (these conditions are this guide's; NPS says only "if it is safe to do so, retrace your steps" [2]). If fog hides the way back, stay put. If you have not reached a known spot in about 30 minutes, stop and stay put (this entry's rule, matching the navigation guide).
   - **Use map and compass** only if you can place yourself on the map within one landmark, there is enough daylight to finish, and nobody is hurt (this entry's rule, matching the navigation guide). Method: [`07_navigation.md`](../../../07_navigation.md).
3. **If you are on a trail, stay on it** [1].
4. **Water is not a way out.** The Forest Service calls following a drainage or stream downhill "a very last resort," and adds "this could be very dangerous" [1]. Do not treat it as a plan. Cold water risks are in [`07_navigation.md`](../../../07_navigation.md#rivers-and-streams-following-and-crossing).

Where the NPS ("if it is safe to do so, retrace your steps") [2] and the Forest Service ("if you are not very, very confident... stay put") [1] differ, take the more cautious choice: stay (this entry's rule).

### Staying: be findable

1. **Call 911 if you have service.** Give GPS coordinates from your phone, the trail or area where you started, your last known location, what you can see, your clothing, your equipment and any medical conditions [2]. After you give your location, stay where you told them (this entry's rule, matching [`08_communication.md`](../../../08_communication.md#order-of-action)).
2. **Save the phone.** There may be no coverage. Searching for a signal drains the battery, so use airplane mode when you are not calling [3].
3. **Shelter and warmth.** Find a place out of sun, rain and snow. Add every layer. Do not build a fire if fire risk is high or it is wildland fire season [2]. Shelter basics: [`04_shelter.md`](../../../04_shelter.md) and [`emergency-shelter-types`](../../04_shelter/entries/emergency-shelter-types.md).
4. **Signal.** Blow a whistle often: three blasts, a break, then three blasts again [13]. Flash a flashlight in the SOS pattern and a mirror at aircraft, and lay out shelter material or rocks in a shape visible from the air [2]. Brightly colored clothing laid out in a clearing helps too [4]. Full signal set: [`signaling-visual-and-audible`](../../08_communication/entries/signaling-visual-and-audible.md).
5. **Conserve energy and wait** [2].
6. **A personal locator beacon (PLB) is for grave and imminent danger.** NOAA: "406 MHz emergency beacons should never be activated unless you are in grave and imminent danger" [6]. A PLB "can only be activated manually" [7]. Registering is required and free [6][7]. Example from the communication guide: hurt, cannot walk out, cold, no way to call. Details: [`08_communication.md`](../../../08_communication.md#personal-locator-beacons-and-satellite-messengers). Do not use it because you are merely uncomfortable or unsure of the trail [7]: it "needlessly diverts SAR resources."

### Children: "Hug a Tree"

NPS teaches children: "Find a tree, hug it, and stay put." If there is no tree, use a park bench or a big rock; "the important thing is to stay in one place." Keep warm and dry by adding layers and a hat or hood, and make yourself big by blowing a whistle, waving your arms or flashing a flashlight so searchers can find you [2]. The NPS program is aimed at ages 5 to 10 [5].

Teach it before the trip, and give a child a whistle (general practice). If a child in your group goes missing, follow the group steps in [`group-survival-and-roles`](group-survival-and-roles.md).

### Moving: what to leave behind

Moving is the exception, so leave a trail for searchers.

- **A note** at the spot you leave: the date and time, who is with you, who is hurt, the direction you are heading and why (general practice). Weigh it down where it will be seen (general practice).
- **Keep to the trail or a clear landmark line** you can describe (general practice).
- **Tell 911 if you reach service,** and say you moved (general practice).
- **Leave a trip plan before you go** with someone who is not going: trailhead, route, return time, vehicle and parking spot, and group size [1][3]. NPS says to set a turnaround time if your trip is taking longer than planned [3]. This is what makes staying put work.

### At home or in a vehicle

- **Evacuation order: go.** Leave early. Follow recommended routes; "Do not take shortcuts, they may be blocked." Keep a full tank if an evacuation seems likely. "Do not drive into flooded areas." Take your emergency supply kit. Take pets, but only service animals may be allowed in public shelters. "Leave a note telling others when you left and where you are going" [8].
- **Shelter in place** (stay inside) when authorities say so, or when there is debris in the air or the air is badly contaminated [9]. Bring everyone inside, lock doors, close windows, air vents and fireplace dampers, turn off fans, air conditioning and forced-air heating, and go to an interior room with few windows if possible [9]. Ready.gov: "Use common sense and available information to assess the situation and determine if there is immediate danger," and "stay informed and follow the instructions of local authorities." Local authorities may not be able to give you information at once [9].
- **Stranded in a car in a winter storm: stay in it.** NWS: leaving "you will become disoriented quickly in wind-driven snow and cold." Run the engine about 10 minutes each hour for heat. Before every run, clear snow from the exhaust pipe: NWS says this is to avoid gas poisoning [10]. The gas is carbon monoxide, which you cannot see or smell. **The clear tailpipe is the carbon monoxide control.** NWS also says to open a window a little; a cracked window does not protect anyone and is not a fix (this guide's rule; see [`vehicle-as-shelter`](../../04_shelter/entries/vehicle-as-shelter.md)). Tie a bright cloth to the antenna or door, use the dome light at night while the engine runs, and raise the hood once the snow stops [10]. Never sleep with the engine running, and burn nothing inside the car (this guide's rule; see [`04_shelter.md`](../../../04_shelter.md#vehicle-as-shelter)).
- **Home with the power out: stay** if the building is intact, you can keep warm and no order has been given (this guide's judgment). **Go** when ordered, or when you smell gas, a carbon monoxide alarm sounds, there is fire or flooding, you cannot stay warm or cool, or someone's powered medical device cannot run. The trigger table is in [`09_urban_survival.md`](../../../09_urban_survival.md#when-to-leave). Entry: [`power-outage-bug-in`](../../09_urban_survival/entries/power-outage-bug-in.md).
- **Before it happens:** agree on a meeting place and how you will reach each other if separated [11].

## Common Failure

- **"I'll go a little further to check."** Each check moves you off the last place you were sure of (see the navigation guide).
- **Walking at dusk "to get a signal" or find a road.** The sources say to stay at nightfall, when hurt, and when worn out [1].
- **Following water downhill because it looks like a path.** The Forest Service says last resort, and dangerous [1].
- **Staying put but not being findable.** Staying does not guarantee a fast rescue. It makes you findable if you also keep warm, dry and visible.
- **Leaving without telling anyone** where the trip is going [1]. No trip plan means nobody knows to look.
- **Waiting past an evacuation order** because the house still looks fine.
- **Leaving a working shelter in the middle of a storm** to find something better, or leaving a stranded car on foot.
- **Moving after you told 911 where you are,** without telling them.
- **Using a PLB for a non-emergency,** or not registering it [6][7].
