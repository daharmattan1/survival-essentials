## The Principle

**A group lives or dies on a few plain habits: one person makes the final call, everyone has a buddy, you count heads on a schedule, and the group never splits without a reason.** Give each person a job they can do. Watch each other for cold, heat and exhaustion. Look after the people who need more help: children, older adults, people with disabilities.

This entry adapts what trained disaster volunteers and park agencies teach. It is general information, not a script. The sources below were written for volunteer teams, hiking parties and families, so where this entry stretches them to your situation it says "(general practice)".

## Why It Holds

- **Clear leadership prevents arguments at the worst time.** The FEMA Community Emergency Response Team (CERT) manual tells teams to develop "a chain of command and roles that are known by all." Each member "has only one person that he or she takes direction from" [1].
- **Safety of the people doing the work comes first.** CERT: "rescuer safety is the number one concern," and the leader sets priorities "based on the team's capability and training" [1]. A rescue that adds a casualty has failed (general practice).
- **Stress degrades thinking.** CERT lists difficulty making decisions among the cognitive reactions to a disaster. It also describes a stage (CERT's "Heroic Phase") where "activity level may be high" but "actual productivity is often low," and "the capacity to assess risk may be impaired, and injuries can result" [1]. This is why a group needs roles and a pause before acting (see [`survival-priorities-stop`](survival-priorities-stop.md)).
- **Sharing the work helps the mood.** CERT: "Sharing the workload and emotional load can help defuse pent-up emotions" [1]. The FAA advises splitting equipment and tasks into responsibility areas, such as signaling, first aid and water [5].
- **Cold and heat hurt people who do not notice it.** The Army manual says to use a buddy system to watch for heat injury, and to check each other's faces for frostbite [6]. Olympic National Park lists the early signs of hypothermia to watch for in your group: poor judgment, lethargy, shivering and clumsiness [4].

## How To Apply

### 1. Pick one leader and say so out loud

1. **Name one person to make the final call** when the group disagrees (this guide's judgment, matching CERT's one-chain-of-command rule [1]). In a family or a hiking party, it is whoever knows the ground, the plan or the first aid best (general practice).
2. **The leader can change.** CERT lets the first person at the staging area start as leader and pass the job on as others arrive [1]. Hand it over openly if someone is better placed (general practice).
3. **The leader's first questions,** adapted from CERT's list of leader objectives: What is the problem? What can we do, and how? Who is going to do what? Write down what you decide [1].
4. **Keep it small enough to lead.** CERT's span of control is five people per supervisor, with two to seven acceptable [1]. A bigger group splits into sub-groups, each with its own leader who reports to the main leader (adapted from CERT, which requires "an identified leader" in each unit [1]).

### 2. Buddy pairs and headcounts

1. **Everyone gets a buddy,** and buddies stay together. CERT: "Two people will 'buddy up'" [1]. Pair the less able with someone strong (general practice).
2. **Buddies check each other.** Cold: check each other's face for frostbite [6]. Heat: "If someone complains of tiredness or wanders away from the group, he may be a heat casualty" [6]. Cold or wet and someone gets clumsy, sluggish or irrational: treat it as possible hypothermia [4][6]. Sleepy, confused or slurring speech in the cold: call 911 right away (this guide's rule; see [`06_first_aid.md`](../../../06_first_aid.md#cold-hypothermia-and-frostbite) and [`thermal-injury-hypo-hyperthermia`](../../06_first_aid/entries/thermal-injury-hypo-hyperthermia.md)). Heat illness steps: [`06_first_aid.md`](../../../06_first_aid.md#heat-illness).
3. **Count heads on a schedule,** not when someone feels uneasy: at every stop, when you move, and at dark (general practice). CERT names accountability as a core duty of the leader and each unit [1].
4. **Name a meeting spot** in case of separation. Ready.gov: "Establish a family meeting place that's familiar and easy to find" and "know how you'll contact one another and reconnect if separated" [7].

### 3. Give out jobs by what people can do

- **Match jobs to ability and training,** not to who talks loudest [1]. Typical jobs: signaling, first aid, water, shelter, warmth, lookout (the first three are the FAA's examples [5]; the rest are general practice).
- **Give uninjured, upset people something to do.** CERT: "Get uninjured people involved in helping... Give them constructive jobs to do such as organizing supplies," which is "especially effective for survivors who are being disruptive" [1].
- **Rotate hard jobs.** CERT leaders rotate people for breaks or new duties, and encourage rest, food and fluids [1].
- **Do not send a rescuer after the lost.** See the lost-member steps below.

### 4. Keep the group together and move at the slowest pace

NPS: "Always stay together," put the slowest hiker near the front [3], and "plan your route and rate of travel around the weakest member" [4]. Stay-or-go rules for the whole group: [`stay-or-go-decision`](stay-or-go-decision.md).

**If someone in your group becomes lost, NPS says:** stop the activity and gather the group; go to the last known place and wait there as long as it is safe; "DO NOT go looking for the person off trail"; call 911 if you have cell service, with the person's last known location, clothing, equipment and medical conditions and your group's meeting spot; if you cannot call, turn the group around and go to the nearest ranger station or visitor center, because other group members could become lost [2].

### 5. Children, older adults and people with disabilities

- **Children.** Think about different ages when you plan [7]. Keep a child with a named adult (general practice). CERT's Psychological First Aid is "Listen, Protect, Connect": listen, answer questions "simply and honestly," tell them what is being done to keep everyone safe, and help them reconnect with family [1]. When families are separated, anxiety about those not present "may detract from the attention necessary for immediate problem solving", so reunite people early (general practice). Children may hide in small places such as cabinets [1]. Teach a lost child to "find a tree, hug it, and stay put" [2].
- **Older adults.** Ready.gov: build a support network of people who can help in an emergency; plan for transportation if you need help to evacuate; plan how assistive devices will come with you [8]. Check on older neighbors who live alone (see [`09_urban_survival.md`](../../../09_urban_survival.md)).
- **People with disabilities and access or functional needs.** Plan accessible transportation; carry printed information for first responders if you have a communication disability; plan for assistive devices, including an extra battery for a power wheelchair; and plan for a service animal's food and supplies [9]. Ask the person what they need instead of guessing (general practice). Medicines and equipment come with them [7]; medication continuity: [`chronic-med-continuity`](../../06_first_aid/entries/chronic-med-continuity.md).

### 6. Conflict and stress inside the group

1. **Expect it.** CERT lists freeze, flight, fight, fright and faint as normal responses to stress [1]. Anger and frustration lead to impulsive choices (see [`psychology-and-will-to-live`](psychology-and-will-to-live.md)).
2. **Do not take it personally.** CERT: volunteers "cannot know--and should never assume to know--what someone is thinking or feeling," and should not take survivors' surface attitudes personally [1].
3. **The leader hears each side, decides, and moves on** (general practice). Stop the argument, assign a task, revisit the disagreement later.
4. **Make talking normal.** CERT teams are asked to "establish a culture of acceptance" and let people describe what they saw and felt [1].
5. **Watch for people who withdraw or cannot function.** CERT says to refer anyone who shows signs of being suicidal or unable to care for themselves to EMS or mental health professionals as soon as possible [1]. Call or text 988 (see [`psychology-and-will-to-live`](psychology-and-will-to-live.md)).

## Common Failure

- **No named leader.** Everyone decides, nobody commits. Pick one.
- **Splitting up to cover more ground.** NPS says to keep the group together and not to search off trail [2][3].
- **Pace set by the strongest.** Plan around the weakest member [4].
- **Counting only when someone seems missing.** Count on a schedule (general practice).
- **Heroics.** Rescuer safety is the first priority; an untrained volunteer who becomes a casualty adds to the problem (CERT's own history notes volunteers died in the 1985 Mexico City earthquake response) [1].
- **Ignoring the quiet one.** Withdrawal and clumsiness are signs, not manners [4][6].
- **Letting an upset person sit idle.** Give them a job [1].
- **Treating people with extra needs as an afterthought.** Plan for them first [7][8][9].
