## Goal & When To Use

Once you have a good site and a ground barrier, build only as much **enclosure** as the weather demands. The principle is counterintuitive: **a small, well-insulated shelter your body can heat beats a large, drafty one.**

The key decision trees:

- **Rain or snow?** Build a roof that sheds water. Wind? Reduce openings. Extreme cold? Minimize volume and trap heat. Mild weather with insects? Thin cover + airflow.
- **Time/energy tradeoff.** A debris hut takes 30 minutes and is crude but effective. A full lean-to takes 1–2 hours. A tarp shelter takes 5 minutes if you have cordage. Choose based on how much daylight and calories you can afford to spend.

## Steps

### 1. Debris Hut (Universal, Minimal Materials)

**When to use:** Cold or wet weather; rain or snow; you have no tarp; you have time before dark.

**Build:**

1. Find a ridgepole (a fallen log or branch, 8–12 feet long, 2–4 inches diameter). Place it horizontally between two trees (or prop it on low rocks/logs) roughly 3–4 feet high.
2. Lean branches against the ridgepole at a steep angle (60–70 degrees from ground), creating an "A" frame. Pack them tightly — no gaps for wind or rain to leak through.
3. Bury the low end of the branches in your leaf/debris pile; cover the entire structure with leaves, bark, and debris until it's 12–18 inches thick.
4. Leave a small opening facing *away* from prevailing wind (usually the entry hole). Make it just large enough to crawl through (fist-sized).
5. Inside, lay a ground barrier (branch lattice + leaf bed), and stuff leaves around the inside edges.

**Thermal rating:** Very good (dense debris traps air; small entrance limits convection loss). Often stays 20–30 °F warmer than ambient.

**Time:** 30–45 minutes with good materials nearby.

**Failure modes:** Branches collapse under snow load (add more bracing inside); gaps leak wind (pack tighter); entrance gets buried in snow (keep clearing it).

---

### 2. Lean-To (Simple, Weather-Dependent)

**When to use:** Rain or sun but mild temperature; you have time; you need ventilation (hot day, high humidity).

**Build:**

1. Lay a horizontal ridgepole (or strong branch) between two trees, 4–6 feet high.
2. Lean branches or saplings at a ~45-degree angle from the ridgepole down to the ground, creating a slanted roof.
3. Thatch or cover the roof with bark, leaves, branches, plastic sheeting, or whatever sheds water. Overlap material so rain runs down (not into gaps).
4. Leave the front open or semi-open for air circulation. The back and sides block wind.
5. Set your ground barrier (leaf bed + branch lattice) against the back wall.

**Thermal rating:** Moderate (open front means wind and convection loss, but airflow prevents stuffiness). Works well in mild rain/sun. Poor in extreme cold or snow.

**Time:** 45–90 minutes.

**Failure modes:** Roof leaks at seams (overlap material better); wind cuts through the open front (add side walls or face away from wind); rain blows in at an angle (angle the roof more steeply, add a wind-break panel on the side the rain comes from).

---

### 3. Tarp/Plastic Shelter (Fastest, Requires Cordage)

**When to use:** You have a tarp or large plastic sheet; moderate rain or sun; you have cordage/rope and anchor points (trees, stakes).

**Build (A-Frame):**

1. Tie one edge of the tarp between two trees (or to stakes) at ~4 feet high.
2. Tie the opposite edge to the ground 6–8 feet away, creating a tent-like ridge.
3. Stake down all four corners and edges.
4. Leave openings (usually the long ends) for entry and ventilation, oriented *away* from wind.
5. Place your ground barrier underneath.

**Build (Lean-To):**

1. Tie the top edge of the tarp to a ridgepole 4–5 feet high.
2. Stake the bottom edge to the ground 6–8 feet out.
3. Leave the sides open or rolled up depending on wind/rain.

**Thermal rating:** Moderate to good (waterproof, reflects some body heat; still has convection loss through openings). Wind-facing side needs to be sealed if cold.

**Time:** 5–10 minutes.

**Failure modes:** Wind lifts or tears the tarp (add more stakes, secure all corners); rain pools on top (angle the ridge so water runs off the ends, not into the shelter); condensation builds inside (leave ventilation gaps at the ends).

---

### 4. Snow Cave (Extreme Cold/Snow, High Effort)

**When to use:** Heavy snow cover, sub-freezing temperatures, no other materials; you have enough daylight and calories.

**Build:**

1. Find or create a drift at least 4–5 feet deep (you need clearance inside to lie down).
2. Dig a horizontal entrance tunnel at ground level, slightly angled upward into the main chamber to trap cold air at the entrance.
3. Carve out an interior sleeping platform 18–24 inches higher than the entrance (cold air sinks; you sleep above it).
4. Smooth the ceiling (rough ice crystals melt and drip; smooth surfaces shed meltwater down the walls instead).
5. Poke a small vent hole in the ceiling (prevents CO₂ buildup and allows meltwater to run out instead of dripping on you).
6. Line the sleeping platform with branches and leaves (ground barrier still applies).

**Thermal rating:** Exceptional (snow is excellent insulation; you stay 30–50 °F warmer than ambient). The trade-off is it's labor-intensive.

**Time:** 1–2 hours, depending on snow depth and your energy.

**Failure modes:** Cave collapses if you dig too large a chamber or if you're below an overhanging cornice (rockfall/avalanche risk). Ceiling melts from body heat and drips on you (smooth ceiling, angle it, poke vent). CO₂ buildup (leave vent open).

---

### 5. Vehicle / Natural Shelter (Existing Structure)

**When to use:** You're stranded in a car, or you find a cave, rock overhang, or dense thicket that already offers protection.

**Build:**

1. **In a vehicle:** Stay inside if weather is extreme (rain, cold, wind). Crack a window for air (unless it's freezing and you need to preserve interior heat). Use the metal frame and seats as a thermal mass; huddle away from windows. In extreme heat, crack all windows and shade interior.
2. **Cave or overhang:** Use as-is, but check for occupants (animals, loose rocks). Build a ground barrier and a fire-reflector wall if fire is possible. Avoid sleeping directly at the entrance (wind/rain exposure); retreat into the shelter depth.
3. **Dense brush / dead-falls:** If a cluster of fallen trees / heavy brush is present, burrow into it and supplement with a ground barrier and debris pile.

**Thermal rating:** Highly variable (structures provide protection but may have poor insulation; caves stay around 50 °F year-round). Vehicles are moderate-to-good if sealed; caves excellent if deep enough.

**Time:** 0–15 minutes (you're adapting, not building).

**Failure modes:** Vehicle engine cooling causes rapid heat loss (insulate with debris around the engine area, huddle in the passenger cabin). Cave collapse or unexpected occupants (inspect before entering; stay near exit). Brush-shelters can collapse under snow load (pack more snow on top to stabilize, or reinforce with branches).

## Failure Modes & Fixes (General)

### Shelter Collapses Under Snow Load
- **Recognize it.** Roof sags inward; cracks appear in the structure.
- **Fix (immediate).** Evacuate and rebuild or reinforce with interior bracing (prop a sturdy branch from the inside up to the roof).
- **Fix (design).** Steeper roof angles (>60 degrees) shed snow better. Debris huts with a strong ridgepole handle snow better than lean-tos.

### Wind Shreds the Shelter (Rips Tarp or Collapses Branches)
- **Recognize it.** Loud wind noise; shelter rocks; you feel drafts.
- **Fix (immediate).** Seal gaps with debris; add windward bracing; reduce the opening facing the wind.
- **Fix (design).** Build in a more protected site (lee of a ridge, in thick brush, not on open ground).

### Interior Becomes Waterlogged
- **Recognize it.** Sleeping area is damp; your insulation bed feels heavy and wet.
- **Fix (immediate).** Bail water if there's a pool; improve drainage by digging a sump pit inside (low point where water collects), separate from your sleeping area. Increase roof slope to shed water faster.
- **Fix (design).** Angle the entire shelter so water runs *out* of the entrance, not toward the sleeping area. Dig a small drainage ditch outside the entrance.

### Shelter Too Small / Claustrophobic
- **Recognize it.** Panic or hyperventilation when fully inside; feel suffocated.
- **Fix.** Leave the entrance open or large enough to see out. A small opening is enough to enter/exit; psychological comfort matters more than the extra R-value.
- **Fix (design).** Build a low, wide shelter (not tall/narrow) so you don't feel trapped.

### Shelter Too Warm, Condensation Drips on You
- **Recognize it.** Interior is humid and warm (70+ °F); ice crystals form on the ceiling and melt, dripping water.
- **Fix (immediate).** Poke a vent hole in the ceiling or roof; increase air exchange by partially opening the entrance.
- **Fix (design).** In milder weather, favor ventilation over insulation. A breeze through the shelter beats condensation soaking you.

## Summary: Match Shelter to Conditions

| Condition | Best Shelter | Time | Notes |
|-----------|--------------|------|-------|
| Cold rain | Debris hut | 30–45 min | High R-value; slow-build |
| Mild sun/breeze | Lean-to or tarp | 45 min–5 min | Airflow reduces heat stress |
| Extreme cold (<20 °F) | Snow cave or debris hut | 1–2 hr / 30 min | Trade time for insulation |
| Stranded vehicle | Stay in vehicle | 0 min | Insulate with debris; vent |
| Wet/snow combo | Debris hut | 30–45 min | Steep roof sheds; dense cover |
