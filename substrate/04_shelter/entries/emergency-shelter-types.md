## Goal & When To Use

Once you have a good site (see `site-selection-and-hazards.md`) and a ground bed (see `insulation-and-ground-barrier.md`), build only as much **enclosure** as the weather demands. The Army manual says a shelter should be large enough and level enough to lie down in, and should "require minimal effort to build" (ATP 3-50.21, paras 6-9, 6-12). A small shelter is easier to warm (general practice).

> **WARNING: nothing burns inside any shelter or vehicle.** No camp stove, lantern, charcoal, heater, candle or fuel gel in a tent, tarp shelter, debris hut, snow shelter or vehicle. Carbon monoxide (CO) is a gas you cannot see or smell, and people who are asleep can die from it before they have symptoms. In Georgia in 1999, campers died of CO in tents; opening tent flaps, doors or windows "is insufficient to prevent build-up" of CO. A vent or an open flap is **not** a fix. Pack bedding and clothing instead of relying on fuel-burning heat (CDC MMWR 1999; CDC CO page). The Army manual and Mount Rainier NPS allow burning devices in snow shelters with ventilation; this entry follows the stricter CDC rule. See also `03_fire.md`.

Choosing among shelters:

- **Rain or snow?** Build a roof that sheds water. **Wind?** Put the back of the shelter into the wind (ATP 6-16). **Extreme cold?** Seal every opening except ventilation holes (ATP 6-39).
- **Time and energy.** The Army manual says a poncho lean-to takes "only a short time" and that natural-material shelters "usually require the most time and energy" (ATP 6-16, 6-23). It gives no build times. Choose based on the daylight and calories you can spare.
- **Thunderstorm.** None of these shelters protects you from lightning. A tent "offers NO protection" (NWS). Only a building with plumbing and electricity, or a hard-topped metal vehicle with the windows closed, counts (see `site-selection-and-hazards.md`).

## Steps

### 1. Debris Hut (Universal, Minimal Materials)

**When to use:** Cold or wet weather; you have no tarp; you have time and enough material before dark. The Army manual calls it one of the best shelters for warmth and ease of construction (ATP 6-38).

**Build (ATP 6-38):**

1. Make a tripod with two short stakes and a long ridgepole (the pole running the length of the shelter), or anchor the ridgepole to a tree at about waist height.
2. Prop large sticks along both sides of the ridgepole to make wedge-shaped ribbing: wide enough for your body and steep enough to shed moisture.
3. Lay finer sticks and brush crosswise on the ribs. This lattice keeps the insulating material from falling through into the sleeping area.
4. Pile light, dry, soft debris (grass, pine needles, leaves) over the ribs until it is **at least 3 feet thick. The thicker the better.** Work from the bottom up, overlapping like shingles, until you cannot see through it from inside (ATP 6-22).
5. Put a **2-foot layer** of insulating material on the floor inside.
6. At the entrance, pile insulating material you can drag in behind you to close it, or build a door. Keep the entrance only as big as you need to crawl through (the manual gives no size).
7. Add branches or shingling material over the debris so a storm does not blow it away.

**Warmth:** Good, because thick debris traps air and a small opening limits heat loss (general practice). No measured temperature gain is claimed.

**Failure modes:** Debris too thin (if you can see the frame from inside, add more); frame too weak to carry the weight of debris plus rain or snow (the manual says the framework must support the covering and precipitation, ATP 6-12); entrance not closed; wet debris insulates poorly (general practice). Do not build under a dead tree or in a drainage.

### 2. Lean-To (Natural Materials)

**When to use:** Mild, wet or windy weather; you have time and no tarp. It is open on one side, so it holds less heat than a debris hut (general practice). Poor choice in extreme cold or heavy snow.

**Build (ATP 6-31, 6-32):**

1. Find two trees (or upright poles) about 7 feet apart. Tie a 7-foot pole, about 1 inch thick, between them at waist to chest height. If no trees stand, make a bipod from Y-shaped sticks.
2. Lean five to eight poles about 10 feet long, about 1 inch thick, against the horizontal pole. Put the back of the lean-to into the wind.
3. Crisscross saplings or vines over the beams.
4. Cover with brush, leaves, pine needles or grass, starting at the bottom and working up like shingles.
5. Put straw, leaves, pine needles or grass inside for bedding (see `insulation-and-ground-barrier.md`).

Roof pitch for shelters meant to shed rain or snow: 40 to 60 degrees, with the cover stretched tight (ATP 6-12, 6-19).

**Failure modes:** Roof leaks at seams (overlap material bottom to top); wind through the open front (back to the wind, block the sides with brush, a pack or other gear, ATP 6-16); rain blown in at an angle (add a side wall on the weather side, general practice). Keep fire out of the shelter and keep any fire at the distance given in `03_fire.md`.

### 3. Tarp / Poncho Shelter (Fastest, Requires Cordage)

**When to use:** You have a poncho, tarp or large plastic sheet, cordage, and anchor points.

**Poncho lean-to (ATP 6-16):** needs a poncho, 7 to 10 feet of rope, three stakes about 1 foot long, and two trees or poles 7 to 10 feet apart.

1. Check the wind. The back of the lean-to goes into the wind.
2. Tie a rope to each top corner and to the trees about waist high.
3. Stake the bottom edge with sharpened sticks through the grommets.
4. Tie a drip stick (about 4 inches) to each rope about 1 inch from the grommet so rain does not run down the rope into the shelter.
5. Put brush, a pack or other gear at the sides. Put leaves or pine needles inside to reduce heat loss to the ground.

**Poncho tent (ATP 6-17):** two 5- to 8-foot ropes tied at about knee height to two trees 7 to 10 feet apart, the poncho stretched tight, both sides staked. Low and protected on two sides, with less usable space.

**Staking (ATP 6-43, 6-44):** sharpened sticks 1 inch thick and 12 inches long, driven at about a 45-degree angle. On rocky or frozen ground, tie to a heavy branch or rock set on the ground instead. In loose sand or dirt, tie to a stick or rock and bury it in a hole.

**Pitch:** 40 to 60 degrees, wrinkle-free and tight. Water pools in wrinkles and leaks. Do not touch the cover while water runs over it (ATP 6-12).

**Warmth:** Keeps rain and wind off; heat loss through open sides remains (general practice).

**Failure modes:** Wind lifts or tears the cover (more stakes, all corners secured, general practice); rain pools (cover not taut); condensation (the manual lists ventilation as reducing internal perspiration, ATP 6-12). Plastic and nylon melt and burn near a fire.

### 4. Snow Cave (Deep Drift, High Effort, Real Hazards)

**When to use:** Heavy snow, no other materials, enough daylight and strength. The Army manual calls it a most effective dwelling because of snow's insulation, but warns that it takes time and energy and that you will get wet while building it (ATP 6-25). Dig slowly and shed layers so you do not sweat.

**Where NOT to dig:**

- **Not in avalanche terrain, and not below it.** Mount Rainier NPS: "Never travel or camp in an avalanche zone." Most slab avalanches start on slopes of 30 to 50 degrees and then run into lower-angled terrain below (Avalanche.org). Do not dig at the foot of a steep slope (this guide's judgment).
- **Not under or near a cornice.** A cornice is an overhanging mass of wind-deposited snow on a ridge. It can break off "suddenly and unexpectedly" and can trigger slab avalanches on slopes below; Avalanche.org advises limiting your exposure to slopes below cornices. This is a falling-snow and avalanche hazard, separate from the collapse of the cave itself.
- **Not in or near a tree well** (the hollow of loose snow around a tree trunk). People who fall in can be trapped upside down and suffocate; in research experiments 90% of participants could not rescue themselves (Deep Snow Safety, specialist source). The Army manual describes a tree-pit shelter (ATP 6-28); this entry does not teach it because of this hazard (this guide's judgment).

**Build (ATP 6-25 to 6-27):**

1. Find a drift about 10 feet (3 m) deep to dig into. With no drift, pile snow into a mound big enough to dig out.
2. Hollow the chamber with an **arched roof**. Arching is stronger and lets meltwater drain down the walls.
3. Build the **sleeping platform higher than the entrance**, separated from the walls or with a small trench between, so melting snow does not wet you. The roof must be high enough to sit up on the platform.
4. Keep the walls and ceiling **at least 1 foot (30 cm) thick**.
5. Block the entrance with a snow block or other material.
6. Install a **ventilation shaft** through the roof. Keep it open and check it often; in snowy or windy weather you may have to go out and dig out the vents (Mount Rainier NPS). The vent is for breathing only. It does not make burning anything inside acceptable.
7. Put branches or other insulation on the platform (see `insulation-and-ground-barrier.md`).
8. Mark the shelter with something bright so others can see it (Mount Rainier NPS), and keep your shovel inside (general practice). When you leave, collapse the cave so no hidden pit or sharp drop remains (Mount Rainier NPS).

**Warmth:** Snow insulates well (ATP 6-25). No temperature gain is claimed here. The Army rule of thumb: if you cannot see your breath, the snow shelter is too warm and should be cooled down to stop melting and dripping (ATP 6-39).

**Failure modes (the cave itself):** Walls or roof thinner than 1 foot, or a flat roof, can collapse or drip (build arched, keep the thickness). Sweating while digging soaks your insulation. A blocked vent. If any part of the roof sags, leave and do not re-enter (general practice). Never burn anything inside.

### 5. Vehicle / Natural Shelter (Existing Structure)

**When to use:** You are stranded in a car, or you find a cave, overhang or dense thicket that already offers protection. The Army manual lists vehicles, caves and rock outcroppings as immediate-action shelters (ATP 6-13).

**In a vehicle in cold weather. Stay with it.** A car is a windproof, waterproof, visible shelter (Ready.gov: "If trapped in your car, then stay inside").

1. **Keep the exhaust pipe clear of snow whenever the engine runs, and check it before each start.** A blocked tailpipe lets CO build up quickly inside the vehicle (NWS: "Clear snow from the exhaust pipe"; CDC tailpipe page).
2. **Run the engine about 10 minutes each hour** for heat (NWS).
3. **Crack a window a little** while the engine runs (NWS). A cracked window and a clear pipe lower the risk. They do not remove it (this guide's judgment).
4. **Never sleep with the engine running** (this guide's rule). People who are asleep can die from CO before they have symptoms (CDC CO page). Set a phone alarm and shut the engine off before anyone dozes. If drifting snow keeps covering the pipe and you cannot clear it, leave the engine off.
5. **Burn nothing in the vehicle** (CDC MMWR 1999).
6. Between engine runs, insulate yourself with seat cushions and extra clothing (ATP 6-45), and keep everyone in one cabin (general practice). Eat and drink, and avoid overexertion (NWS).

**Cave or overhang:** The Army lists caves and rocky crevices as natural shelters (ATP 6-14). Check for loose rocks or dead limbs overhead and for snakes, ticks, scorpions and stinging ants before settling (ATP 6-11). Build a ground bed. **In a thunderstorm, cave entrances are dangerous and small overhangs can let lightning arc across the gap** (NWS). Going well into a cave "increases your safety somewhat," but even deep caves can be struck, so no cave counts as a lightning shelter.

**Dense brush / deadfall:** Fallen trees with thick branches and clumps of bushes are natural shelters (ATP 6-14). Check overhead for dead limbs first, and add a ground bed.

**Warmth:** Highly variable. This entry makes no claim about cave or vehicle temperatures.

**Failure modes:** Running the engine all night; a tailpipe buried by drifting snow; a stove or heater lit "just for a bit"; loose rock overhead in a cave or overhang.

## Failure Modes & Fixes (General)

### Shelter Collapses Under Snow or Rain Load
- **Recognize it.** Roof sags inward; cracks appear in the structure.
- **Fix (immediate).** Get out and rebuild or add interior bracing (general practice).
- **Fix (design).** The framework must carry the weight of the covering plus precipitation and resist gusts (ATP 6-12). The Army recommends a pitch of 40 to 60 degrees for shelters that shed rain and snow.

### Wind Shreds the Shelter
- **Recognize it.** Loud wind noise; the shelter rocks; you feel drafts.
- **Fix (immediate).** Seal gaps with debris; add bracing on the windward side; reduce the opening facing the wind (general practice).
- **Fix (design).** Build behind natural screens such as fallen trees, rocks or large trees (ATP 6-12), not on open ground.

### Interior Becomes Waterlogged
- **Recognize it.** Sleeping area is damp; the bed feels heavy and wet.
- **Fix (general practice).** Move the bed to higher ground, dig a small sump away from the sleeping area, and angle the shelter so water drains away from the bed.

### Condensation or Meltwater Drips on You
- **Recognize it.** Moisture or melting snow drips from the ceiling.
- **Fix.** In a snow shelter, the Army rule is that if you cannot see your breath it is too warm and should be cooled (ATP 6-39). Keep the ceiling arched so melt runs down the walls (ATP 6-25). In other shelters, keep the ventilation openings (ATP 6-12). A ventilation opening is not permission to burn anything inside.

### Feeling Trapped or Panicked Inside
- **Recognize it.** Panic or a feeling of suffocation when fully inside.
- **Fix (general practice).** Get out and breathe; make the shelter a little larger or keep the entrance partly open. Keep the breathing vent clear.

## Summary: Match Shelter to Conditions

| Condition | Best Shelter | Notes |
|-----------|--------------|-------|
| Cold rain | Debris hut, or poncho / tarp lean-to | Debris hut: at least 3 ft of debris, 2-ft floor layer, door. Tarp pitch 40 to 60 degrees, taut |
| Mild sun or breeze | Lean-to or tarp | Airflow reduces heat stress (general practice) |
| Deep snow, extreme cold, a shovel and daylight | Snow cave | Drift about 10 ft deep; walls and ceiling at least 1 ft; arched roof; platform above entrance; vent shaft. Not in avalanche terrain, not below a cornice, not near a tree well. Burn nothing |
| Stranded vehicle | Stay in the vehicle | Tailpipe clear and checked before each start; engine about 10 min per hour; window cracked; never sleep with the engine running; burn nothing |
| Wet and snow together | Debris hut | Steep ribs shed water; thick, dry debris; strong frame |
| Thunderstorm | Building with plumbing and electricity, or hard-topped metal vehicle with windows closed | No tent, lean-to, debris hut or cave entrance protects against lightning |
