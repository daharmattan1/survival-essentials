## Goal & When To Use

Use this for a night outside in the cold, or in an unheated shelter. The aim is simple: lose less heat than your body makes. Cold does not need to be extreme. Hypothermia (a dangerously low body temperature) can start above 40 F if rain, sweat or cold water has chilled you [7].

Build the system from the bottom up: ground layer, dry sleep clothes, cover, then a waterproof outer layer. Pick the site first ([`site-selection-and-hazards.md`](site-selection-and-hazards.md)) and read [`insulation-and-ground-barrier.md`](insulation-and-ground-barrier.md) for the bed. Survey guide: [stop heat loss](../../../04_shelter.md#stop-heat-loss-ground-wet-and-wind).

> ⚠ **NEVER BURN A STOVE, HEATER, GRILL, LANTERN OR CANDLE INSIDE A TENT OR SHELTER TO STAY WARM. CARBON MONOXIDE (CO) KILLS PEOPLE WHILE THEY SLEEP.**
>
> CO is a gas you cannot see or smell. People who are asleep can die before they have symptoms [10]. In Georgia in 1999, six people died in two zipped tents. One had a propane stove burning inside; the other had a charcoal grill brought in for warmth [6]. Opening tent flaps, doors or windows is insufficient to prevent CO build-up [6]. CDC's answer is to bring enough bedding and clothing, and to eat and drink extra [6]. Keep any vent open for breathing only. Full rule: [`03_fire.md`](../../../03_fire.md#fire-indoors-in-tents-and-in-vehicles-the-carbon-monoxide-rule), [`generator-and-heater-co-prevention.md`](../../09_urban_survival/entries/generator-and-heater-co-prevention.md).

## Steps

### 1. Ground first

1. FM 21-76 says that when you are at rest you can lose as much as 80 percent of your body heat to the ground [1]. It also says never sleep directly on the ground [1].
2. Under you, best to worst: a sleeping pad, then seat cushions or extra clothing, then dry ferns, grass or boughs [12]. Make natural material at least 12 in. thick, because it compresses. Fluff it between uses and add more as it flattens [12].
3. On wet ground, put a waterproof sheet under the bed (general practice). NPS describes a pad as both cushion and a way to keep moisture from building up under your body [2].

### 2. Keep the space small

FM 21-76 calls it a fatal error in cold shelters to make the space so large that it steals body heat rather than saving it. Block the entrance with a pack or other gear to keep heat in and wind out, FM 21-76 also says to ventilate an enclosed shelter, so keep a small gap for air [1].

### 3. Eat and drink in the evening

1. Your body burns a lot of calories keeping you warm. NPS Yosemite: eat often, drink warm liquids, and do not run out of fuel [3]. CDC's camping report says to bring extra calories and fluids [6].
2. No alcohol. It makes you lose body heat [7][8].
3. FM 21-76 advises against drinking a lot just before bed, because getting out of a warm bag in the night costs rest and heat [1]. Drink earlier in the evening.
4. If you cook, cook outdoors and finish before you get in. Nothing burns in the sleeping space ([`03_fire.md`](../../../03_fire.md)).

### 4. Dry clothes for sleep

1. NPS: "Before turning in for the night, change into dry clothes" [2]. Keep one dry set just for sleep, and keep it from getting damp during the day (general practice).
2. **Change only somewhere warm and sheltered** (this entry's rule; AFP: wet clothes should not be removed until the person is in a warm environment [9]). In the open, wet clothes stay on: wrap dry insulation and a waterproof layer over them, then put on dry layers when you are under cover.
3. Avoid cotton. It holds water [4][8]. Wool and synthetic clothing keeps you warm when wet [3].
4. Damp socks or mittens can dry against your body overnight if you place them unfolded near you [1].

### 5. Layers, loose

1. Red Cross layering: a base layer that wicks sweat, a middle layer that traps warm air, and an outer layer against wind, rain or snow [8]. FM 21-76: several thin layers beat one thick layer, because of the air between them [1].
2. Wear them loose. Tight clothing and footwear reduce circulation and the air between layers [1][7]. Loosen tight boots or socks at night.
3. Cover your head. Hat, socks and mittens (mittens are warmer than gloves) [8]. For sleeping NIA adds long underwear and a head covering [7].
4. Before you get in: take off a layer if you are sweaty, so you start dry and do not chill when sweat cools (FM 21-76 "avoid overheating") [1].

### 6. Cover

1. Use a sleeping bag matched to the weather [2]. Keep any down dry: wet down loses much of its insulation [1].
2. With no bag, use every blanket, coat and spare layer. FM 21-76 also describes a homemade bag: dry leaves, pine needles or moss between two layers of cloth [1].
3. Add a waterproof layer over you in rain or wet snow (general practice). The Wilderness Medical Society treats insulation plus a vapor barrier (a waterproof layer) between a cold person and the ground and weather as key [9].
4. Share heat. People huddling together keep each other warmer [3].

### 7. Get in, and keep it dry inside

1. Do not breathe into the bag: that puts a lot of moisture in it, which makes you colder in the long run [2]. Keep your nose and mouth outside the covers (general practice).
2. Keep water next to you so it does not freeze, and do not fill a bottle to the top, so it can slosh [1].
3. Nothing burns inside. See the warning above.

### 8. Check through the night

1. Fluff and add to the bed as it compresses [12]. Waking cold at 3 AM is often a flattened bed.
2. Watch each other for the signs below. Shivering, fumbling hands and slow thinking are the early ones [5].

## Vapor and condensation

- **Where the wet comes from:** your breath, your sweat, and snow or frost melting off clothing. FM 21-76 says to brush snow and frost off before entering a shelter, and to adjust clothing so you do not sweat [1].
- **A waterproof layer stops rain but also holds in your own moisture** (general practice). Leave a small gap near your face. Do not seal yourself in.
- **A vent is for breathing.** It is not permission to burn anything [6].
- **Drying wet clothing near a flame in the shelter** is not allowed here. FM 21-76 describes heated shelters and drying before a fire [1]; this entry does not follow that for the sleeping space.

## Heat sources for a cold person

Sources differ. The Red Cross allows heating pads or hot-water bottles filled with warm water, wrapped in thin, dry cloth, for a person who is far from medical care [8]. MedlinePlus says: "Do not use direct heat (such as hot water, a heating pad, or a heat lamp) to warm the person" [11]. This entry takes the safer choice: body heat and dry insulation first, then wrapped heat packs on the chest, armpits and back, never on bare skin, with the skin rechecked every 20 to 30 minutes [9]. It does not teach hot bottles or heating pads, and does not teach any hot bottle as a sleep aid.

## Hypothermia signs and what to do

Adult signs: shivering, exhaustion, confusion, fumbling hands, memory loss, slurred speech, drowsiness [5]. Shivering that stops is a bad sign [8]. NIA adds cold feet and hands, a puffy face, pale skin, and feeling sleepy, angry or confused [7]. Body temperature below 95 F: CDC says get medical attention immediately [5].

- **Any sign of hypothermia, and especially sleepiness or confusion: call 911** if you can reach help (MedlinePlus: call 911 "anytime you suspect someone has hypothermia" [11]; matches survey guide 06).
- Out of the wind and rain. Body heat and dry blankets first. Wrapped heat packs on the chest, armpits and back, never on bare skin; the Wilderness Medical Society says to apply external heat to the armpits, chest and back and recheck the skin every 20 to 30 minutes [9].
- A warm, sweet drink only if the person is alert, shivering and able to swallow (Red Cross: small sips if alert and able to swallow [8]; MedlinePlus: warm, sweetened, nonalcoholic fluids if alert and able to swallow easily [11]). Otherwise nothing by mouth (the Wilderness Medical Society table lists "No food or drink" for a person who has lost alertness [9]). No alcohol [7][8]. Warmed, high-calorie fluid only for an alert person with effective shivering [9].
- Full protocol: [`../../06_first_aid/entries/thermal-injury-hypo-hyperthermia.md`](../../06_first_aid/entries/thermal-injury-hypo-hyperthermia.md). This is general information, not medical advice.

## Failure Modes & Fixes

| What goes wrong | Why | Fix |
|-----------------|-----|-----|
| Cold from below by midnight | Thin or compressed bed; no pad | Add material; roll and re-fluff; waterproof sheet if the ground is wet |
| Bag or clothes damp by morning | Breathing into the bag; sweat; snow melt | Face outside the covers; start dry; brush off snow before entering [1][2] |
| Cold feet | Tight boots or socks; wet socks | Loosen; put on dry socks; dry the wet pair near your body [1] |
| Cold even in a bag | Bag too light; skipped dinner | Add layers and covers; eat something [3][6] |
| Shaking will not stop, or the person stops shivering and gets sleepy or confused | Hypothermia worsening | Treat as an emergency; call 911 if you can [5][8] |
| Temptation to bring in a stove or grill | Cold and desperation | Do not. Layers, bed, food, huddling [6] |
| Alcohol "to warm up" | It increases heat loss [8] | Skip it |
