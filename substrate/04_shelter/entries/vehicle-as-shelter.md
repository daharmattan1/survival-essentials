## Goal & When To Use

Use this when your car is stuck, stalled or stopped by snow, and you are not near shelter you can walk to. A car is a windproof, waterproof room that rescuers can see. Ready.gov: "If trapped in your car, then stay inside" [2]. NWS: "Stay in the vehicle!" [1].

Two things kill people in this situation: cold, and carbon monoxide (CO), a gas you cannot see or smell that comes out of the exhaust. This entry is mostly about managing the engine so you get heat without CO.

Related: [survey guide, vehicle as shelter](../../../04_shelter.md#vehicle-as-shelter), [`emergency-shelter-types.md`](emergency-shelter-types.md), [`../../06_first_aid/entries/carbon-monoxide-poisoning.md`](../../06_first_aid/entries/carbon-monoxide-poisoning.md), [`../../06_first_aid/entries/thermal-injury-hypo-hyperthermia.md`](../../06_first_aid/entries/thermal-injury-hypo-hyperthermia.md).

> ⚠ **CARBON MONOXIDE IN A STRANDED VEHICLE: a blocked tailpipe or a sleeping occupant can be fatal, and you may feel nothing first.**
>
> - **Clear the snow from the tailpipe whenever the engine runs.** Check it before every start. A blocked tailpipe lets CO build up quickly inside the vehicle and can poison everyone in it [3].
> - **Never sleep with the engine running** (this entry's rule). People who are asleep can die from CO before they have symptoms [4]. Shut the engine off before anyone dozes.
> - **Burn nothing inside the car.** No stove, lantern, candle, charcoal or heater. Fuel-burning equipment should never be used inside a tent, camper or other enclosed shelter, and opening flaps, doors or windows is insufficient to prevent CO build-up [5].
> - **A cracked window is not a fix.** NWS and NHTSA both advise opening a window slightly during engine runs [1][6]; with the tailpipe cleared first, that lowers the risk but does not remove it (this entry's rule). The clear tailpipe, checked again while snow falls or blows [6], is the CO control.
> - **Headache, dizziness, weakness, upset stomach, vomiting, chest pain or confusion** are CO symptoms [4]. Get everyone out into fresh air first, then call 911 (this entry's rule).

## Steps

### Decide: stay or go

1. **Stay with the vehicle** [1][7]. NWS: if you leave, "you will become disoriented quickly in wind-driven snow and cold" [1]. NHTSA, as quoted by Michigan, allows leaving only when a safe place is in clear sight [6]. In blowing snow that is hard to judge, so this entry says: do not leave.
2. **Do not wear yourself out digging.** You will get wet and then cold [6].
3. **Call 911** (or text 911 if there is no voice service), say you are stranded and ask how long help will take. Turn off phone data and use a power bank if you have one [6].
4. **Seat belt on** while stopped, in case another vehicle hits you [6].

### Clear the tailpipe first

5. **Clear the snow from the exhaust pipe before the engine runs** [1][3]. Do it by hand or with a scoop. Let a hot pipe cool before you touch it (general practice).
6. **Check the pipe again before every start** [3], and every so often while the engine runs, especially when snow is blowing or drifting [6].

### Be seen

7. **Tie a bright cloth, preferably red, to the antenna or door** [1].
8. **At night, turn on the dome light while the engine runs** [1]. Michigan, quoting NHTSA, adds hazard lights, reflective triangles if you have them, and headlights off with the dome light on, which uses less electricity [6].
9. **After the snow stops, raise the hood** to show you need help [1].

### Heat in short runs

10. **What the sources say.** NWS: run the motor "about 10 minutes each hour" and "clear snow from the exhaust pipe to avoid gas poisoning" [1]. NHTSA, as quoted by Michigan: run it "only 5-10 minutes each hour"; running it continuously "could lead to asphyxiation from carbon monoxide poisoning" [6]. Both also say to open a window slightly; that lowers the risk but does not remove it, and the tailpipe must be clear first.
11. **The plan: clear pipe first, then about 10 minutes, then off.** Once the pipe is clear, crack a window slightly while the engine runs [1][6]; that and a clear pipe lower the risk but do not remove it (this entry's rule). Never run it longer than about 10 minutes an hour (NWS's figure [1][7], inside the NHTSA 5-10 minute range [6]). Set a phone alarm for the shut-off time.
12. **Shut the windows tightly when the engine is off** to keep the warmth [6].
13. **Watch fuel.** Each run uses it. Check the gauge each time; if it runs low, shorten the runs (general practice). Ready.gov's advice is to keep a full tank in winter [2].

### Stay warm between runs

14. **Layer up.** Hat and mittens, dry clothes, extra blankets [6]. With no blankets, floor mats, newspapers and seat covers add insulation [6]. Seat cushions and spare clothing between you and the cold also help; the Army manual lists vehicle seat cushions and extra clothing as ground insulation [13].
15. **Share heat.** Passengers can huddle together [6].
16. **Loosen shoelaces** so blood reaches the toes [6].
17. **Move your arms, legs, fingers and toes** now and then [7]. Keep it light enough that you do not sweat; wet clothes pull heat away [6] and sweating soaks your layers (general practice).
18. **Eat and drink.** Cold burns calories (general practice). Stay hydrated [6].
19. **No one sleeps with the engine on.** NHTSA's tip is to try to stay awake and take turns sleeping if others are with you [6]. Combine that with the CO rule: the engine is off before anyone dozes, and the pipe is clear before it starts again.

### Watch for hypothermia

20. **Signs:** shivering, exhaustion, confusion, fumbling hands, memory loss, slurred speech, drowsiness [8]. Below 95 F, CDC says get medical attention immediately [8]. **Any sign of hypothermia, and especially sleepiness or confusion: call 911** (this entry's rule, matching the survey guide 06). Details: [`../../06_first_aid/entries/thermal-injury-hypo-hyperthermia.md`](../../06_first_aid/entries/thermal-injury-hypo-hyperthermia.md).

## Before winter

Keep a full tank, blankets, warm clothes, bottled water, non-perishable snacks and a flashlight in the car [2]. CDC's winter list adds extra hats, coats, mittens and blankets, a bright flag or help sign, and flares [12].

## Other weather in a vehicle

- **Heat.** A parked car is not a heat shelter. Never leave people or pets in a closed car on a warm day [10]. Never leave a child, a disabled person or a pet locked in a car, even in winter [9]. For heat stroke signs and care, see [`../../06_first_aid/entries/thermal-injury-hypo-hyperthermia.md`](../../06_first_aid/entries/thermal-injury-hypo-hyperthermia.md).
- **Lightning.** A metal-topped vehicle with the windows closed counts as a shelter [11]. A convertible or open vehicle is not covered by the source (general practice: do not rely on it).

## Failure Modes & Fixes

| What goes wrong | Why it kills | Fix |
|-----------------|--------------|-----|
| Tailpipe buried by drifting snow | Exhaust backs up into the cabin [3] | Clear it before each start. If drifting keeps burying it and you cannot clear it, leave the engine off (this entry's rule) |
| Engine runs all night | CO builds; fuel runs out | About 10 minutes per hour, alarm set |
| Someone sleeps with the engine on | Sleepers die before symptoms [4] | Shut the engine off before anyone dozes |
| Walking out in a whiteout | Disorientation, exposure [1] | Stay with the car [1][7] |
| A stove, grill or candle "just for a bit" | Fatal CO in a closed space [5] | Nothing burns inside. Use layers, mats, shared body heat |
| Sweating while digging | Wet layers lose warmth [6] | Stop digging; clear only the tailpipe and the door |
| Phone dies | No contact | Data off, power bank, call early [6] |
| Cabin feels warm and you feel sleepy or have a headache | CO can look like fatigue or flu | Open the doors, get everyone out into fresh air and beside the car (not in the exhaust), then call 911 (this entry's rule). Symptoms: [4] |

Source disagreements. Michigan's page, quoting NHTSA, also suggests lighting a candle in a coffee can for warmth [6]. This entry does not teach that: nothing burns inside a vehicle [5]. NHTSA's 5-10 minutes and NWS's "about 10 minutes" overlap; this entry takes 10 as the ceiling.
