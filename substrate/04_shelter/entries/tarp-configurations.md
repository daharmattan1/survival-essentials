## Goal & When To Use

A tarp or poncho is a fast roof: FM 21-76 says a poncho lean-to takes only a short time and minimal equipment [1]. NPS describes a rope and tarp as a rain shelter or wind screen [3]. It keeps rain and wind off you. It does not make heat. You still lose heat to the ground, so the ground layer matters as much as the roof (see below, and [`insulation-and-ground-barrier.md`](insulation-and-ground-barrier.md)).

Use this entry after you have picked a site ([`site-selection-and-hazards.md`](site-selection-and-hazards.md)). The basic poncho lean-to is already in [`emergency-shelter-types.md`](emergency-shelter-types.md) and the survey guide ([`04_shelter.md`](../../../04_shelter.md#tarp-or-poncho-shelter)). This entry covers how to choose a pitch, how to run the ridgeline, and what can go wrong.

> ⚠ **NOTHING BURNS UNDER OR INSIDE A TARP SHELTER. A fire, stove, lantern, candle or heater makes carbon monoxide (CO), a gas you cannot see or smell.** Opening a flap or leaving a side open is not a fix [9]. Plastic and nylon also melt and burn (general practice). Keep any fire at least 15 ft from tents and shelters [10]. Full rule: [`03_fire.md`](../../../03_fire.md#fire-indoors-in-tents-and-in-vehicles-the-carbon-monoxide-rule).

## Choose a pitch

| Pitch | Best for | Cost |
|-------|----------|------|
| Lean-to | Fast set-up, moderate wind, light rain. One side stays open [2] | Open front lets wind and rain in |
| A-frame | Rain, snow or cold. Covers you on both sides and, with an end partly open, still lets you get in and out [2] | Needs a ridgeline and stakes on both sides [2] |
| Poncho tent (FM 21-76) | A low roof, protected on two sides [1] | Less room than a lean-to [1] |

A plow-point pitch (one corner pulled up as a doorway) is not described in any source this entry opened, so it is not taught here.

## Steps

### Before you rig anything

1. **Check the wind.** Pick your anchor trees or poles only after you know which way the wind blows. For a lean-to, the back goes into the wind [1][2].
2. **Look up.** Stay away from dead trees, dead limbs and clusters of dead trees. Limbs and damaged trees can fall at any time. Be extra careful in wind or after a snowstorm, when branches carry snow [6]. Anchor only to live, sound trees with nothing dead overhead.
3. **Look down.** Use the site checks in [`site-selection-and-hazards.md`](site-selection-and-hazards.md): not in a gully, dry wash or on a stream bank [7][8].

### Lean-to

1. Tie a ridgeline between two trees, low and tight (or between trekking poles above the tree line) [2]. FM 21-76 puts the rope about waist high [1].
2. Tie the tarp's back edge to the ridgeline [2].
3. Stake or tie the bottom edge to the ground so the roof slopes [2]. FM 21-76 uses sharpened sticks pushed through the grommets (metal-ringed holes) into the ground [1].
4. Face the back wall into the wind [1][2].
5. Put a pad, packs or a ground sheet under you [2].
6. Block the sides with brush, a pack or gear [1].

### A-frame

1. Run the ridgeline low and tight between two trees or poles [2].
2. Drape the tarp evenly over the line [2].
3. Stake out both sides [2].
4. Leave one end partly open for air and for getting in and out [2]. Which end: this entry's rule is the end away from the wind (general practice). FM 21-76 puts the door of its parachute tepee 90 degrees or more from the wind [1].

### Poncho tent (FM 21-76)

Tie a 1.5 to 2.5 meter rope (about 5 to 8 ft) to the center grommet on each side. Tie the other ends about knee high to two trees 2 to 3 meters (about 7 to 10 ft) apart. Pull the poncho tight and stake each side [1].

### Rope, knots and pitch

- **Knots.** FM 21-76 ties tree lines with a round turn and two half hitches finished as a quick-release knot [1]. CMC suggests a bowline at the tarp and a taut-line hitch at the stake, so you can tighten it [2].
- **No slack.** For more than one night, or if rain is expected, FM 21-76 adds a center support line from the poncho hood to an overhanging branch, "no slack" [1]. Test that branch first: it must be live, sound and not carrying snow.
- **Drip sticks.** Tie a short stick (about 4 in.) to each rope near the tarp so rain does not run down the rope into the shelter [1].
- **Stakes.** The Army manual says to sharpen sticks about 1 in. thick and 12 in. long and drive them into the ground at about a 45-degree angle, tied to the cover with a hitch [12]. On frozen or rocky ground, tie the cover to a large branch or rock laid on the ground near the shelter. In loose sand or soft dirt, tie it to a small piece of wood or a rock and bury that in a hole [12].
- **Taut and steep.** For a shelter of parachute or other porous cloth, the Army recommends a pitch of 40 to 60 degrees, stretched wrinkle-free and tight, and says not to touch the cloth while water runs over it, because that lets water drip in [12]. A tight, sloped waterproof tarp also keeps water from pooling (general practice).

### Ground sheet and bed

1. FM 21-76 says that at rest you can lose as much as 80 percent of your body heat to the ground [1]. Put the bed in first.
2. Under you, best to worst: a sleeping pad, seat cushions or extra clothing, then ferns, grass or boughs. Natural material should be at least 12 in. thick once compressed [12]. See [`insulation-and-ground-barrier.md`](insulation-and-ground-barrier.md).
3. On wet ground, lay a waterproof sheet under the bed. Keep its edges inside the tarp's footprint so roof runoff does not pool on it (general practice).

## Hazards at the tarp

- **Falling trees and limbs.** See step 2 above [6]. If wind picks up in the forest, move to a clearing out of reach of anything that could fall [6].
- **Flash floods.** Distant rain can be channeled into gullies, narrow canyons, ravines and burn scars, and debris flows can bury a camp in minutes. At even a slight rise in water, go to higher ground at once [7]. If you are camping low, get to higher ground immediately [8]. Six inches of moving water can knock you off your feet [8].
- **Lightning.** A tent gives no protection [4]. A tarp is a cloth roof, so this entry treats it the same (this entry's rule). Only a building with plumbing and wiring, or a metal-topped vehicle with the windows closed, counts [5]. Stay off ridges and hilltops, and away from a tall, isolated tree [4][7]. Do not use a tall lone tree on a ridge as your anchor.
- **Wind.** NWS says a tent or camper is not safe in high winds [7]. If the wind rises, lower the tarp or turn it into a low wind screen (general practice).
- **Fire.** FM 21-76 shows a fire reflector wall beside a lean-to and a fire or candle in some shelters [1]. This entry does not follow that; see the warning above.

## Failure Modes & Fixes

| Problem | Cause | Fix |
|---------|-------|-----|
| Water pools and leaks | Slack or wrinkled cloth, shallow angle | Re-tension the lines; steepen the roof (general practice) |
| Rain runs down the rope inside | No drip stick | Add a drip stick to each rope [1] |
| Wind lifts or tears the tarp | Few anchors, flat to the wind | Add stakes and secure every corner; angle the low end to the wind (general practice) |
| Rain blows in the open side | Open front to the weather | Block sides with brush, pack or gear [1]; back wall into the wind [1][2] |
| Damp inside, drips from the cloth | Breath and sweat condense | Leave one end partly open for air [2]; keep a breathing gap and burn nothing |
| Cold from below | Ground layer thin or compressed | Add bed material as it flattens; fluff it between uses [12] |
| Ridgeline anchor fails | Dead or rotten tree or limb | Re-anchor to a live, sound tree [6] |

If a flood, a rockfall, a falling limb or lightning threatens you right now, leave the shelter. A tarp can be re-rigged; you cannot.

To be found, lay the tarp or rocks out in a shape that can be seen from above [11]. If you can choose a color, CMC prefers orange for emergency tarps [2].
