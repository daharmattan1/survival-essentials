## Goal & When To Use

Heat leaves your body by conduction into cold ground when you lie on it. The Army manual says this heat loss "can be somewhat controlled" by putting a layer of insulation between you and the ground (ATP 3-50.21, para 6-45). The Wilderness Medical Society summary calls insulation and a vapor barrier (a waterproof layer) between the person and both the ground and the elements key to all hypothermia treatment (AFP/WMS). This method builds that barrier and traps a layer of dead (unmoving) air.

Even if you have no other shelter materials, **make the ground bed before the roof.** No source here measures how much faster conduction cools you than wind does, so this entry makes no such comparison.

---

## Steps

### 1. Gather Insulation Materials
- **Best to worst (ATP 6-45):** a sleeping pad made for the purpose; vehicle seat cushions, aircraft parts or extra clothing; then natural materials such as ferns and grass. In a coniferous forest, boughs from a tree work.
- **Collect in bulk.** For a natural bed, the Army says make it **a minimum of 12 inches thick** (ATP 6-46). It compresses under your weight, so 12 inches is the starting thickness, not the finished one.
- **Prefer dry material.** Wet insulation insulates poorly because water conducts heat well (general practice); wet clothing conducts heat away from the body up to 25 times faster than dry clothing (ATP 6-7). Once wet, cotton loses about 90 percent of its insulation value (ATP 6-4, stated for cotton socks).
- **A mix of fine (leaves, needles) and coarse (boughs, bark) material** holds air well (general practice).

### 2. Create the Base Layer
- **Clear sharp rocks and twigs** from the sleeping area (ATP 6-11).
- **Optional (general practice):** lay branches or twigs crosswise on the ground so your weight does not press the bed straight into cold soil.

### 3. Build the Insulation Bed
- **Pile material to at least 12 inches** (ATP 6-46).
- **Expect it to flatten.** Fluff it between uses and add material as it compresses (ATP 6-46). The bed is air; flattened material insulates less.
- **Keep it under your torso and hips** where the most weight sits (general practice).

### 4. Add a Waterproof Layer (if available)
- **If you have plastic sheeting, a tarp, a poncho or a trash bag,** put it *under* the bed on wet ground and, if you have a second one, over you (general practice, consistent with the vapor-barrier guidance in AFP/WMS).
- **If you have no waterproof layer,** choose higher, drier ground and add more bed material (general practice).

### 5. Add a Layer on Top (Optional)
- **If time and material permit,** pile dry leaves or boughs over yourself as you lie down (general practice). The top layer will shift; pull material back up as needed.

### 6. Seal Gaps & Edges (and keep a breathing vent)
- **Stuff leaves or gear into gaps** between your body and the shelter walls, especially at the head, neck and feet (general practice).
- **Seal every opening except ventilation holes** (ATP 6-39).
- **CO caveat: keep a breathing vent open and burn nothing inside.** Sealing an entrance tight does not make a fire, stove, candle or heater acceptable. People who are asleep can die from carbon monoxide before they have symptoms, and opening flaps or windows does not prevent the build-up. Bed and clothing are the CDC's alternative to fuel-burning heat (CDC MMWR 1999). The vent is for breathing only.

## Failure Modes & Fixes

### Insulation Compacts Overnight (Loses Warmth)
- **Recognize it.** You are much colder in the early morning although the weather has not changed.
- **Why it happens.** Your body weight flattens the material and the air pockets collapse.
- **Fix (real-time).** Reach out and pull loose material up around your sides and under you. Roll from back to side to redistribute compression (general practice).
- **Fix (design).** Start with at least 12 inches and add more as it flattens (ATP 6-46).

### Moisture Wicks Up into the Bed (from Damp Ground)
- **Recognize it.** Your back feels damp by morning.
- **Fix (immediate).** Put a plastic or other waterproof layer beneath the bed if you have one.
- **Fix (if nothing else).** Move the bed to higher, drier ground (general practice).

### Insulation Wet from Rain or Breathing Moisture
- **Recognize it.** The bed feels heavy and does not insulate; you are cold despite being out of wind and rain.
- **Fix (real-time).** Replace the top layers with dry material if you have it. Dry damp clothing against your body, or in wind and sun (ATP 6-7).
- **Fix (design).** Keep rain off the bed with the roof and a waterproof layer. You may reduce moisture exchange by closing the entrance, but **keep a breathing vent open and burn nothing inside** (see step 6).

### Too Much Insulation Creates Claustrophobia or Stuffy Air
- **Recognize it.** You feel suffocated or panicked when fully buried in material.
- **Why it happens.** A sealed shelter plus thick material can trap exhaled breath and moisture.
- **Fix.** Leave a small breathing opening at your head and keep it clear (general practice, and see the vent rule in step 6). Do not bury your face.

### Branches Break or Shift Under Your Weight
- **Recognize it.** You sink more than expected or feel sharp edges against your back.
- **Fix (real-time).** Lay more branches or thicker pieces underneath.
- **Fix (design).** Test the base by putting weight on it before lying down (general practice).

## Linking to Related Care

If you suspect **hypothermia**, see [`../../06_first_aid/entries/thermal-injury-hypo-hyperthermia.md`](../../06_first_aid/entries/thermal-injury-hypo-hyperthermia.md) (and the survey guide [`../../../06_first_aid.md`](../../../06_first_aid.md)). Signs in adults: shivering, exhaustion, confusion, fumbling hands, memory loss, slurred speech, drowsiness. Body temperature below 95 F is a medical emergency; get medical attention immediately (CDC). A good insulation bed helps prevent it; if it starts, get the person into shelter and insulation, and call 911 if you can. This entry is not medical advice.
