## Goal & When To Use

Before building any shelter, choose ground that won't actively kill you. A good site is:

- **Not in a hazard zone** — not directly downwind of dead trees (widowmakers), not in a flash-flood channel or dry streambed, not at the base of a slope (rockfall, avalanche), not in a cold-air sink.
- **Stable and sloped** — not waterlogged, not on unstable scree or loose soil; gentle slope drains water and cold air.
- **Protected from wind** — not on exposed ridge tops or bare plateaus; wind accelerates heat loss and collapses shelter.
- **Close to water** — proximity to water (but not *in* the water) means drinking and drinking *well* (safe, defensible, hidden from view).

The site is the first shelter problem. A poor site can make a well-built shelter lethal (exposed to wind gusts that collapse it, or slowly filling with water). A good site with a crude shelter beats a perfect shelter on a bad site.

## Steps

### 1. Avoid Flash-Flood Zones
- **Never shelter in a streambed** — even one that looks dry. A rainstorm miles away sends a wall of water through channels unexpectedly.
- **Avoid the base of gullies and ravines** — water concentrates here during rain.
- **Check for debris marks** — look for lines of dead leaves, branches, sand deposited high on tree trunks. These mark the high-water line.
- **Move to higher, drier ground** — at least 10–15 feet above the water mark if the stream is visible; farther if the channel is broad.

### 2. Scout for Widowmakers
- **Look up before settling.** Dead branches and whole dead trees hang in crowns above campsites; wind can drop them on a shelter with little warning.
- **Move away from standing dead trees** (snags). A 60-foot dead tree can fail across your site when you're asleep.
- **Check living trees for dead limbs.** Look for limbs that are gray, missing bark, or hang at odd angles.
- **Choose a site with healthy, well-spaced trees overhead** if you need rain cover from branches. Avoid dense tangles where fallen deadwood accumulates.

### 3. Identify Cold-Air Sinks
- **Avoid the very bottom of valleys** — cold air flows downhill and pools at the lowest point, making that spot 10–20 °F colder than slightly higher ground.
- **Choose ground 20–50 feet up from the valley floor** — this is often "the sweet spot" where you get wind protection from the valley walls but avoid the stagnant cold pocket.
- **Look for air movement.** A slight breeze is a sign you're not in a sink. Perfectly still air on a clear night = cold-air pooling risk.

### 4. Assess Wind Exposure
- **Avoid ridge tops** — wind there can shred a shelter or cause you to build so heavily that the effort exhausts you.
- **Look for natural windbreaks** — dense brush, rock outcrops, dense stands of conifers. Even a 6-foot thicket provides significant wind damping.
- **If you must stay on open ground,** identify the prevailing wind direction (usually visible in tree lean or bent vegetation), and plan to shelter on the lee (downwind) side of a natural feature.
- **Recognize katabatic wind.** On clear nights, cold air slides downslope — even a slight incline will push it away from higher ground. Sleep slightly upslope if possible.

### 5. Proximity to Water
- **Locate water first.** Once you find water, build shelter within 100 feet if possible (easy access, defense position, less work to carry water).
- **But not in the water.** Stay above the high-water mark and never in a marsh, which adds humidity, reduces wind circulation, and can harbor insects and pathogens.
- **Hidden is better.** If shelter is visible from a trail or clearing, choose a site tucked into brush or below a rise. A visible camp is a target.

### 6. Sun Exposure
- **In cold months, favor morning-sun sites.** Shelter on the east or southeast slope so the rising sun warms your shelter entrance and you can start the day with heat gain.
- **In hot months, favor shade.** North-facing sites or deep brush stay cooler throughout the day; the slight morning warmth is less critical than staying out of sun-stroke territory.
- **On equinox days, a compromise:** south-facing sites at moderate elevation tend to be thermally neutral over a full 24-hour cycle.

### 7. Soil & Drainage
- **Feel the ground.** Squeeze soil in your hand. Wet, compacted soil = poor drainage and ground saturation (massive heat loss).
- **Look for sandy or rocky soil** — it drains quickly and doesn't conduct cold as efficiently as saturated clay.
- **Avoid boggy or marshy ground** entirely — the water table is at or above the surface, and your body heat will have nowhere to escape except up through the insulation barrier.

## Failure Modes & Fixes

### Flash Flood During the Night
- **Recognize the signs.** Water table rises, ground becomes soggy, or you wake to rumbling and can't see the creek in the dark.
- **Move immediately.** Grab essentials and move to high ground (at least 30 feet higher). Don't pack completely in a panic; survival > gear.
- **Fix.** When you choose a new site next day, use the debris-line rule obsessively: camp *visibly* above old high-water marks.

### Widowmaker Falls
- **Recognize it.** A loud crack followed by a crash, or you wake under a tree that has a fresh break.
- **Move away from the hazard.** If a large branch falls but misses, the tree is compromised and may drop more. Move to open ground or under a different, healthy tree.
- **Fix.** Never re-shelter under a tree with fresh breaks or a lean suggesting instability.

### Hypothermia from Cold-Air Sink
- **Recognize it.** You're shivering intensely even inside your shelter, ground feels ice-cold, and the shelter feels warmer than the night air. (That's the sign—you're in a sink.)
- **Move at first light.** Climb 30–50 feet higher in elevation. Even a small rise breaks the pool.
- **Fix.** Scout before dark tomorrow; choose a site where you can feel *any* air movement.

### Wind Collapse
- **Recognize it.** Strong gusts; if unattended, the shelter can be shredded by sustained wind over 20 mph.
- **Rebuild or dig in.** Either rebuild in a more sheltered spot (dense brush, lee of rock), or dig your shelter deeper into a bank if possible.
- **Fix.** During site selection, look for natural wind-damping features *before* you build.

### Insect / Pathogen Swarms from Nearby Marsh
- **Recognize it.** Mosquitoes or biting flies increase sharply in evening, or you notice the shelter is very humid.
- **Move away from the water source.** A marsh helps drainage but is a vector for insects. Move back 150+ feet or to higher, drier ground.
- **Fix.** On the next site, factor in wind *away* from marsh land.
