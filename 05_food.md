# Food: Plan the Calories, Keep Food Clean, Never Guess on Wild Plants or Mushrooms

Use this guide when the store is out of reach, the power is off, or you are stuck outdoors and thinking about food. A healthy adult can live several weeks without food [7], so water and shelter come first (see [`02_water.md`](02_water.md) and [`04_shelter.md`](04_shelter.md)).

**This guide never tells you a wild plant, mushroom or animal is edible.** Questions like "can I eat this" or "what can I eat here" go to [`regional/_index.md`](regional/_index.md), then to your region's food hub, for example [`regional/mid-atlantic-appalachian/food_hub.md`](regional/mid-atlantic-appalachian/food_hub.md). Positive ID or do not consume.

This guide is not medical advice. It is commonly taught practice for when help is far away.

## What hurts you first

Not hunger. These:

1. **Poisoning from plants and mushrooms.** Mushroom poisons can take hours or days to show, by which time it can be too late to reverse them [7][20].
2. **Germs and parasites in meat and fish.** Raw or undercooked wild game can carry *Trichinella*, a parasitic worm that lives in muscle [13]. Rabbits can carry tularemia, a bacterial disease [10][11]. Freshwater fish can carry parasites [7].
3. **Food that warmed.** Bacteria multiply fast between 40 F and 140 F [1].
4. **Chronic wasting disease (CWD),** a prion disease (a misfolded-protein disease) of deer, elk and moose. No human case has been reported, but it is not yet known whether people can be infected [8].

---

## Decide in this order

1. **Water and shelter first.** If you are cold, wet or dehydrated, fix that before food [7].
2. **Eat what you already have,** perishables first, then the freezer, then pantry staples [4].
3. **Do not forage.** Wild plants and mushrooms are among the highest-risk food sources (this guide's judgment). See [the rule](#wild-plants-and-mushrooms-the-rule).
4. **If you must get food from the land, start with hook and line,** where the law allows. See [fishing](#fishing).
5. **Trapping is last.** It is regulated almost everywhere, takes skill, and brings handling risks [25][7].
6. **Cook everything wild to the [cook-to table](#what-temperature-to-cook-to).**
7. **If you cannot tell whether food is spoiled, cooked through, or what it is, do not eat it** (general practice).

## Plan your calories

**What to know.** A planning figure for an adult is about 2,000 calories a day. Real need depends on age, sex, size and activity [6]. The Army manual also uses 2,000 as the minimum to function [7]. Cold and heavy work push it higher (general practice).

**Steps**

1. **Count the people,** noting children and anyone pregnant or sick.
2. **Count the calories you have.** On each package, multiply calories per serving by servings per package [6]. Add them up. Leave out anything that needs a freezer or fridge you no longer have.
3. **Divide.** Total calories, divided by the household's daily calories, is days of food. Example (this guide's arithmetic): three people at 2,000 each eat 6,000 a day. A cupboard holding 36,000 calories lasts 6 days at full portions.
4. **Eat in this order:** perishables and fridge food, then freezer food, then pantry staples [4].
5. **Ration only if the count says you must.**
   - Food, unlike water, can be rationed, except for children and pregnant women [4].
   - An archived 2004 FEMA/Red Cross page says healthy people can survive on half their usual food intake for an extended period [4].
   - Never ration water [4].
   - This guide's rule: if you are cold or doing hard work, do not cut portions. If you take medicine tied to meals, such as for diabetes, ask your clinician before cutting food, and do not change doses on your own.
6. **Keep a manual can opener.** Canned food can be eaten cold from the can [3][4].
7. **Do not eat from** swollen, dented or corroded cans [3]; throw them out. Never taste food to test it [2].

**Water counts too.** Plan about 1 gallon per person per day [28]; treat questionable water as in [`02_water.md`](02_water.md).

**What goes wrong:** no can opener; pantry food eaten while fridge food spoils; a child's portion cut.

## Cooking and eating when the power is out

Full fridge and freezer rules: [`09_urban_survival.md`](09_urban_survival.md#food-what-to-keep-and-what-to-throw-out). Short form:

- A closed fridge holds its cold about 4 hours. A full freezer holds about 48 hours, a half-full one about 24 hours [5][2]. After 4 hours without power, throw out meat, fish, eggs, milk, cut fruit and leftovers, unless they stayed at 40 F or below in a cooler with ice [2].
- Perishables left out more than 2 hours, or 1 hour above 90 F, go in the trash [1]. Thawed food that still has ice crystals, or is at 40 F or below, can be cooked or refrozen [2].

**Cooking.** Eat cold food first. Camp stoves and charcoal grills go outdoors, at least 20 ft from windows, doors and garages [5]. Nothing burns inside a tent, vehicle, garage or house. See the carbon monoxide rule in [`03_fire.md`](03_fire.md#fire-indoors-in-tents-and-in-vehicles-the-carbon-monoxide-rule).

**What goes wrong:** a grill moved into the garage "just for the rain."

## What temperature to cook to

A food thermometer is the only reliable test. Color is not [1].

| Food | Cook to |
|---|---|
| Fish | 145 F [1] |
| Ground meat and ground game | 160 F [15][16] |
| Venison, wild or farm-raised, all cuts | 160 F [32] |
| Bear, wild pig and other wild meat-eaters or omnivores | 160 F [14] |
| Rabbit and hare | 165 F [11][32] |
| Poultry and game birds | 165 F [16][17][1] |
| Soups, stews, casseroles, leftovers | 165 F [15] |

- **Sources differ in two places.** Penn State gives 145 F for whole venison cuts [15]; this guide uses 160 F. USDA's chart lists 160 F for rabbit [32] and Missouri sets 165 F [11]; this guide uses 165 F so both are met.
- **No thermometer:** cook until the meat is well done all the way through, then keep cooking. This is general practice and not reliable; a thermometer is the only real test. The same applies to bear and wild pig. Bear meat is very dark, so color cannot guide you [14].

## Wild plants and mushrooms: the rule

**Positive ID or do not consume.** Positive ID means you know, with certainty and from several features at once, exactly what a plant is, and you know its look-alikes. One feature, one photo, a smell, or a guess is not positive ID. If you are not sure, you do not eat it. The Army manual agrees [7].

**Never eat a wild mushroom on your own identification.**

This guide does not list wild foods. Your region's food hub, via [`regional/_index.md`](regional/_index.md), starts with its look-alike tables.

### Why guessing fails

- **Deadly plants look like plants people eat.** Poison hemlock gets confused with wild carrots and parsnips and has killed people who made that mistake [7].
- **Color rules fail.** The idea that red, dark or blue berries are fine is wrong. Red baneberry berries are glossy red; large amounts can cause death [22]. Pokeweed has purple-black berries; the whole plant is poisonous, the berries especially [23]. Birds eat pokeweed berries, so "animals eat it" is not a test either [23].
- **Poisons can be delayed.** Mushroom toxins may not show for 6 to 24 hours, then seem to ease before liver and kidney failure [20]. Symptoms from different mushrooms can start after about 20 minutes or after days [21].

### Mushrooms: the doctrine

**Do not eat wild mushrooms in a survival situation.** The Army manual says there is no room for experimentation, and that the worst nerve-affecting mushrooms may not show symptoms for several days [7]. Toxic mushrooms get confused with look-alikes; two commonly confused pairs are false morel and morel, and jack-o'-lantern and chanterelle [21]. Unless you are a mycologist (a mushroom scientist), telling them apart is difficult. Poison experts say "when in doubt, throw it out" [21].

The working rules, in plain words (this guide's rules):

1. No eating without confirmation by an expert, such as a regional mycological society. A photo alone is never enough.
2. Skip all little brown mushrooms (too many toxic look-alikes) and any white-gilled mushroom with a ring on the stem or a cup at the base (that pattern includes the deadliest mushrooms).
3. Skip decayed or insect-damaged ones. Never mix finds in one bag.
4. Cooking never makes an unidentified mushroom fit to eat.
5. When in doubt, throw it out. A wrong one can destroy your liver.

### The Universal Edibility Test: last resort, with hard limits

The Army manual (FM 21-76) describes a step-by-step test for unknown plants, for someone with no other food and no knowledge of local plants. Each plant part needs more than 24 hours to test [7]. This guide does not walk you through it, for these reasons:

- **It does not work for mushrooms.** The Army manual says to avoid all mushrooms and not to eat them in a survival situation [7].
- **It cannot rule out slow poisons.** The test depends on feeling a reaction within hours. Some mushroom poisons take 6 to 24 hours and then relapse [20]. A test that waits a few hours cannot catch a poison that appears after a day (this guide's judgment).
- **A "pass" is not a guarantee.** Toxin levels differ between plants of one species with growing conditions [7].

The test is a last resort, never for mushrooms, and it cannot catch poisons that act hours later.

### If someone ate something

1. **Call Poison Control, 1-800-222-1222**, at the first suspicion. Do not wait for symptoms [21][30]. Have ready the person's age, weight and condition, and what and how much was eaten [24].
2. **Call 911** if the person collapses, has a seizure, has trouble breathing, or cannot be woken [29][30].
3. **Do not make the person vomit.** Poison Control's guidance is "Do not induce vomiting" [30]. Do it only if Poison Control tells you to.
4. **Save a sample.** Photograph it beside a coin for scale and keep pieces in a paper bag, not plastic [21].
5. **If it may have been a mushroom, get medical care now,** even if the person feels fine [20].

## Fishing

Fish are the most reliable animal food for someone with a hook and line (this guide's judgment), but carry parasite, spoilage and pollution risks.

**Know the law first.** Rules differ by state. Virginia is one example. Not legal advice.

- **License:** all inland fishing in Virginia needs one unless you are exempt [27].
- **Banned methods:** snagging (hooking a fish by the body), grabbing, snaring or gigging (spearing) fish is unlawful unless a section allows it [26].
- **Set lines:** trotlines (one long line with many hooks), juglines (a single hook and line on a float), limblines (a hooked line tied to a tree limb) and set poles (a fixed pole with a hooked line) are lawful only for nongame fish and snapping turtles. Not on trout-stocked waters or within 600 ft of a dam [26].
- **Bait:** no live bait, with one narrow exception for trotlines on the Clinch River in Russell, Scott and Wise Counties [26].
- **Marking and checks:** mark each line with your name, address and phone number, check it at least once a day, and remove it from the water when not in use [26].
- **Juglines:** 20 per angler at most, and always within your sight [26].

**Steps**

1. **Choose the spot.** Fish rest in slack water near rocks, in deep pools, under overhanging brush, and around logs. They feed heavily before a storm and poorly after one when the water is muddy [7].
2. **Rig.** A pin, small nail or wire can make a hook [7]. Use legal bait only.
3. **Fish by hand line, or by set line where the law allows it.** Follow the list above or your state's rules.
4. **Handle with care.** Catfish spines on the dorsal (top) fin and barbels (the whisker-like feelers near the mouth) cause painful wounds that quickly become infected [7]. Wear gloves if you have them [16].
5. **Clean quickly.** Fish spoil fast, especially on a hot day. Remove the gills and large blood vessels near the spine, gut any fish longer than about 10 cm (4 in), and scale or skin it [7]. Cool it to 35 to 40 F if you can [16].
6. **Check for spoilage.** Healthy fish have bright, clear eyes and red gills [16]. Sunken eyes, an odd odor, gray or faded color, flesh that stays dented when pressed, or a slimy body mean spoiled fish. Do not taste it to find out [2]. Cooking does not fix spoiled fish [7].
7. **Cook to 145 F** [1]; see the [cook-to table](#what-temperature-to-cook-to). Cook all freshwater fish to kill parasites [7].

**What goes wrong**

- **Polluted water:** fish from fouled or chemical-smelling water can carry chemicals that cooking does not remove (general practice).
- **Saltwater fish:** some species are poisonous or carry ciguatera, a fish poisoning [7]. Saltwater food questions go to [`regional/_index.md`](regional/_index.md).

## Traps and snares (emergency knowledge only)

**Legal note.** Trapping and snaring are regulated almost everywhere. Virginia, for example, requires a trapping license in most cases, sets seasons, bans deadfall traps, allows rabbit trapping only with box traps in season, limits snare size and height, requires the landowner's written permission for land snares, and requires traps to be checked once a day [25]. For set lines, see the list under [fishing](#fishing). No source found says survival is a legal excuse (this guide's judgment). Not legal advice.

**A simple snare, step by step** [7]

1. Find where animals move. Look for runs and trails, tracks, droppings and chewed vegetation.
2. Make a noose big enough to pass over the animal's head. Use wire if you have it, because cord can loosen and slip off.
3. Place the noose across the trail, with the other end tied to a firmly planted stake. Hold it open with small twigs or grass blades.
4. Set several rather than one.
5. Check at least once a day [25], and remove everything when you leave (general practice).

**What goes wrong**

- **The animal is alive and cornered.** A simple snare usually does not kill it, and cord may loosen. Even a squirrel can inflict a serious wound, and any bite risks infection. Approach with caution [7].

Handle every catch as in [game meat](#game-meat-handling-cooking-temperatures-diseases).

## Game meat: handling, cooking temperatures, diseases

This section covers game taken lawfully, or any wild animal you already have in hand. Do not handle or eat one that looks sick, acts strangely, or was found dead [8].

### Handling, in order

1. **Wear disposable gloves.** If you have none, wash hands and arms thoroughly before and after [16]. Wash with soap and warm water for at least 20 seconds before and after handling raw meat [18].
2. **Field dress as soon as possible** (remove the internal organs in the field) to cool the meat and slow bacteria [16][15]. Do not cut the stomach or intestines; they carry germs [15].
3. **Do not use your kitchen knives** for field dressing [8].
4. **Cool the carcass to 35 to 40 F** [15]. If the air is above 40 F, put bags of ice or snow in the body cavity and tie it shut [16]. Keep it out of the sun. Do not tie a deer on a hood or roof, and do not put a warm deer in a trunk [15].
5. **Do not rinse the carcass in a pond or stream.** If you rinse, dry the cavity thoroughly [16]. For birds, wipe the cavity with a clean cloth or paper towel, not grass or snow [17].
6. **Hold at 40 F or below,** two to three days at most if you process it yourself [15].
7. **Throw out** meat from a carcass whose organs smell bad or show greenish discharge, black blood, or blood clots in the muscle [15].

### Cook it

Cook to the temperatures in the [cook-to table](#what-temperature-to-cook-to), with a thermometer.

The CDC lists bear, wild boar, wildcat, fox, wolf, seal and walrus as meats that can carry *Trichinella* [13]. It does not name raccoon, but this guide's rule is to treat raccoon the same way.

*Trichinella and freezing.* Penn State says three weeks of freezing kills the parasite [16]. The CDC says some wild-game strains survive freezing, and curing, drying and smoking do not reliably kill the worms [12][14]. When sources differ, take the more cautious choice: cook it.

### Diseases to know

**Chronic wasting disease (CWD).**
- Where CWD is present, strongly consider testing the animal before you eat it. If it tests positive, do not eat it [8].
- Avoid handling internal organs, especially the brain and spine. Wear gloves [8].
- Do not eat the brain, spinal cord, eyes, spleen, tonsils or lymph nodes [9][31]. (CDC names only brain and spine; the rest is from Alberta Health and Indiana DNR.)
- **Cooking does not destroy CWD prions** [9]. The CDC pages on CWD do not address cooking, so this comes from Alberta Health.
- Wildlife agencies advise boning out the meat and trimming fat and membranes [31]. That helps you avoid the high-risk tissues but is not a guarantee. Testing is the only way to know (this guide's judgment).

**Tularemia (rabbits, hares, rodents).**
- Wear gloves when handling rabbits and other rodents [10][11].
- Cook rabbit to 165 F (this guide's rule; see the [cook-to table](#what-temperature-to-cook-to)) [11][32].
- Check the liver. If it has many white spots about the size of a pinhead, discard the rabbit [11].
- Flu-like symptoms after handling a rabbit: see a doctor and mention the rabbit [11].

**Trichinellosis.** Caused by *Trichinella* worms from undercooked meat. Early signs include nausea, diarrhea, tiredness, fever and belly pain. Severe cases can be fatal [13]. Homemade jerky and sausage can carry it [13]. Cooking to temperature is the protection [12].

## Keeping food: drying, smoking, salting, canning

Food that sits between 40 F and 140 F spoils fast [1].

**Drying jerky.** Heat meat strips to 160 F first (165 F for poultry and game birds [17][1]), in marinade or in an oven. Then dry at 140 F until a test strip cracks but does not break when bent [18]. Heating first matters because bacteria such as *E. coli* may be present [18]. Jerky keeps one to two months refrigerated below 40 F [15].

**Smoking and salting.** This guide gives no tested home formula for either. Smoke and salt do not reliably kill *Trichinella* [12]. Cold smoking in the 40 to 140 F range for days sits where bacteria multiply [1]. (This guide's judgment: cook fresh meat within two to three days, keep it cold, or dry heated strips.)

**Freezing** holds well-wrapped game for 9 to 12 months, but it is not a way to cook meat [15][7].

**Canning.** Do not improvise. Low-acid foods, which include meat, fish and most vegetables, need a pressure canner, which is the only recommended method for them [19]. If you do not know how a jar was canned, throw it out [19]. Boil home-canned low-acid food for 10 minutes before eating, plus 1 minute for each additional 1,000 ft of elevation [19]. Boiling is not a rescue for a bulging lid, spurting, a bad smell or an unknown jar (this guide's judgment). You cannot see, smell or taste botulism toxin (the toxin of botulism, a food poisoning that attacks the nerves), and a small taste can be deadly [19].

## Worked scenario A: day 5 of a power outage

*Two adults and a child, a stocked pantry, no power since Monday.*

1. **Count.** About 24,000 calories are left. Full portions need 6,000 a day, so that is 4 days.
2. **Decide.** No restocking is expected. The child eats a full portion. The adults, resting indoors, cut about a quarter. The household eats about 5,000 a day, so the food lasts about 5 days (this guide's arithmetic and judgment) [4].
3. **Fridge and freezer.** The fridge is long past 4 hours and the freezer's 48 hours are over [5]. A bag of chicken has thawed, with no ice crystals and no 40 F reading. Throw it out [2].
4. **Jars and cans.** Home-canned green beans of unknown origin go in the trash [19], and so does a bulging can of corn [3].
5. **Cook outside.** The camp stove goes on the porch, 20 ft or more from windows and doors [5].

What would have gone wrong: a taste test of the chicken.

## Worked scenario B: a stranded hiker with a fishing kit

*Day 2. A cold creek, a small kit with hooks and line, no food, a lighter and a pot.*

1. **Water and warmth first.** Treat creek water before drinking (see [`02_water.md`](02_water.md)). Build shelter and a fire ([`04_shelter.md`](04_shelter.md), [`03_fire.md`](03_fire.md)).
2. **Skip foraging.** No wild plants or mushrooms. [The rule](#wild-plants-and-mushrooms-the-rule) applies.
3. **Fish.** Hook and line is the method most likely to be legal (general practice; check the rules where you are) [27]. The hiker picks slack water behind a rock, below an overhanging bush. Fish feed heavily before storms, and clouds are building [7].
4. **Catch and clean.** The hiker removes the gills, guts the fish well away from camp, and keeps it cool in the creek [7][16].
5. **Cook.** The fish goes in the pot or on a stick over the fire. The hiker has no thermometer, so the fish is cooked until it is well done all the way through, then cooked longer (general practice, not reliable). A fish with sunken eyes, a bad smell or a slimy body is thrown out [7].

What would have gone wrong: eating the fish half-raw.

## Worked scenario C: a deer taken legally in season

*A lawfully tagged deer on a 50 F day, 3 miles from the truck, in a county with CWD.*

1. **Gloves on, field-dressing knife only** [8][16]. **Dress it quickly,** without cutting the stomach or intestines [15]. Avoid the brain and spine [8].
2. **Cool it.** The air is above 40 F, so bags of ice or snow go in the cavity [16]. Keep it out of the sun [15].
3. **Test.** Check your state's testing rules, strongly consider testing before eating, and do not eat a positive animal [8].
4. **Haul.** No deer on the hood or roof [15]. At home, hold it at 40 F or below, two to three days at most [15].
5. **Check.** Discard on the warning signs in handling step 7 [15].
6. **Cook and trim.** Ground venison, steaks and roasts to 160 F [32]; stew to 165 F [15]. Keep the brain, spinal cord, eyes, spleen, tonsils and lymph nodes out of the food [9][31].

What would have gone wrong: the deer left on the tailgate in the sun.

## Common mistakes

- Eating a plant or mushroom because it "looks like" something, or because of berry color or what animals eat [22][23]. See [the rule](#wild-plants-and-mushrooms-the-rule).
- Treating the Universal Edibility Test as a green light [20][7].
- Making someone vomit instead of calling Poison Control [30].
- Cooking by color instead of a thermometer [1], or taste-testing for spoilage or botulism [19][2].
- Eating deer brain, spinal cord, eyes, spleen, tonsils or lymph nodes [9][31], or handling rabbits without gloves [10].
- Cooking in a tent, garage or house [5]; see [`03_fire.md`](03_fire.md#fire-indoors-in-tents-and-in-vehicles-the-carbon-monoxide-rule).
- Setting lines or traps without checking the law [25][26].

## Quick-reference card

- **Wild plant:** positive ID or do not consume.
- **Wild mushroom:** never eat one on your own identification. A photo is never enough.
- **This guide never says a wild plant, mushroom or animal is edible.** Go to [`regional/_index.md`](regional/_index.md).
- **Poisoning:** Poison Control 1-800-222-1222. 911 if the person collapses, seizes, has trouble breathing or cannot be woken. Do not induce vomiting [30].
- **Planning figure:** about 2,000 calories per adult per day [6][7].
- **Rationing:** food can be rationed, but not for children or pregnant women. Never ration water [4].
- **Power out:** closed fridge about 4 hours, full freezer about 48, half-full about 24 [5][2].
- **Danger zone:** bacteria multiply fast between 40 F and 140 F. Perishables out more than 2 hours go (1 hour above 90 F) [1].
- **Cooking outdoors:** stoves and grills at least 20 ft from windows, doors and garages [5].
- **Cook to a thermometer reading. Color is not a test [1].**
  - Fish: 145 F [1]
  - Ground meat, ground game, venison (all cuts): 160 F [15][16][32]
  - Bear, wild pig, other wild meat-eaters or omnivores: 160 F [14]
  - Rabbit and hare: 165 F [11][32]
  - Poultry and game birds: 165 F [16][17][1]
  - Soups, stews, casseroles, leftovers: 165 F [15]
- **Deer:** cool to 35 to 40 F. Hold at 40 F or below, two to three days at most [15].
- **CWD:** do not eat brain, spinal cord, eyes, spleen, tonsils or lymph nodes. Cooking does not destroy prions. Test where CWD is present [8][9][31].
- **Rabbits:** gloves; discard if the liver has white pinhead spots [10][11].
- **Jerky:** heat to 160 F first (165 F for poultry and game birds [17][1]), dry at 140 F [18].
- **Home canning:** unknown jar, throw it out [19].
- **Traps, snares, set lines:** regulated almost everywhere. Check the law [25][26].
