# Communication - Call for Help, Get Warnings, Be Found

Use this guide when you need to reach 911, tell your family you are alive, hear a warning before it arrives, or be found by rescuers. Three things hurt first: you cannot call for help or be found, you miss the warning, or you act on a rumor. In a disaster, phone networks jam, so calls fail and texts are delayed [3]. In remote country there may be no coverage at all.

Radio rules are federal law. Rules and fees change; check the Federal Communications Commission (FCC) at fcc.gov before you buy or transmit.

## Order of action

1. **Out of danger first.** Then call 911 by voice if you can [2].
2. **Call will not connect?** Try a text to 911 where your area offers it [2]. Do not walk far hunting for a signal (this guide's rule; see [`01_core_principles.md`](01_core_principles.md#lost-outdoors-stay-put-and-be-found)).
3. **No service and you are in grave and imminent danger?** Activate a personal locator beacon (PLB) or satellite device if you carry one [24]. Example: hurt, cannot walk out, cold, no way to call (see [Scenario B](#worked-scenario-b-injured-hiker-no-cell-signal)).
4. **Signal while you wait.** Whistle: three blasts, rest, three again [28]. Flashlight: SOS, three quick, three slow, three quick [28]. Three fires (night) or three columns of smoke (day) mean distress [34]. On a sunny day, flash a mirror toward aircraft [34]. More in [Signal for rescue](#signal-for-rescue).
5. **After you call 911 or activate a beacon, stay where you told rescuers you are.** Moving makes you harder to find [28]. (Beacon case: this guide's rule.)
6. **When you are safe, text your out-of-state contact** [4].
7. **Keep a warning source on:** phone alerts and a weather radio [5].

## Call 911 well

Make a voice call whenever you can. Voice gives the call center more information than text [2].

1. **Say where you are first.** Give the location of the emergency right away [1]: street address, cross streets, mile marker or trail name. The phone may send a location, but say it anyway [1].
2. **Say what is happening** in a few plain words: fire, crash, not breathing, bleeding, lost.
3. **Give your phone number** in case the call drops [1].
4. **Answer the questions.** Stay on the line until the dispatcher says to hang up (general practice).
5. **Lost or hurt outdoors:** also give GPS coordinates from your phone, the trail or area where you started, your last known location, what you can see, your clothing and gear, and your medical conditions [28]. For an injury, describe it. Then stay put unless something threatens your life [29].

**Text to 911.** Text is the fallback, not the first choice [2]. It works only where the local 911 center has set it up; the FCC publishes a list by area [2]. Where it is not offered, your carrier must send an automatic "bounce-back" telling you to call another way. Read it; do not assume the text arrived [2]. Put your location and the emergency in the first message (general practice).

**Deaf, hard of hearing or speech disabled?** If texting is not available, use a TTY (text telephone) or relay service if possible [2].

**What goes wrong**

- **The call drops.** Call back. A phone with no active service plan can still reach 911, but the dispatcher will not have your number [1].
- **Accidental calls tie up dispatchers.** Lock your keypad, and turn off 911 auto-dial if your phone has it [1].

## Get warnings in

A warning only helps if it reaches you. Use at least two sources (this guide's rule).

**Wireless Emergency Alerts (WEA).** Officials send short, location-targeted alerts to phones in the danger area. They are free, need no sign-up, make a special sound and vibration, and are not text messages [6]. Ready.gov says network congestion does not slow them [5]. Your phone gets WEA only if it supports WEA, is on, is not in airplane mode, and is near a tower of a carrier that takes part. Participation is voluntary [6]. Check your alert settings; some carriers call them "Government Alerts" [5].

**NOAA Weather Radio All Hazards.** NOAA is the National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration. This is a nationwide network of stations broadcasting weather and other hazard information from the nearest National Weather Service (NWS) office [7]. It uses seven frequencies, 162.400, 162.425, 162.450, 162.475, 162.500, 162.525 and 162.550 MHz (megahertz, the unit for radio frequency), and you need a special receiver or scanner [7]. Reliable reception typically reaches about 40 miles from the transmitter on level ground, less in mountains [9].

- Choose a receiver with SAME (Specific Area Message Encoding). It lets you pick your county so it responds only to alerts for your area [8].
- Choose battery backup. Outages come with storms [8].
- NWS sends a 1050 Hz tone before most warnings, which can wake you at night [8].

**Other sources.** Sign up for your community's warning system [10]. Local alerts add to WEA and to Emergency Alert System (EAS) alerts on radio and television [3]. If your town has sirens, learn the tone [10]. A battery or hand-crank radio carries local news in an outage [3].

**What goes wrong:** airplane mode or a switched-off phone blocks WEA [6]; a receiver without SAME sounds for the whole transmitter area [8]; the one radio you own has dead batteries.

## Family communication plan

Do this before anything happens.

1. **Pick one out-of-state contact,** and have every family member contact the same person. It can be easier to reach someone who is out of town [4].
2. **Choose two meeting places:** one in your neighborhood, one in your region [4].
3. **Write it down.** Keep a copy in your purse, car, office and disaster kit. Review it every year [4]. Add numbers for emergency services, utilities, medical providers and insurance [3].
4. **Put an "ICE" (In Case of Emergency) contact in every phone** and tell that contact about your medical issues [3].
5. **Make sure everyone can text,** including your out-of-town contact [3].
6. **Know your home phone type.** Broadband (VoIP) service will not work in a power outage without a battery or backup power. A corded landline may work [3].

**In an emergency**

- **Text family rather than call, unless you are in danger. If you are in danger, call 911 first.** Texts may get through when calls do not, and you avoid tying up lines emergency workers need [4].
- Keep calls short. **Wait 10 seconds before redialing;** rapid redialing adds to congestion [3].
- Send one short message to your out-of-state contact, such as "I'm OK. At the library." (general practice). They tell the others.
- If you evacuate, forward your home number to your cell phone [3].

**What goes wrong:** nobody memorized the number and the phone died (carry the paper card [4]); family members call different relatives and nobody knows who is safe (one contact fixes this [4]).

## On a boat: channel 16 and DSC

A boat in distress calls on marine VHF radio [32].

1. **If your marine radio has Digital Selective Calling (DSC),** send a DSC distress alert, then contact the Coast Guard on channel 16 [32][38]. A DSC alert carries your position only if the radio has GPS [38].
2. **By voice, tune VHF channel 16 first,** unless you know you are out of range of shore and ships [32].
3. **Say:** "MAYDAY, MAYDAY, MAYDAY. THIS IS" your vessel name three times, then your call sign or registration number once. Then "MAYDAY" and the vessel name again [32].
4. **Then give** your position, the problem, the help you need and the number of people aboard [32].

**What goes wrong:** a false distress call, including a MAYDAY radio check, can be prosecuted as a felony [32]; a DSC alert from a radio with no GPS gives no position [38].

## Radios: what each service allows

On land, a radio can reach family, neighbors or a traveler when phones fail. It cannot call 911, and no rule guarantees anyone is listening (this guide's judgment). Boat in distress: [see above](#on-a-boat-channel-16-and-dsc).

W means watts, the measure of transmit power.

**FRS (Family Radio Service)**
- **License:** none [12].
- **Channels and power:** 22 channels. 2 W on 1-7 and 15-22, 0.5 W on 8-14 [12].
- **Range, per the FCC:** under half a mile on 8-14; longer on the others [12].
- **Good for:** short-range check-ins in a group (this guide's judgment).
- **Combo-radio trap:** an older combination FRS/GMRS radio (new ones may not be sold since Sept 30, 2019 [40]) is FRS only if it keeps to FRS channels and power. With extra channels or more power it is a GMRS device and needs a license [12].
- Radios must be FCC-certified, unmodified and not connected to the phone system. FRS and GMRS share channels, so you may hear licensed users [12].

**GMRS (General Mobile Radio Service)**
- **License:** individual FCC license, ten years, age 18 or older [13]. $35 application fee (FCC fee guide effective May 23, 2025) [14]. No test [15].
- **Channels and power:** 30 channels [13]. Up to 50 W for mobile, base and repeater stations on the main channels; interstitial channels are 5 W or 0.5 W [15]. A base station is one at a fixed location, such as a home [15]. A repeater is a hill or tower station that retransmits to extend range. Interstitial channels are the extra ones between the main channels.
- **Range, per the FCC:** one to 25 miles, depending on station class, terrain and repeaters [13].
- **Good for:** a licensed family that wants more range than FRS (this guide's judgment).
- **Who is covered:** the licensee's close family, in-laws included, regardless of age. Cousins and friends are not, except to send an emergency message [13][15]. The FCC GMRS page has the full list [13].
- Give your call sign at the end of transmissions and every 15 minutes during long ones [15].

**MURS (Multi-Use Radio Service)**
- **License:** none [17].
- **Channels and power:** 5 channels, 2 W [17].
- **Range, per the FCC:** under a few miles; an outside antenna can reach ten miles or more [17].
- **Good for:** short-range group use with no license (this guide's judgment).

**CB (Citizens Band)**
- **License:** none; authorized by rule [18].
- **Channels and power:** 40 channels. 4 W AM (amplitude modulation, the standard CB voice mode), 12 W SSB (single sideband) [18].
- **Good for:** channel 9 is for emergency communications or traveler assistance only. Any CB channel may carry an emergency [18].

**Amateur ("ham") radio**
- **License:** a volunteer-examiner exam before you transmit [19]. The entry-level Technician exam has 35 questions. Licenses last 10 years [20].
- **Fee:** $35 application fee [14]; the exam session may charge its own, so ask (general practice).
- **Channels and power:** bands across the spectrum [19].
- **Good for:** long-range and organized emergency communication by licensed operators; repeaters extend range (general practice). Get licensed before you need it.

**Emergency use**

**On land, use 911, FRS or CB.** This guide does not tell an unlicensed person to transmit on ham or GMRS frequencies.

- **FRS, CB:** no license needed, and emergency messages are permitted [16][18].
- **GMRS:** a licensee may let anyone operate the licensee's station to send an emergency message [15]. That is the licensee's permission, not an exemption for unlicensed people.
- **Amateur:** the person controlling the station must be named in a license before transmitting on any amateur frequency [21]. People ask about 47 CFR 97.403, which lets an amateur station use any means at its disposal for essential communication about immediate safety of life or property when normal communication systems are not available [21].

**Imported low-cost handhelds.** Many cheap radios transmit outside the ham bands, so they cannot be sold or used in the U.S. Even a ham-only radio needs an amateur license. Look for an FCC label [22].

**Plain talk on the air.** Each rule is listed here once.

- **GMRS:** coded messages and false or deceptive ones are banned. "10 codes" are allowed. Only a ship, aircraft or other vehicle in immediate danger may say "Mayday" [15].
- **CB:** two-way plain-language voice communications [18].
- **Amateur:** messages encoded to obscure meaning are barred [21].
- **FRS:** plain talk anyway (this guide's rule).
- Radios are not private (general practice). Do not announce what you have or give your address.

**Using a radio**

1. Agree on a channel and check-in time with your group beforehand (general practice).
2. Listen first; the FCC calls this "listen-before-talk" [12].
3. Hold to talk, speak plainly, keep it short: who, where, what you need.

**Mesh radios** relay text between small devices without towers. They need other nodes nearby, and only nodes on the same channel and key can read messages [37]. A mesh does not reach 911 (this guide's judgment).

**What goes wrong:** nobody else has a radio on your channel; batteries die ([power](#keep-phones-and-radios-powered)); an uncertified or modified radio draws a fine and can interfere with public safety radio [22].

## Personal locator beacons and satellite messengers

**PLB.** A personal locator beacon sends a distress signal on 406 MHz to the Cospas-Sarsat satellite system. SARSAT (Search and Rescue Satellite-Aided Tracking) is the U.S. side of that system, run by NOAA with partners the U.S. Air Force, NASA and the U.S. Coast Guard [24]. No FCC license is needed, but the device must be FCC-certified and registered with NOAA [23].

- **Register it** in the NOAA database with your name, contacts and medical details [23]. Registration is required by law, you must update it when information changes, and it lasts two years [26]. Rescuers then know who you are, and most accidental activations of registered beacons are settled with a phone call [25][27].
- **Activate only in grave and imminent danger** [24]. Example: hurt, cannot walk out, cold, no way to call (see [Scenario B](#worked-scenario-b-injured-hiker-no-cell-signal)).
- A PLB works only when you turn it on by hand [24]. GPS models can include your position, accurate to about 100 meters, and transmit at least 24 hours [25].
- A PLB sends an alert and your registered details; it is not a chat device (this guide's judgment).
- **Test only with the self-test feature** [24]. Most false alerts happen during testing and handling [27]. If you set one off by accident, call the U.S. Air Force Rescue Coordination Center at the number on the SARSAT site [27].

**Satellite messengers.** The National Park Service (NPS) lists a 2-way satellite messaging device among gear you may need [31]. A two-way device can describe the problem; a PLB cannot (this guide's judgment). Most need an active paid subscription (general practice). Some newer phones can reach emergency services by satellite; check yours (general practice).

**No device is a rescue guarantee.** NPS says "neither technology nor support from others can take the place of proper planning and preparation" [30].

**What goes wrong:** an unregistered beacon (rescuers do not know who they are looking for [27]); a dead battery or lapsed subscription; moving after you activate (stay put; this guide's rule, matching [Order of action](#order-of-action)).

## Signal for rescue

First signals are in [Order of action](#order-of-action). This section adds detail. Shelter-side signals: [`04_shelter.md`](04_shelter.md#be-found-from-the-shelter).

- **Whistle.** Blow it instead of yelling: three blasts, rest, three again [28].
- **Mirror.** On a sunny day it is the best signaling device [34]. A mirror still works on overcast days [33]. Sweep the flash toward the aircraft or its sound. Do not flash rapidly or hold the beam on a cockpit more than a few seconds [34]. A phone screen is not a mirror (this guide's judgment).
- **Flashlight.** SOS: three quick, three slow, three quick. Use it sparingly [28].
- **Fire and smoke.** Three fires in a triangle, or in a line about 25 meters (about 80 feet) apart, mean distress at night. Three columns of smoke mean distress by day. If alone, keep one [34]. Make smoke that contrasts with the background; green leaves, moss or ferns on a fire make white smoke [33][34]. Rubber and oil make black smoke [34], but do not burn tires or plastic: the fumes are toxic [33] (this guide's rule). No fire when fire risk is high or in wildland fire season [28]. Fire and CO: [`03_fire.md`](03_fire.md).
- **Ground marks.** Large, straight-edged, in the open, against a contrasting background [34]. The FAA suggests a 6 to 1 length-to-width ratio, preferably about 18 feet by 3 feet [33]; the Army says at least 1 meter by 6 meters [34]. Shelter material or rocks laid in a shape visible from the air also work [28].
- **Letter codes.** Sources disagree: the FAA lists "X" as cannot continue [33]; other lists use "X" for medical help [34]. Spell **SOS** or **HELP** instead (this guide's judgment).
- **Bright cloth.** Spread contrasting clothing in the open [34]. Wear bright clothing from the start [31].

**What goes wrong:** rapid mirror flashing can be mistaken for hostile fire [34]; signals too small or the wrong color for the ground [33]; smoke dispersed by wind, rain or snow [34]; leaving your spot after you told 911 where you are [28].

## Keep phones and radios powered

A dead phone ends every other plan.

- **Charge before a storm.** Keep extra batteries, a solar charger or a car charger on hand [3]. Plan for backup power [36]. Pack an extra phone battery on every hike [31].
- **Save battery.** Dim the screen, close apps you do not need, limit calls [3].
- **No signal bars?** The FCC/FEMA tips say consider turning the phone off, since it drains power hunting for a signal [3]. Trade-off (this guide's judgment): while a warning is possible, leave the phone on so WEA can reach you [6].
- **Charging in a car.** Do not go out to the car if it is unsafe [3]. Never run a car in a garage, even with the door open (this guide's rule, matching [`09_urban_survival.md`](09_urban_survival.md)). The CDC says never leave the motor running in a vehicle parked in an enclosed or partially enclosed space, such as a garage [39]. See [`03_fire.md`](03_fire.md).
- **Generators** go outdoors at least 20 feet from windows, doors and attached garages [36]; the CDC adds vents [39]. Never run one in a garage, basement or shed (this guide's rule). See [`09_urban_survival.md`](09_urban_survival.md).
- **Hand-crank or solar radios** can also charge phones; check the manual first [3].

## Rumors and bad information

After a disaster there are many rumors and scams [35].

- Share only from trusted sources, and discourage others from sharing unverified items [35]. For what is happening where you are, trust local officials, WEA alerts and the NWS [5][35].
- **Before you evacuate, move or spend money because of a message,** confirm it with a second official source (this guide's rule).

## Worked scenario A: tornado warning, phone networks jammed

You are home in the evening. A storm is coming. Your phone has alerts on, and you have a weather radio with SAME.

1. **The warning arrives.** The weather radio sounds its tone [8] and the phone gives a WEA alert [6]. You do not wait for a call.
2. **Move now.** Go to the basement, a safe room, or an interior room away from windows [11]. Stay away from windows, doors and outside walls. Cover your head and neck, and put furniture or blankets over you [10]. Take the phone and radio.
3. **Keep listening** to EAS, NOAA Weather Radio or local alerts for updates [10]. Leave the phone on and not in airplane mode [6].
4. **After it passes,** check for injuries and hazards. Call 911 only for a real emergency [3].
5. **Tell your family.** Text your out-of-state contact: "OK. At home." [4]. Do not call; the network is jammed [3]. If the text does not send, wait 10 seconds and try again [3].

## Worked scenario B: injured hiker, no cell signal

You and a partner are four hours up a trail. Your partner falls and breaks an ankle. There is no phone signal. You carry a whistle, a mirror, a flashlight, a registered PLB and extra layers.

1. **Treat the injury and stop heat loss.** See [`06_first_aid.md`](06_first_aid.md) and [`04_shelter.md`](04_shelter.md).
2. **Try the phone.** Check for one bar and try a call or text to 911 [2]. Do not climb a cliff for signal.
3. **Decide about the PLB.** A person who cannot walk out, in cold weather, with no way to call, is a grave and imminent danger [24] (this guide's judgment). Activate it and set the antenna as the manual says; SARSAT calls antenna position "very important" [27]. Most manuals call for the antenna up and a clear view of the sky (general practice). Stay put [29].
4. **Signal.** Three whistle blasts, rest, three again [28]. Keep the mirror ready for aircraft [34].
5. **If other hikers pass,** ask one to go where there is signal and call 911 with your location [29].
6. **Keep your partner warm** off the ground and out of the wind. Leave the beacon on until rescuers arrive, unless the manual says otherwise (general practice).
7. **Do not split up** unless you must (this guide's judgment). NPS says to stay put once you have shared your location [29]. With no beacon, no signal and no one passing, walking out is a hard call. If you go, leave shelter, water, layers and a whistle with the injured person (this guide's judgment). See [`07_navigation.md`](07_navigation.md).

**What went wrong before the trip:** nobody left a trip plan. Tell someone the trailhead, route and return time [28].

## Common mistakes

- Expecting calls to connect in a disaster. Text and wait [3][4].
- Giving the story before the location, or redialing again and again [1][3].
- Airplane mode during a storm warning [6].
- A weather radio with no battery backup [8].
- Using or modifying a radio you are not allowed to [12][22].
- Coded or hidden-meaning talk on the air. Each service's rule is in [Plain talk on the air](#radios-what-each-service-allows) [15][18][21].
- Texting family instead of calling 911 when you are in danger [2][4].
- A false MAYDAY, including a MAYDAY radio check [32].
- An unregistered PLB, or one set off for a non-emergency [24][26].
- Passing on a rumor you cannot confirm [35].

## Quick-reference card (print on one page)

- **911:** voice call if you can. Location first, then what is happening, then your phone number. Stay on the line [1][2]. Text only where offered; otherwise you get a bounce-back, so call [2].
- **Family:** one out-of-state contact, two meeting places, everyone can text, a paper card [3][4].
- **Jammed network:** text rather than call family when networks are busy, unless you are in danger: call 911 first. Keep calls short. Wait 10 seconds to redial [2][3][4].
- **Warnings:** phone alerts on and not in airplane mode, plus a weather radio with SAME and battery backup [6][8].
- **Boat in distress:** VHF channel 16. If your radio has DSC, send a DSC distress alert first. Say MAYDAY three times, your vessel name, position, the problem, the help needed and the number aboard [32][38].
- **FRS:** no license, 22 channels. An older combination FRS/GMRS radio (new ones may not be sold since Sept 30, 2019 [40]) needs a GMRS license if it has extra channels or more power [12].
- **GMRS:** license, $35 application fee, ten years, close family covered [13][14].
- **MURS:** no license, 5 channels, 2 W (watts), short range [17].
- **CB:** no license, channel 9 for emergencies [18]. **Ham:** license and exam [19].
- **Radio talk:** use plain language. The rules differ by service; see Plain talk on the air in the radio section [15][18][21].
- **PLB:** register with NOAA, renew every two years, use only in grave and imminent danger [24][26].
- **Signals:** whistle three blasts, rest, three again. Flashlight SOS: three quick, three slow, three quick. Mirror on sunny days, no rapid flashing. Three fires or three smoke columns. Ground mark in the open, about 18 ft by 3 ft, spelling SOS [28][33][34].
- **Stay put** once you have told 911 where you are or activated a beacon [28][29]. (Beacon case: this guide's rule.)
- **Power:** charge early, dim the screen, carry spares, generators outdoors only [3][36].
- **Rumors:** confirm with a second official source first [35].
